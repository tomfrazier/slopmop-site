When AI-assisted writing is good, and when it's slop

AI help doesn't make writing slop; missing substance does. What useful AI-assisted writing has in common, with examples and a checklist.

6 min read

on this page What makes AI-assisted writing useful When AI-assisted writing turns into slop A checklist for AI-assisted writing before you post Using AI-assisted writing with a clear conscience

Slop Mop folds slop whether a person or an AI wrote it, and it leaves useful posts alone the same way. That position surprises some people. It follows from a simple observation: what makes a post worth reading is what's in it, and an AI tool can help put that in or help leave it out.

What makes AI-assisted writing useful

Useful AI-assisted writing shares the traits of any useful writing:

  • It starts from something the writer knows. A project, a mistake, a number from their own work. The tool helps with structure or wording; the substance comes from the person.
  • It's specific. Names, dates, amounts, places, steps. Details a reader could check or act on.
  • It has been read, cut and corrected. The writer took responsibility for every sentence, including deleting the ones the tool added that weren't true or weren't needed.
  • It admits a cost. Real advice has trade-offs. A post with none is usually a no-catch promise.

Oren Etzioni and Daniel S. Weld proposed a name for the good end of this in their GeekWire glossary of AI-era language: AI cream, the rare AI-assisted writing that's genuinely worth reading.

When AI-assisted writing turns into slop

The failure mode is just as recognisable. The writer starts with a topic instead of something to say, accepts the first draft, and posts it. The result reads smoothly and says nothing.

The glossary has names for the bad version too. Sending AI output you never read is an AI slap; the person who does it is a meat proxy. In the office it's workslop: polished-looking work that pushes the real effort onto whoever receives it, which a Harvard Business Review study found was common enough to measure.

A checklist for AI-assisted writing before you post

  1. Can you say the point in one sentence? If not, the post doesn't have one yet.
  2. Is there one detail only you could have written? A number, a name, what actually happened.
  3. Did you delete what isn't true? Tools invent plausible facts. Check every claim.
  4. Did you cut the stock phrases? Watch for stiff phrasing ("Furthermore", "Ultimately"), hype words and "not X, but Y" slogans.
  5. Would you answer a question about any line in it? If not, that line goes.

Paste a draft into the content value density calculator to see how many concrete details it carries before you post it.

Using AI-assisted writing with a clear conscience

You don't need to hide that you used a tool, and readers who judge the writing won't care. What they'll notice is whether you had something to say. The learn page on AI detection versus writing quality explains why guessing authorship is a poor substitute for reading.
