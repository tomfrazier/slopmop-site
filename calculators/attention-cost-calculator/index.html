Attention cost calculator: what slop costs you in time

Enter how many posts you scroll past and how long each slop post takes to dismiss, and see the hours it adds up to in a year.

Each slop post only takes a few seconds: you read the hook, realise there's nothing behind it and scroll on. The attention cost calculator above adds those seconds up across a day, a week and a year, using your numbers rather than anyone's survey.

How the attention cost calculator works

There are four inputs, and all of them are yours to set:

  1. Posts you scroll past in a day. Count across every feed: LinkedIn, X, Reddit, Facebook, Substack Notes, Medium and anything else you read.
  2. Share of them that are slop. This is your own estimate. The quickest way to get it is to scroll one feed for five minutes and count how many posts you'd call engagement bait, a scroll-stopper hook or filler.
  3. Seconds each one takes to dismiss. Most slop costs a few seconds: long enough to read the opening line and decide. A post you open, read and regret costs far more.
  4. Days a week you scroll.

The example settings (120 posts, 40% slop, 6 seconds each, every day) are an illustration, not a measurement. They come to about 29 hours a year. Change any of them and the result updates.

The attention cost formula

The arithmetic is short enough to check by hand:

StepCalculation
Slop posts a dayposts × share of slop
Minutes a dayslop posts × seconds each ÷ 60
Hours a weekminutes a day × days a week ÷ 60
Hours a yearhours a week × 52
Working days a yearhours a year ÷ 8

Nothing you type is sent anywhere. The calculator runs in this page.

What the number leaves out

The attention cost calculator counts only the seconds spent dismissing posts. It doesn't count:

  • Switching cost. Getting back to what you were doing after a feed break takes longer than the break.
  • Mood. Feeds full of rage bait and humblebrags leave some people worse off than the minutes suggest. The glossary's entries on doomscrolling and brain rot cover that side, and Merriam-Webster's definition of doomscrolling is a good place to start.
  • The posts you missed. Every second spent on slop is a second not spent on the post from a colleague you'd have wanted to see.

So treat the result as a floor. Jakob Nielsen's essay on information pollution made a similar point in 2003: the cost of low-value information is paid in attention, which you can't get back.

Lowering your attention cost

Three things move the number, and you can try each one in the calculator:

  • See fewer posts. Unfollow, mute and use each network's own controls. The guides cover them network by network.
  • Spend fewer seconds per slop post. Learning the nine tells makes the decision faster. You stop reading past the hook.
  • Don't see the slop at all. That's what Slop Mop is for. It reads each post ahead of you on LinkedIn, X, Reddit, Facebook, Substack and Medium, and folds likely slop into a one-line strip, so its cost drops to the time it takes your eye to pass a strip. How it works explains how it scores posts and why it isn't an AI detector.

If you set the share of slop to what Slop Mop folds in your feed, the calculator shows roughly what you'd get back. Add Slop Mop to your browser and it folds those posts for you.
