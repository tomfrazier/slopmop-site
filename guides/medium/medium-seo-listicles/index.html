How to spot SEO-padded listicles on Medium

Formulaic Medium listicles share a shape: a number, a promise and filler items. Here is how to spot them before you click.

6 min read · steps checked 18 october 2026

A padded listicle has a big number in the headline, items that are each a sentence of advice with a paragraph of filler around it, and no evidence the writer tried any of it. The quickest test is to read the item headings alone: if the list still makes complete sense with the paragraphs removed, the paragraphs were padding. Useful lists have items you couldn't guess from the headline.

Why Medium has so many SEO listicles

Lists are easy to write, easy to skim and easy to rank for. A headline like "15 Python libraries every developer should know" matches what people type into a search engine, and Medium stories are often found that way as well as through the feed. That makes the format attractive to anyone writing for volume, and cheap text generation has made volume cheaper still.

Google's March 2024 spam policy update named the target "scaled content abuse": many pages made mainly to rank, with little original value, however they were produced. A padded listicle is the single-page version. None of this means lists are bad. Plenty of the best reference pieces on Medium are lists. The problem is the list that exists for the headline.

Headline tells

Most padded listicles can be spotted from the preview.

  • A big round number with a superlative. "50 best", "the only 10 you need", "every developer must know".
  • A promise with no cost. "Tools that will save you 10 hours a week" with no mention of setup, price or what they replace. That's a no-catch promise.
  • A year stapled on. "in 2026" on a list whose items haven't changed in five years.
  • A second hook in brackets. "(Number 7 will surprise you)" or "(You're probably not using #3)" is a scroll-stopper bolted to a list.

Structure tells, once you're in

Items you could have written yourself

Read the subheadings. "1. Wake up early", "2. Drink water", "3. Set goals" are the advice equivalent of a stock photo. A useful list has at least a few items that surprise someone who knows the subject.

The same paragraph under every item

Padded items follow a template: what it is, why it matters, a sentence of praise, then the next item. If you could swap the paragraphs between items without anyone noticing, nothing specific is being said.

3. WorkspaceApp. WorkspaceApp is a powerful all-in-one workspace that can transform how you work. With its intuitive interface and endless flexibility, it empowers you to stay organised and achieve more.

fine

3. A shared wiki, for client notes only. We tried running tasks in ours and went back to a plain tracker after two weeks, because search got slow once we had a few thousand pages. As a read-mostly wiki it's been fine for a year.

No dates, versions or prices

Tools change. A list of software, libraries or services that never mentions a version, a price or when it was checked is probably copied from older lists. Look for "as of" dates, version numbers and costs.

Links that only go to the writer

Some listicles are a front for a course, an affiliate link or a newsletter sign-up, with every item pointing to the same place. The list itself is the advert.

An ending that asks for something

"Which one is your favourite? Let me know in the comments, and follow for more" ends a lot of padded lists. It's engagement bait, and it often marks a piece written to collect reactions rather than to help.

The heading test, step by step

  1. Open the story and scroll fast, reading only the numbered headings.
  2. Ask whether you learned anything from the headings alone.
  3. Pick the item you know least about and read its paragraph.
  4. If that paragraph adds a number, a limit, a comparison or a first-hand result, the list is probably worth finishing.
  5. If it restates the heading with adjectives, close it.

The whole test takes under a minute and saves the full read time printed under the title.

What a useful list looks like

The good ones tend to share three habits. They say how the list was made (tested, used at work, surveyed readers). They rank or cut, so the writer has taken a position. And each item carries a reason to skip it as well as a reason to try it.

fine

Six things I'd change about our code review process after two years. The first two cost nothing. The last needs a manager to agree, and ours didn't.

That's a list with a method, a cost and an admission. You'd read the next line.

What to do about them

  • Use show less like this on padded lists in your feed, and mute writers who publish them several times a week. The steps are in the recommendations clean-up guide and the muting guide.
  • If a topic you follow is full of them, try a narrower topic.
  • Don't punish the format. A well-made list from a practitioner is often the most useful thing in a topic feed.

Medium already gives extra reach to stories its curators pick under its Boost distribution tier, but that doesn't stop thin lists reaching you through topic feeds and search.
