How to spot a low-quality Medium article in two minutes

A two-minute check for Medium stories: look for specific claims, sources and first-hand detail before you commit to the full read.

6 min read · steps checked 18 october 2026

on this page Why Medium needs a quick check Spot a low-quality Medium article: the two-minute check What to look for, at a glance Things that don't tell you much What to do with a bad one

Skim the opening, one middle section and the last paragraph, and count the specific things: numbers, names, dates, steps, sources. A useful Medium story has several in every section; a low-quality one has a confident tone and almost none. If you can't find three concrete claims in two minutes, leave.

Why Medium needs a quick check

Medium stories are long by design, and the reading time under the headline is a promise of how much of your attention the writer wants. Medium does sort stories. Its distribution rules put every story that follows the rules into Network distribution, send some to readers of related topics through General distribution, and give extra reach to stories human curators pick for Boost. That sorting decides who sees a story. It doesn't tell you whether a particular story deserves the next ten minutes, and plenty of thin stories still reach topic feeds.

The problem is old. In March 2024 Google described a new spam policy against "scaled content abuse": large amounts of unoriginal content made to rank, whether produced by automation, people or both. Open platforms such as Medium attract the same kind of writing, and the check below works whoever or whatever wrote the piece.

Spot a low-quality Medium article: the two-minute check

1. Read the headline against the reading time

A headline that promises a lot in a short read is a warning: "Everything you need to know about Kubernetes" in a four-minute story can't deliver. So is a long reading time for a simple question. Good headlines are specific about scope.

slop

The Secret Habit That Changed My Life Forever (And It Will Change Yours Too)

fine

I tracked my sleep for 90 days with a cheap wrist sensor. Here is what changed when I stopped drinking coffee after 2pm

The first could sit on top of any story at all. The second tells you what you'll get and lets you decide.

2. Read the first two paragraphs

Look for the point of the piece. A strong opening tells you what happened, what the writer did or what the reader will be able to do. A weak one spends its words warming up: a one-line hook, a rhetorical question, a statement that the topic "has never been more important". That's the scroll-stopper tell in long form.

3. Jump to the middle and count specifics

Scroll to a section near the middle and read one paragraph properly. Count the concrete items: a number with a unit, a named tool or version, a date, a step you could follow, a quoted source with a link. Two or more per paragraph is a good sign. Zero, paragraph after paragraph, means the article is mostly framing.

slop

Consistency is key. When you show up every day and commit to the process, results will follow. Remember, small steps lead to big outcomes.

fine

I wrote 300 words before work, Monday to Friday, for eight weeks. The first draft of the book came to 41,000 words. About half survived the edit.

4. Check where the facts come from

Statistics without a link, studies that are named but not cited, and "research shows" with nothing after it are all reasons for doubt. A careful writer links to the source or says plainly that a figure is their own measurement. If the claim matters to you, click one link and see whether it says what the article says it does.

5. Look for first-hand detail

The most valuable Medium stories are written by someone who did the thing: ran the migration, sold the house, taught the class. That shows up as detail nobody would invent because it's inconvenient: the part that went wrong, the cost, the thing they'd do differently. Stories made from other stories have smooth edges and no mess. Watch out for the opposite failure too, the too-tidy story, where one anecdote exists only to deliver a moral.

6. Skim for repetition

Low-value articles often say the same thing three times under three subheadings. If each section opens by restating the headline in new words, you've already read the article.

7. Read the last paragraph

A good ending adds something: a caveat, a next step, a limit on what worked. A weak ending summarises what you just read, tells you the topic "matters more than ever", or asks you to clap and follow. Generic wrap-ups full of stiff phrasing such as "In conclusion" and "Ultimately" usually follow a body that was generic too.

What to look for, at a glance

SignWorth readingProbably skip
HeadlineSpecific scope and subjectVague promise or secret
OpeningStates the point or the resultHook, question or throat-clearing
MiddleNumbers, names, steps, linksAdvice anyone could give
SourcesLinked or clearly the writer's own"Studies show" with no study
ExperienceCosts, mistakes, trade-offsSmooth and universal
EndingCaveats or next stepsSummary or a request to follow

One weak sign isn't a verdict. A fine engineering post can have a clumsy headline, and an opinion essay can be worth reading without a single statistic. You're looking for several signs pointing the same way.

Things that don't tell you much

  • Claps and responses. Popular stories can be thin, and new stories haven't had time to collect either.
  • The publication's name. Big publications vary from editor to editor and story to story.
  • Whether AI helped. A story drafted with AI and checked by an expert can pass every test above. A human can write pure empty praise. Judge the writing.
  • Length. Long can mean thorough or padded; the specifics count tells you which.

What to do with a bad one

Close it, then use show less like this from your feed so Medium learns from it. If one writer or publication keeps failing the check, mute them, and follow the writers who pass it. The recommendations clean-up guide has the steps.
