How to judge an X thread before you read it

A two-minute check for X threads: read the opener for a claim, the middle for evidence and the end for an ask, then decide.

6 min read · steps checked 18 october 2026

Most hollow X threads follow the same formula: a huge promise in the first post, a "🧵" or "a thread", ten or so one-sentence posts that each name something without explaining it, and a final post asking you to follow and repost. To judge one quickly, read the first post for a specific claim, skim two posts from the middle for evidence, then read the last post. If the opener is vague, the middle has no numbers or sources and the ending is an ask, skip it.

Why the X thread formula exists

A thread gives a writer several posts' worth of space and several chances to be shared. That's useful when there's a lot to say. It also makes a thread an easy container for very little: each post can be one sentence long, and the length itself suggests effort. The 🧵 emoji became shorthand for "there's more below", which is why it appears on threads with nothing much below.

None of this means long threads are bad. Some of the most useful things on X are threads: live reporting from an event, a step-by-step fix for a software problem, a researcher walking through a new paper figure by figure. The formula is the problem, and it's recognisable.

The two-minute check for an X thread

1. Read the opener for a claim

A good first post tells you what the thread will show. A formula first post tells you how you'll feel about it.

slop

I spent 300 hours studying the world's best founders.

What I found will change how you think about success forever.

A thread 🧵

fine

We moved 40 TB of customer files from S3 to our own servers last month. Total cost so far, what went wrong on day 3, and the one thing I'd do differently. Thread.

The first promises a transformation and tells you nothing. The second tells you the subject, the scale and what you'll get. You can decide whether you care before reading further.

Watch for: round-number effort claims ("300 hours", "1,000 books"), "most people don't know", "will change how you think", and a run of one-line paragraphs before the 🧵. These are the scroll-stopper tell.

2. Skim two posts from the middle

Open the thread and read posts 4 and 7, or whichever two are in the middle. Ask whether each one would survive on its own.

slop

4/ Steve Jobs obsessed over details.

Elon Musk works 100-hour weeks.

Oprah journals every morning.

fine

4/ The migration broke on day 3 because our backup job still pointed at the old bucket. We lost 6 hours of uploads. Fix: we now run the backup against a checksum list, not a path.

The first is a list of names and familiar claims, unsourced, with nothing you could act on. The second has a date, a cause, a cost and a fix. A thread made mostly of the first kind is padding.

Other middle-post signs:

  • Every post is the same length and shape, as if filled into a template.
  • Famous names stand in for evidence, with no link or source.
  • Each post ends on a cliffhanger ("But that's not the crazy part").
  • Hype words where specifics should be: "game-changing", "insane", "unlocks".

3. Read the last post

The ending tells you what the thread was for.

slop

That's a wrap!

If you found this useful:
1. Follow me for more
2. Repost the first post to help others
3. Bookmark this for later

That's engagement bait, and it often arrives after a thread that didn't earn it. A useful thread usually ends with a source list, a link to the full write-up, a caveat or simply its last point.

4. Decide

If all three checks fail, skip the thread. If the opener is vague but the middle is solid, it may be worth a read despite the packaging. If the opener is specific but the middle is a list of names, the writer probably ran out of material.

Whichever way you decide, the check costs about two minutes. A formula thread of 15 posts takes longer than that to read in full, and you'll usually know by the third post you open.

Patterns that reliably mean padding

  • The lesson list. "10 lessons from 10 years in sales", each lesson a proverb. Lessons with no incident behind them are opinions in numbered form.
  • The borrowed story. A famous founder anecdote retold without a source, ending in a moral. That's the too-tidy story tell.
  • The tool roundup. "15 AI tools that will replace your team", each with a one-line description and no sign the writer has used them.
  • The thread of threads. A thread whose posts each link to other threads by the same account.

Signs a long thread is worth it

  • Numbers, dates and places specific enough to check.
  • Links to primary sources: the paper, the filing, the documentation.
  • The writer was there, did the work or has the data.
  • A trade-off or a thing that went wrong.
  • Posts that differ in length because the content does.

If you want to keep the good thread-writers

When you find someone whose threads pass this check, keep them where For You can't bury them. The Following tab shows only accounts you follow (X Help Center), and the tips in how to clean up your For You timeline help with the rest. For the shorter cousins of the formula thread, see engagement farming on X.
