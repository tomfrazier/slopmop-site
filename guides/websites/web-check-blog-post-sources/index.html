How to check a blog post's sources in five minutes

Open the links, trace the statistic, read about the site elsewhere: a five-minute way to test whether a blog post's sources hold up.

6 min read · steps checked 18 october 2026

on this page Why links aren't enough Before you check a blog post's sources 1. Stop and find the claim that matters 2. Open the link and read the right part 3. Read about the site somewhere else 4. Look for better coverage What to look for Watch for tidy stories as evidence If the sources fail

Pick the one claim the post depends on, open its link and check that the source says the same thing, with the same numbers, about the same subject. Then spend a minute reading about the site somewhere else. If the link is missing, broken or says something weaker, treat the claim as unsupported, however confident the post sounds.

Why links aren't enough

A page full of links looks well sourced. That's why padded articles add them. Wikipedia's guide to signs of AI writing lists vague attributions ("experts say", "studies have shown") and an overemphasis on how reliable the citations are among the habits editors watch for. A source only counts if it exists and supports the specific sentence it's attached to.

Researchers have studied how good checkers do this. In Sam Wineburg and Sarah McGrew's study of lateral reading, which compared professional fact checkers with historians and students, the fact checkers judged unfamiliar sites faster and more accurately because they left the page early and read what other sources said about it. Mike Caulfield turned similar habits into four moves he calls SIFT: Stop, Investigate the source, Find better coverage, and Trace claims to the original context. The steps below borrow from both.

Before you check a blog post's sources

Decide how much the post matters. Caulfield's own advice is that most checks should be quick and shallow, saving the effort for things you'll act on. A tip about houseplants needs one minute. Advice you'll spend money on, or repeat to your team, needs five. For health, legal or money decisions, go to a qualified professional or an official body rather than relying on any blog post, checked or not.

1. Stop and find the claim that matters

Most posts rest on one or two load-bearing claims: a statistic, a study, a quote, a "most people do X". Find it. Everything else is usually framing.

slop

Studies show that 80% of people who journal every morning report higher productivity, making it one of the most powerful habits you can build.

fine

In a 2019 survey of 412 software engineers (linked), 61% said a written daily plan helped them finish more tasks. It was self-reported, and the sample was one company.

The first gives you nothing to check: no study, no date, no sample. The second tells you where to look and admits the limits.

2. Open the link and read the right part

Click through and find the passage that supports the claim. Ask four questions:

  1. Does the page exist? Broken links and links to a homepage are common in thin posts.
  2. Is it the original? Often the link goes to another blog that cites a third blog. Keep clicking until you reach the study, the dataset, the official page or the person quoted.
  3. Does it say the same thing? Watch for upgrades: "may be linked to" becoming "causes", a survey of one company becoming "most people", a 2015 figure presented as current.
  4. Is it about the same subject? A study on mice, students or one country gets quoted as if it covered everyone.

That's the Trace move from SIFT. It takes the most time and catches the most problems.

3. Read about the site somewhere else

Open a new tab and search for the site's name, plus words like "review" or "who owns". You're looking for a sense of what it is: a known publication, a company selling the product it praises, a one-person blog, or a site you can find nothing about.

Then look at the site itself, briefly. Google's guidance for publishers asks whether it's "self-evident" who wrote a page, whether there's a byline and whether bylines lead to more about the author. Those are reasonable questions for a reader too. A named author with a page of related work is a better sign than "Admin" or "Staff writer".

4. Look for better coverage

If the claim is important and you still can't verify it, stop working on this post. Search for the claim itself and see who else reports it. If a reputable source covers the same topic properly, read that instead. This is SIFT's "Find better coverage" move, and it's often faster than rescuing a weak post.

Watch for tidy stories as evidence

Some posts replace sources with an anecdote: a client, a mentor or a stranger on a train who proves the point. Stories can be true and still not be evidence. When one carries the whole argument with no other support, it's the too-tidy story tell. The same applies to testimonial-style praise with no detail behind it, the empty praise tell.

slop

One of my clients was sceptical too. Three weeks later, she called me in tears to say it had changed her business forever.

fine

Of the 14 clients who tried the new invoice template last quarter, 9 were paid faster. Five saw no difference.

If the sources fail

  • One weak source, rest solid: use the post, but don't repeat that claim.
  • Main claim unsupported: treat the post as opinion and look for better coverage.
  • Sources misrepresented: stop trusting the site as well as the post. A writer who upgrades "may" to "will" once tends to do it again.

Posts that dress opinion up as fact for a cause are sometimes called slopaganda. The checks are the same whichever side they come from.
