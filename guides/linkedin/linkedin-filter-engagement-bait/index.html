How to filter engagement bait on LinkedIn

Spot comment-for-DM offers, reaction polls and chain posts, then hide, unfollow or report them without blocking the people behind them.

6 min read · steps checked 18 october 2026

on this page What counts as engagement bait on LinkedIn What LinkedIn treats as spam How to filter LinkedIn engagement bait, step by step When it isn't bait If that doesn't work

LinkedIn has no setting that filters engagement bait as a type. You can hide each bait post with Not interested, unfollow people who post it every week, and report the worst kinds, which LinkedIn's own spam policy names. To fold bait automatically before you read it, you need a filter that reads the post itself.

What counts as engagement bait on LinkedIn

Engagement bait is the part of a post whose only job is to collect comments, reactions or reposts so the post travels further. LinkedIn has said it doesn't want it. In May 2022 it announced it would stop promoting posts that "expressly ask or encourage the community to engage with content via likes or reactions" purely to boost reach (Social Media Today). The format survived anyway.

These are the versions you'll see most often:

I spent 200 hours building the ultimate cold email playbook. 📘

Comment "EMAIL" and I'll send it to you for free.

(Must be connected so I can DM you.)

Our cold email template, with the three subject lines we tested and their reply rates, is in a public doc linked in the first comment. The follow-up section is the weakest part; it hasn't beaten a plain one-line nudge yet.

slop

Which matters more for a startup?

👍 for product
❤️ for distribution
😂 for luck

Vote below 👇

fine

We asked 40 founders in our accelerator cohort what they'd fix first with an extra month of runway. Twenty-six said sales hiring, nine said product, five said fundraising prep.

The keyword giveaway is the most common. The "must be connected" line is the tell inside the tell: the post is also collecting connection requests. For the full list of forms, and when a request for replies is fair, see the engagement bait pattern.

What LinkedIn treats as spam

Most bait is just annoying. Some of it breaks LinkedIn's rules, and those posts are worth reporting. LinkedIn's spam page gives these examples of content it may remove or limit (LinkedIn Help):

  • emoji or reaction polls that artificially boost engagement
  • posts that misrepresent how LinkedIn works (its example is a fake "double-tap" feature) to get more engagement
  • chain-letter posts that request likes, reactions and shares
  • excessive, irrelevant or repetitive comments

LinkedIn's Professional Community Policies also say not to "agree with others ahead of time to like or re-share each other's content" (LinkedIn). That describes engagement pods: groups that agree to comment on each other's posts within minutes of publishing. You can't prove a pod from outside. A run of near-identical comments ("Great insights!", "So true, thanks for sharing") arriving within the first hour from the same faces week after week is a reasonable hint.

A comment-for-a-PDF post usually isn't against the rules. Hide it rather than report it.

How to filter LinkedIn engagement bait, step by step

1. Don't comment the keyword

LinkedIn says your feed includes posts, comments and reactions from your network (LinkedIn Help). Typing "EMAIL" under a bait post can put it in front of your own connections and tells the feed you like the format. If you want the thing on offer, check the author's website or newsletter; it's usually there.

2. Hide the post with Not interested

  1. Click the More icon (three dots) at the top right of the post. In the app, tap the three dots to the right of the post.
  2. Choose Not interested.

This removes the post and asks the feed to show fewer similar ones (LinkedIn Help). The author isn't told. Where LinkedIn asks for a reason, give one, so the feed learns what you disliked about the post.

3. Unfollow or hide the repeat offenders

Some people post a keyword giveaway every Tuesday. For them, use Unfollow [name] in the same menu, or Hide posts by [name] if that's what you're offered. If you're connected you stay connected, and they aren't notified (LinkedIn Help).

4. Report the rule-breakers

For reaction polls, chain letters and fake-feature posts:

  1. Click the More icon on the post.
  2. Click Report this post.
  3. Pick the reason that fits in the Why are you reporting this? window (LinkedIn Help).

To report a single comment, open the comments, click the More icon on that comment and choose Report. In the iOS and Android apps the steps are the same, with taps.

5. Fold bait automatically

Steps 2 to 4 only work after you've seen the post. Slop Mop, a browser extension for Chrome, Edge, Firefox, Safari and Chromium browsers, reads LinkedIn posts as you scroll and scores them on nine tells, one of which is engagement bait (How it works has all nine). A genuine question at the end of a useful post barely registers; keyword giveaways and repost prompts do.

A single clumsy "follow for more" won't fold a post on its own, because the score is cut when only one tell stands out. Bait usually travels with a dramatic hook and some hype words, and that combination is what gets flagged. When it isn't bait

Filtering too hard costs you the useful asks. Leave these alone:

  • A question the writer needs answered. "Has anyone moved payroll providers mid-year? What broke?" The replies are the point.
  • A call for help. Job searches, fundraisers and urgent requests ask for reposts because spreading the word is the purpose.
  • A pointer. "The report is linked in the comments" tells you where something is without charging a comment for it.

A quick test: if every reply vanished after a minute, would the writer lose anything? If not, the replies were for the feed.

If that doesn't work

If your feed is still full of bait after a fortnight, it may be coming from people you follow rather than recommendations. The clean-up guide covers pruning who you follow and switching the feed to Recent. For the X version of the same habit, see engagement farming on X.
