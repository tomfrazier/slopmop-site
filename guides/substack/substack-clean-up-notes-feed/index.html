How to clean up your Substack Notes feed

Use Substack's Content preferences to hide words, mute or block accounts, and set the topics and formats your Notes feed shows.

Most of the clean-up happens in one place: Settings → Content preferences. There you can hide Notes and posts containing up to 50 words or phrases, review who you've muted or blocked, pick topics you want more or less of, and turn down video, audio, chat and long posts. For individual accounts, use the three-dot menu on any note or profile and choose Mute or Block.

You can't switch Notes off. Substack's help pages say Notes can't be disabled or removed from your account, so the job is to shape what reaches you.

Before you clean up your Substack Notes feed

  • Know where your Notes come from. Your Home feed mixes notes from people you subscribe to or follow, notes from publications recommended by the Substacks you read, and restacks, replies and comments from people you follow. A noisy feed is often one or two prolific accounts, or a recommendation chain you never asked for.
  • Following and For you. Substack describes two Home views: a Following feed made of the people and publications you chose, and a For you feed where those get priority alongside suggestions. If For you is the problem, spending more time in Following may fix most of it before you touch a setting.
  • Changes save straight away. There's no Save button in Content preferences, so you can try a setting and undo it without losing anything.

1. Open Content preferences

  • On the web: click More, then Settings, or go straight to substack.com/settings. Scroll to Content preferences.
  • In the Substack app: tap your profile picture, then the Settings icon, and look for Content preferences. The app's menus may be labelled slightly differently from the web, so if you can't find a control there, try the web version.

2. Hide words and phrases

  1. Select Muted words & phrases.
  2. Type a word or phrase.
  3. Click Add.

Posts and notes containing it are hidden from your feed. You get up to 50 entries of up to 100 characters each. To remove one, click the X beside it under Hidden words and phrases.

Use phrases rather than single words where you can, since a single word catches far more than you meant. Good candidates are the stock lines that show up in bait Notes, such as the exact wording of a recurring "drop your link below" thread, or the name of a topic you've had enough of this month. Substack's page doesn't say whether muted words also apply to restacks of someone else's post, so check a few days later that the thing you muted has actually gone.

3. Mute or block an account

From any note, or from someone's profile, open the three-dot menu and choose Mute [name] or Block [name]. The menu is in the same place on the web and in the app.

Two details from Substack's help pages matter here. When you mute or block an author, Substack says it won't recommend their notes or posts to you, so either one also cleans up suggestions. Blocking hides someone's replies on your notes from your view only; other people can still read them.

Mute is the right tool for someone who's fine but posts too much. Block is for someone you don't want reading or contacting you.

To review or undo either, go back to Content preferences and open Muted accounts (click Unmute) or Blocked accounts (click Unblock).

4. Choose topics

  1. Select Manage interests.
  2. Under Interested, add topics you want more of.
  3. Under Not interested, add topics you want less of.

Grey topics are ones you've already picked; white ones are suggestions. This shapes the For you feed. It doesn't remove anything from the people you follow.

5. Turn down formats

Select Content types and move each slider towards Less or More:

  • Video
  • Audio and podcasts
  • Chat
  • Longer posts

If your feed is full of clips from live videos, the video slider is the quickest fix. If you mainly come to Substack for essays, move Longer posts towards More.

6. Optional: label fully AI-generated content

Content preferences also has an AI Content section. Under 100% AI-Generated Content, turning on Label content puts a badge on other people's posts and notes when Substack's detection partner, Pangram, judges the text to be 100% AI-generated. Only text longer than 100 words is checked, and a separate badge shows when the author has switched detection off. The setting is off by default and only changes your own view.

Treat the badge as one clue. It says nothing about whether a note is useful, and plenty of tired Notes are typed by hand. AI slop is a judgement about the writing, and so is the rest of this page.

7. Optional: fold low-value Notes with Slop Mop

Everything above works on sources, words and topics. None of it looks at whether a particular note says anything. Slop Mop is a browser extension that does that part on the desktop web: in Substack's web app it reads the Notes and posts in your feed and scores each one against nine tells of slop, such as hype words and engagement bait, with credit for posts that are useful or sound like a person. Hide mode folds likely slop into a one-line strip you can reopen; Highlight mode only marks it. It judges the writing, not whether AI wrote it, and it never mutes, blocks or reports anyone.

The limits: it works in Chrome, Edge, Firefox, Safari and other Chromium browsers on a computer, not in the Substack phone app. The free plan covers one feed you pick, with 250 checks a day; Slop Mop XL adds the rest. If it folds a note you wanted, vote on it and your vote wins in your feed.

What to look for once it's tidy

A cleaner feed still needs a reader's eye. The Notes that waste the most time tend to share a few habits:

slop

Most writers will never hit 1,000 subscribers.

The ones who do know this one thing.

Restack if you agree 👇

fine

Moved my send day from Sunday to Tuesday in August. Open rate went from 41% to 47% over 6 issues. Small list (900), so could be noise.

The first is a scroll-stopper with engagement bait tacked on: a promise, no content, and a request. The second gives a change, a number and its own caveat in three lines. If the same account posts the first kind every day, that's what Mute is for.

If that doesn't work

  • The same people keep appearing through restacks. Mute the person doing the restacking, not only the original author. Substack's help pages don't describe a setting that hides restacks as a category.
  • A muted word still gets through. Check spelling and try the phrase as it's actually written in the posts. Hashtag-style or punctuated variants may need their own entries.
  • The app doesn't match the web. Make the change on substack.com/settings; it applies to your account everywhere.
  • You've hit the 50-word limit. Prune the entries that no longer catch anything and mute the accounts behind the rest, or let a filter that reads the writing take over some of that work.

The same clean-up habits apply on LinkedIn and Reddit, where Slop Mop also works. Add Slop Mop to your browser and it folds Notes like the first example for you.
