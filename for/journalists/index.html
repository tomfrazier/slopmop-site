Slop Mop for journalists

Mark the press-release padding and viral hooks on LinkedIn so a reporter can spot the posts with names, dates and facts in them.

What your feed looks like

Reporters follow a lot of people they would never choose to read for pleasure: company spokespeople, executives on your beat, agency staff, analysts and the occasional person who might one day be a source. The feed that results is heavy on announcement posts. A product launch is "thrilled and humbled". A redundancy round becomes "an exciting new chapter". A conference panel turns into three paragraphs about how inspiring everyone was, with no record of anything anyone said.

On top of that sits the viral layer: the first-person story with a twist ending, the "I asked 100 hiring managers" post with no method, the screenshot of a supposed exchange with a rude client. Many read as constructed, and checking them takes time you would rather spend elsewhere.

What you want is the opposite: a post where someone at a company says something specific and on the record, an engineer explains what broke, a former employee describes a policy with a date attached. Those posts tend to be plain and short, and they get fewer reactions than the polished ones.

Examples

slop

Today I'm incredibly proud to share that we're entering an exciting new chapter. This is a truly transformative moment for our people and our customers. More to come! 🚀

fine

From 1 November our Leeds warehouse moves to a four-day week for packing staff. Pay stays the same. We tried it on one shift since June and error rates didn't go up.

slop

A cleaner at our office once asked me why I looked tired.

I told her the truth.

She said something I'll never forget.

That day I learned what leadership really means. ♻️ Repost if this moved you.

fine

Our support team logged 212 complaints about the new billing page in its first week. Most were about one button. We moved it on Thursday and the count dropped to 30 the following week.

The first slop example is announcement padding: empty praise in place of what actually changed. The second is a too-tidy story built around a moral and finished with a repost request. The fine posts give you dates, places and numbers you could phone someone to confirm.

How Slop Mop helps

Slop Mop is a browser extension for Chrome and other Chromium browsers. It reads the posts in your LinkedIn feed before you scroll to them, scores each one against nine tells of slop and three counter-signals, and shows its reasons when you click the mop beside a post.

It judges the writing. It is not an AI detector, and you shouldn't treat a score as evidence that a person or a machine wrote something. A high score means the post has more of the tells and reads as low-value. For a reporter it's a reading aid. It can't check facts, and it says nothing about whether a claim is true.

Today it works on the main feed of desktop LinkedIn only. X, Reddit, Facebook, Substack, Medium, websites and email are coming soon, so it won't yet help with tip-line inboxes or other platforms. It never deletes, reports or blocks a post. The free version covers one feed with 250 checks a day, which resets at midnight UTC.

Setting it up for journalists

Start with Highlight mode at Aggressive sensitivity.

That combination sounds odd, so here is the reasoning. Highlight never hides anything, so a spokesperson's padded post about a restructure stays in view. That post may be the story, and you need to read it whatever its score. Aggressive means more posts get marked, so the yellow and red mops act as a quick colour code. Unmarked posts are the ones most likely to contain something concrete, and you can read those first.

When the panel opens on a red post, the Slopprint shows which tells drove the score. A spike in too-tidy story and engagement bait on a viral anecdote is a useful prompt to be careful before you quote it, though the post could still be true.

Vote when the mark is wrong. A source's terse post that gets flagged can be voted No, and your vote overrides the score in your feed. Votes that disagree with the verdict are reviewed by a person at Artimovo.

If you use LinkedIn mainly for reading and not for monitoring, switch to Hide at Moderate. Folded posts sit in a one-line strip and open again with one click.

If you post too

Reporters promoting their own work on LinkedIn fall into a few habits:

  • "Thrilled to share my latest piece" adds nothing. Lead with the finding itself. See empty praise.
  • The cliffhanger opener ("What we found shocked us") borrows from headline writing and reads as a hook in a feed. See scroll-stopper.
  • "Please share widely" is a mild form of the ask the model looks for. A strong story travels without it. See engagement bait.

The best post about a story is usually two or three sentences with the most surprising fact in the first one, plus the link.
