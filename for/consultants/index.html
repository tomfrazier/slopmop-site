Slop Mop for consultants

Keep client signals visible and the framework filler marked, so a consultant's LinkedIn scroll turns up something billable.

What your feed looks like

A consultant's LinkedIn feed is mostly other consultants. Rivals and solo advisers post for the same reason you do: to stay visible to people who buy advice. That produces a particular kind of slop. There are 2x2 grids with four words in them, "five things every board should ask about AI" with nothing a board couldn't have guessed, and transformation posts that name no client, no sector and no result.

Buried in that are the posts you actually scroll for. A client's operations director mentions they've paused a system migration. A prospect's new CFO describes the first thing they changed. A former colleague shares the real numbers from a pricing project. Those posts are often short and plain, and louder ones built to be shared push them down.

What you want from the feed is signal about specific organisations and specific problems. Generic advice about "change" is the thing you already sell, so reading other people's version of it costs you time and gives you nothing to act on.

Examples

slop

Most transformations fail. Not because of strategy. Because of people.

The firms that win in 2027 will be the ones that put culture at the centre of every decision. Agree? 👇

fine

We spent six weeks mapping how purchase orders move through a 900-person distributor. The approval step everyone blamed took a day. The real delay was a weekly batch job nobody owned, which added up to four days.

slop

Introducing our proprietary RESET Framework™: Reimagine, Engage, Scale, Embed, Transform. A proven methodology to unlock value at every stage, with zero disruption to the business.

fine

The framework we use for supplier consolidation has three steps and the second one usually fails. Suppliers you cut will raise prices on the categories you kept. Budget for that before you announce savings.

The slop piles up tells: a dramatic opener, "not X, but Y" framing, an engagement ask, hype words and a promise with no cost. The fine posts name a size, a number and a downside you could use with a client.

How Slop Mop helps

Slop Mop is a browser extension for Chrome and other Chromium browsers. It reads each post in your LinkedIn feed before you reach it, asks nine questions about the tells of slop and three about whether the post is useful, sounds like a person and has drawn real responses, then gives it a score. Click the mop on any post to see the reasons.

It judges the writing and makes no guess about who wrote it, so it isn't an AI detector. A partner who used AI to tidy up a genuinely useful case write-up will usually pass. A hand-typed post that is all framework and no substance can be flagged.

Today it works on the main feed of desktop LinkedIn. X, Reddit, Facebook, Substack, Medium, websites and email (Gmail and Outlook) are coming soon, so for now it won't help with newsletters or industry sites. The free version covers one feed with 250 checks a day.

Setting it up for consultants

Start with Highlight mode at Moderate sensitivity.

Highlight hides nothing. Possibly slop gets a yellow mop and Likely slop gets a red one. That matters for consultants because a client or prospect might write a post full of tells, and you still want to see it. A sloppy post from the CEO of an account you're bidding for is still useful intelligence about what they care about. Hide mode would fold it into a strip, which you could miss.

With Highlight on, you can scan for unmarked posts first, then go back to the red ones only if the author matters to you. After a week, if the red posts are all from people you don't work with, switch to Hide: folded posts open again with one click, and nothing is deleted, muted or reported.

Use the vote when it gets someone wrong. If a client's dry, hedged update gets marked, vote No. Your vote overrides the score in your own feed. If you know a post is padding that slipped through, vote Probably. When you disagree with the verdict, the post's text goes to a person at Artimovo for review, which is how the tells get tuned.

Aggressive marks more borderline framework posts, but also more of the short, plain posts from clients, which are the ones you most want to see clearly.

If you post too

Consultants post to be found, and the pressure to sound authoritative pushes writing towards the tells. A few habits to watch:

  • Framework names and acronyms read as packaging. Say what the steps are and which one fails most often. See hype words.
  • "It's not about strategy, it's about people" is the most common consulting line on LinkedIn. State the finding directly. See "not X, but Y".
  • Benefits with no cost. Clients know every change has a cost, so a post that names none reads as a pitch. See no-catch promises.
  • Client stories with a neat moral. If you can't name the sector, the size and what went wrong, the story will read as invented. See too-tidy story.

Client confidentiality limits what you can share, but a sector, a headcount band and one honest number usually survive an NDA. Those details are what make a post worth reading.
