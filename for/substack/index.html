Slop Mop for Substack

Coming soon to Substack. Until then: the Notes formulas and padded newsletters to watch for, and how to use Substack's mute and block.

Substack began as a place to send a newsletter and get paid for it. In April 2023 it added Notes, a feed of short posts with restacks in place of retweets (SiliconANGLE). That gave Substack two kinds of slop: the social kind, in Notes, and the long kind, in the newsletters themselves.

Notes is where writers go to find subscribers, so it fills with advice on finding subscribers. Some of it is useful. A lot of it is the same few formulas: support-each-other threads, "how I hit 1,000 subscribers" posts and numbered truths about writing.

The newsletters have a quieter problem. A paid post can feel like it has to be long to be worth the money, and so an idea that needs 400 words arrives in 2,000, with a seasonal preamble and three paragraphs of throat-clearing.

What slop looks like on Substack

Here are the three versions that turn up most, each with a version that respects the reader's time.

The mutual-growth note:

slop

Writers, let's lift each other up! 🤝 Restack this and I'll subscribe to everyone who comments. Small creators deserve to be seen ✨

fine

Moved my newsletter from weekly to fortnightly in August. Open rate went from 41% to 48% over two months. My guess is I stopped sending the filler weeks.

The newsletter that takes three paragraphs to start:

slop

Dear friends, as autumn settles over us and the leaves begin their quiet descent, I find myself reflecting on what it truly means to write, to share, to show up in this space we've built together...

fine

This week: why the council's new parking plan costs residents more than it says, using the three figures buried in appendix C of the budget.

The list of truths:

slop

7 truths about writing nobody tells you:
1. Consistency beats talent.
2. Your voice matters.
3. Done is better than perfect.

fine

The edit that helps my drafts most: I delete the first paragraph. It's almost always me warming up, and the piece starts in the second.

The first is engagement bait for a subscriber count. The second is flowery prose standing in for an opening. The third is the kind of advice that's true and so general it changes nothing. Each fine version could only have been written by someone who did the thing.

What Slop Mop does here

Slop Mop for Substack is coming soon. It doesn't work on Substack yet.

The plan is for it to work the way it does on LinkedIn today. It will read the content ahead of you, ask twelve questions about the writing (nine tells of slop and three counter-signals: sounds like a person, useful to readers, and reader response) and then fold likely slop into a one-line strip you can reopen, or leave it visible and mark it. Every call shows its reasons, and your vote overrides the model.

It judges the writing, and it isn't an AI detector. A newsletter drafted with AI help that's dense with useful detail should pass; a hand-written one that's mostly preamble may not. It won't delete, report, mute or block anything, and it won't touch your subscriptions.

What you can do today

Substack's own controls work on writers rather than posts (Substack Support).

  1. Mute someone in Notes. Open the three-dot menu on one of their notes and choose Mute. Their notes and their replies to your notes are hidden from you.
  2. Block when you need more. Blocking also hides their notes, and stops them seeing your notes and your Substack profile.
  3. Review the list. Everyone you've muted or blocked is listed under Content Preferences in your account settings.
  4. Unsubscribe from padded newsletters. It's the plainest control there is. A newsletter you skim past every week is costing you more attention than its price suggests.

Muting is all or nothing per writer. Plenty of good newsletter writers also post growth formulas in Notes, and muting them loses both.

Who it's for

People who read newsletters for the substance and use Notes to find more of them:

  • Creators and freelancers who write on Substack themselves and want to read peers without absorbing the formulas.
  • Journalists and researchers who follow independent reporting there and would like to reach it faster.
  • Consultants who subscribe to industry newsletters to stay current.

If you write a Substack, the nine tells make a decent pre-send checklist. The stiff phrasing one catches a lot of first paragraphs.
