Slop Mop for investors

For investors and VCs: mark the hype in founder and market posts on LinkedIn, X, Substack and email, keeping every post visible for deal flow.

What your feed looks like

Investors read across a handful of networks as a loose early-warning system. LinkedIn shows which founders are hiring, which portfolio companies are quiet and who just left a company you're watching. X is where operators and other investors argue about a market before it shows up in a report. Substack carries the newsletters (yours or your firm's among them, perhaps) that the rest of the industry reads on Sunday. And the inbox is where deal flow lands: cold pitches, investor updates from portfolio companies, and intro emails forwarded three times.

A lot of what you see on all of them is written to be noticed by investors:

  • Traction posts with large, unexplained numbers: "10x growth", "massive demand", "the fastest-growing company in the category".
  • Market-map and "state of AI" posts and threads that rename a trend every quarter and promise that a category will be worth trillions.
  • Investor content about investing: "what I look for in founders", built on a few contrasts and a borrowed quote.
  • Congratulations replies under funding announcements, each one a variation on "huge news, well deserved".
  • Cold pitch emails that call the company "category-defining" before saying what it sells.

What you want is the opposite of a pitch: an operator's honest numbers, a founder explaining why churn went up, an engineer's account of what a new model actually changed in production, a customer saying why they switched. Specific posts are easier to check, and checking is your job.

Examples

slop

Insane week. 🚀 Our AI-native platform is seeing explosive, unprecedented demand. Pipeline is through the roof. Category-defining moment. More soon. 👀

fine

Monthly revenue reached £140k in September, up from £95k in June. About a third of that is from two customers on three-month pilots, so we're reporting it separately until they convert.

slop

Massive congratulations to this incredible team on this incredible milestone. Truly visionary founders building the future. Couldn't be more excited!

fine

We led this round because their median customer went from one team to four within a year, without a sales hire. The open question is whether that holds outside UK mid-market companies.

The first pair contrasts hype words with a number and its caveat. The second contrasts empty praise with an investment thesis someone could disagree with. In 2025 one investor quoted by Fortune described pilots and one-off deals counted as recurring revenue as vibe revenue. The first fine example shows the plain alternative: report the pilots separately.

How Slop Mop helps investors

Slop Mop is a browser extension for Chrome, Edge, Firefox, Safari and Chromium browsers such as Brave and Arc, on desktop. It reads each post before you reach it and scores the writing against nine tells of slop and three counter-signals. Click the mop on any post and you see the score, the strongest signs and the Slopprint, a chart of all nine tells. For a founder's traction post, that's a quick way to see whether it leans mostly on hype words, on a no-catch promise, or on a scroll-stopping opener. How it works explains the scoring.

On an investor's networks:

  • LinkedIn and X. It reads the posts in your feed and timeline and marks or folds the hype-heavy ones.
  • Substack. It reads Notes and posts, so the market-commentary newsletters get the same treatment as a LinkedIn post.
  • Email. Slop Mop for Gmail and Slop Mop for Outlook check what the sender wrote in a cold pitch or an investor update. Before a message is checked, personal details are replaced with placeholders in your mailbox, and attachments (the deck included) and quoted earlier messages are never sent. Private, legal and transactional mail is never read unless you check one by hand.

Be clear on what it measures. A post's writing quality says nothing reliable about the company behind it. Plenty of strong teams write badly, and Slop Mop isn't an AI detector or a due-diligence tool. It never deletes, reports, mutes or blocks anything.

Setting up Slop Mop for investors

Start with Highlight mode on Aggressive sensitivity.

Highlight hides nothing, which matters when a single post from a portfolio company or a target might be the one you needed to see. Each post's mop turns yellow for Possibly slop or red for Likely slop. Because nothing is folded, an Aggressive setting costs you nothing in coverage. It just marks more posts, which makes the unmarked ones (usually the specific, numbers-first posts) easier to find in a fast scroll.

Ways to adjust:

  • Too much yellow. If most of your feed is marked and the colours stop helping, step down to Moderate.
  • You never open the red posts. Switch to Hide mode. Folded posts become a one-line strip, and one click reopens any of them.
  • A portfolio founder keeps getting flagged. Click the mop to see why. If it's wrong, vote No. Your vote overrides the score in your feed, and when it disagrees with the verdict, a person at Artimovo reviews it. Voting isn't available for email.

The free plan covers the one feed you pick, with 250 checks a day. Choose the network where you find deals: LinkedIn for most, X for investors in markets that argue there. Slop Mop XL, at US$5.99 a month or US$59.99 a year, covers every feed, the sites you read, Gmail and Outlook, with a higher daily limit, on up to five browsers. If your pitches arrive by email, that's where XL earns its fee.

If you post too

Investor posts and newsletters are read closely by founders, so they set the tone for how founders pitch you.

  • Explain the thesis behind a deal in a sentence a founder could argue with. That's more useful to them than adjectives. See empty praise.
  • Skip the market-sizing superlatives and the predictions with no date or number attached. The hype words page lists the usual vocabulary.
  • If you post "what I look for", give a real example from a past decision, with the outcome, including the misses. The too-tidy story page explains why the perfect anecdote reads as made up.

The nine tells

Hype words

Marketing vocabulary such as game-changing, unlock, elevate and seamless, used where a number or a plain verb would say more.

The nine tells

Empty praise

Big verdicts like incredible, powerful and transformative, with no detail to show what the writer actually saw.

Glossary

Vibe revenue

Money or funding an AI company gets from excitement about AI rather than proven success, often presented as durable recurring income.

The nine tells

Scroll-stopper

A dramatic hook, a drumroll of one-line paragraphs and a moral at the end, all wrapped around one ordinary idea.
