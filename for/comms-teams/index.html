What the feed looks like for PR teams

Communications and PR people read the internet differently from everyone else. You're watching for mentions of your organisation, what competitors are announcing, what journalists on your patch are posting, and how your own leaders' posts are landing. That watch covers several places at once: LinkedIn for corporate news and executive posts, X for journalists and breaking complaints, Reddit for the threads where customers and former staff talk plainly, Facebook for local groups and community pages, the news and trade sites you read, and a press inbox full of pitches. You also write a fair share of what appears, under other people's names.

That puts you on both sides of the slop problem. The feeds you monitor are thick with announcement language: "thrilled to announce", "proud to partner with", "an exciting milestone", "humbled". Competitor launches and agency case studies use the same dozen phrases. Employee-advocacy programmes produce waves of near-identical reshares with "So proud to be part of this team!" on top. Meanwhile the posts that matter to you, such as a customer complaint gaining traction on X, a Reddit thread from a former employee describing a policy, or a journalist asking for comment, are often short and blunt, and easy to scroll past.

What you want from the feed is early warning and an honest read of tone. What you want from your own posts is for readers to finish them.

Examples

slop

We're thrilled to announce our strategic partnership with a world-class leader in sustainable innovation. Together, we will unlock new possibilities and deliver unparalleled value to our customers. 🌱

fine

From January, our delivery vans in Bristol and Cardiff will charge at their depot overnight on a tariff we've agreed with the local energy co-op. It covers 140 vans. The rest of the fleet stays diesel until at least 2028.

slop

I'm incredibly proud of our amazing team. This year has been a truly remarkable journey, and I couldn't be more grateful. Here's to an even brighter future together!

fine

Our customer support team answered 41,000 tickets this year with two fewer people than last year. I asked them what to fix first in 2027. The answer was the returns form, so that's the first project.

The slop examples are almost pure announcement language: hype words, empty praise and a closing line that could end any post. The fine versions answer the questions a reader or reporter would ask next: where, how many, when, and what's not changing.

How Slop Mop helps PR teams

Slop Mop is a browser extension for Chrome, Edge, Firefox, Safari and Chromium browsers such as Brave and Vivaldi, on desktop. It reads each post before you reach it on LinkedIn, X, Reddit (posts and comments), Facebook, Substack and Medium, and in the articles you open on news and trade sites. It rates each one on nine tells of slop and three counter-signals, and gives a score and a verdict. Click the mop beside the post to see which tells drove it, shown as a Slopprint chart. How it works covers the scoring in full.

For a comms team that panel is the useful part. It shows you, post by post, which habits make corporate writing read as filler. It judges the writing, so it doesn't matter whether a post was drafted by a person, a team or a model. It isn't an AI detector.

For the press inbox, Slop Mop for Gmail and Slop Mop for Outlook check what the sender of a pitch wrote, with personal details replaced by placeholders first; attachments and quoted earlier messages are never sent. It never deletes, reports, mutes or blocks posts, which matters if you're reading about your own organisation.

Setting up Slop Mop for PR teams

Each person on a team installs their own copy. The free plan covers one feed you pick, with 250 checks a day; a social lead might pick X, a corporate comms lead LinkedIn. Slop Mop XL covers every feed, the sites you read, and Gmail and Outlook, with a higher daily limit, on up to five browsers, for US$5.99 a month or US$59.99 a year. It suits whoever monitors across networks and handles the press inbox.

Start with Highlight mode at Mild sensitivity.

Highlight hides nothing, which is the right default for anyone monitoring. A sloppy post about your company is still a post about your company, and you need to see it. With Mild, only the clearest cases get a yellow or red mop, so the marks stay meaningful and your feed doesn't fill with colour. Highlight also pairs well with LinkedIn's own option to sort your feed by recent posts, so you see mentions in order.

Use the marks as a tone check on announcements in your sector. If a competitor's launch post scores red, open the panel and look at the Slopprint. It's a quick lesson in which phrases to keep out of your own.

Vote when it gets one wrong. A No on a blunt, useful post that was marked overrides the score in your feed. Votes that disagree with the verdict are reviewed by a person at Artimovo. You can't vote on your own posts; the panel shows no vote there, and voting isn't available for email.

Team members who use the feeds mainly for reading can switch to Hide at Moderate for a shorter scroll.

If you post too

Most comms teams post every day, for the company page and for leaders. Readers who use Slop Mop will see those posts scored like anyone else's, on every network. The tells that show up most in corporate writing:

  • "Thrilled", "proud", "humbled" in the first line. Lead with the fact. See empty praise.
  • Partnership and launch vocabulary: "unlock", "unparalleled", "world-class". See hype words.
  • Approved-statement hedging: "may help to support positive outcomes". Say what changes and for whom. See stiff phrasing.
  • Leadership anecdotes with a lesson attached, especially when ghostwritten. See too-tidy story.

When you draft for an executive, ask them for one detail only they would know: a place, a number, a name they'd be happy to share. That detail does more for the post than any amount of polish. Add Slop Mop to your browser and it marks posts like these for you.

Empty praise

Stiff phrasing

Stock transitions and hedges such as furthermore, moreover and may potentially, bolted onto sentences that don't need them.

Too-tidy story

An anecdote with no date, no names and no loose ends, which exists only to deliver the moral in the last line.
