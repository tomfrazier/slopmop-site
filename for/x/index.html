Slop Mop for X

Coming soon to X. Until then: why engagement farming pays, what it looks like, and how to cut it down with X's own muting tools.

On X, slop follows the money. The platform shares revenue with some creators, and in October 2024 it changed how: payouts would depend on engagement with a creator's posts from Premium subscribers, instead of on ads shown in their replies (TechCrunch). TechCrunch noted at the time that the change seemed to reward anything that got replies, for whatever reason.

What followed is familiar to anyone who reads the For You feed. Accounts promise to follow everyone who replies. Threads open with a warning that most people have no idea what's coming. Big posts collect a fringe of replies that say nothing except "this". In July 2026 X's head of product said accounts that repeatedly solicit engagement with lines like "I'll follow everyone who replies" would be removed from the revenue programme, and that nearly 4,000 accounts had been removed in a single day (Legit.ng). Those figures come from X itself.

What slop looks like on X

X slop is short, loud and built to be answered. Three formats do most of the work.

The thread with a drumroll and a numbered promise:

slop

AI just changed everything.

Most people have no idea what's coming.

Here are 11 tools that will make you unstoppable in 2027 🧵👇

fine

We ran three transcription tools over 40 hours of support calls. The open-source one handled accents best. The cheapest mangled our product names in about a third of calls. Numbers per tool below.

The engagement farm, which asks for a reply and offers nothing to reply to:

slop

Drop a 🔥 if you agree.

I'll follow everyone who replies. Let's grow together 🚀

fine

Question for people running small Postgres setups: do you still vacuum by hand, or have you left it to autovacuum? We're seeing table bloat on one busy table and nowhere else.

The reply guy, who turns up under a big account's post:

slop

This. 💯 So underrated. Everyone needs to read this.

fine

The chart starts in 2020, which hides the 2018 dip. Start it at 2015 and the trend is a lot flatter.

The first is a scroll-stopper with hype words attached. The second is pure engagement bait. The third is harder to spot one at a time and obvious in bulk: thirty of them under one post push the useful replies out of sight.

What Slop Mop does here

Slop Mop for X is coming soon. It doesn't work on X yet.

When it arrives, the plan is for it to do what it already does on LinkedIn. It will read posts ahead of you, ask twelve questions about the writing (nine tells of slop and three counter-signals: sounds like a person, useful to readers, and reader response) and either fold likely slop into a one-line strip you can reopen or leave it in place and mark it. Each call comes with its reasons, and your vote overrides the model in your own feed.

It judges the writing, and it isn't an AI detector. A sharp post drafted with AI should stay. A hand-typed "drop a 🔥" probably won't. It won't delete, report, mute or block anything, so it won't touch who you follow.

What you can do today

X's muted words are the closest built-in tool, and they're more flexible than most people realise (X Help Center).

  1. Mute words, phrases, emojis and hashtags. In settings, open the muted words list and add an entry. Phrases count, so "follow everyone who replies" or "drop a" will work, as will an emoji such as 🧵.
  2. Choose where it applies. On the web you can apply a mute to your Home timeline, Notifications or both.
  3. Choose whose posts. Pick "From anyone" or "From people you don't follow". The second is useful for formula threads, which mostly reach you from accounts you never chose.
  4. Choose how long. Mutes last forever by default. You can pick 24 hours, 7 days or 30 days instead.
  5. Review the list now and then. You can see and remove muted words in the same settings page.

Muted words are literal. They catch the exact phrase and miss the next variation, and they'll also hide a friend who uses the phrase sincerely. That's the gap a writing-based judgement is meant to fill.

Who it's for

People who still use X for news, expertise and the occasional good argument:

  • Journalists following breaking stories, where farmed replies bury eyewitnesses and corrections.
  • Developers and researchers who follow specialists and want their posts without the thread-merchant layer.
  • Investors and founders reading industry chatter that's increasingly written to be quoted.
  • Creators who want to see what their peers make, and who'd prefer not to absorb the formulas by osmosis.
