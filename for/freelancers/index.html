What your feed looks like

For a freelance writer, designer, developer, editor or translator, LinkedIn is partly a shop window and partly a lead source. You post your work so past clients remember you, and you scroll looking for the posts that turn into paid work: "Does anyone know a good copywriter for a B2B launch?", an agency asking for freelance capacity, a former client moving to a new company.

Those posts are short and plain, and they sit in a feed that's full of people selling freelancing itself. You know the type: "How I went from $0 to $20k months in 90 days", "Stop trading time for money", "Comment CLIENTS and I'll DM you the cold outreach script that books 10 calls a week". There are endless posts telling you to raise your rates, written by people whose income comes from telling you to raise your rates. Plus the AI tools that will "replace your entire workflow", and the reflective stories about the client from hell, each ending in a lesson about boundaries.

What you want is leads, honest notes from peers about rates and contracts, and news from the companies you'd like to work for.

Examples

slop

90 days ago I had zero clients.

Today I'm fully booked at $250/hour.

The secret? I stopped selling and started serving.

Comment "SCRIPT" and I'll send you my exact outreach template. 👇

fine

Looking for a freelance illustrator for a children's science book, 24 spot illustrations, black and white. Budget is £3,000 to £3,500. Deadline is end of February. Portfolio links in a DM are fine.

slop

A client once refused to pay my invoice. I was devastated. But that moment taught me the most important lesson of my career: know your worth. 💜

fine

I've switched to a 30% deposit on any project over £2,000. I've lost two enquiries since June because of it. I also haven't chased a late invoice in four months.

The slop examples are a scroll-stopper with "not X, but Y" framing and a comment-for-script giveaway, and a too-tidy story whose only job is the moral. The fine ones are a real lead with a budget and a deadline, and a pricing change with its cost stated.

How Slop Mop helps

Slop Mop is a browser extension for Chrome and other Chromium browsers. It reads each post in your LinkedIn feed before you get to it, scores the writing on nine tells of slop and three counter-signals, and either folds likely slop into a one-line strip or marks it, depending on your settings. Click the mop on any post to see the reasons.

It judges what a post says and how. It doesn't guess whether AI wrote it. A hurried lead post from a client is judged on what it says, and a polished income boast written entirely by hand can still be flagged. It never deletes, reports, mutes or blocks anyone, so a client whose post gets folded never knows.

It works on the main feed of desktop LinkedIn today. X, Reddit, Facebook, Substack, Medium, websites and email are coming soon, so job boards, freelance platforms and your inbox aren't covered yet. It's free for one feed with 250 checks a day.

Setting it up for freelancers

Start in Highlight mode at Moderate for your first week, then switch to Hide at Moderate.

A missed lead costs you money, so it's worth seeing how the extension treats the posts you care about before you let it fold anything. In Highlight mode nothing is hidden; posts get a yellow or red mop. Watch what happens to "looking for" posts and to updates from past clients. If they come through unmarked, as you'd expect for plain, specific posts, you can switch to Hide with some confidence.

Hide makes the scroll much quicker. Folded posts sit in a one-line strip, and you can reopen any of them with one click. If you see a client's name on a strip, open it.

If a lead or a client's post gets marked or folded, vote No. Your vote overrides the score in your feed, and because it disagrees with the verdict, a person at Artimovo will review that post's text to see why it missed. Vote Probably on any income boast that gets through.

Aggressive will fold more of the "freelance coach" posts, but it also catches more borderline posts from real people. For a lead source, Moderate is the safer balance.

If you post too

Freelancers post to stay in clients' minds, and the format that seems to perform is the one readers have learned to skip. The tells to keep out:

  • Income and booking claims with no detail behind them. Show the work and say who it was for, if you're allowed. See no-catch promises.
  • "Comment X and I'll DM you" giveaways. If you have a template worth sharing, link it. See engagement bait.
  • Client-from-hell stories with a lesson. Readers who might hire you wonder what you'd say about them. See too-tidy story.

A short post with the project, the brief, one image and what you'd do differently next time does more for a freelancer than a motivational thread, because it shows a prospective client exactly what they'd be buying.
