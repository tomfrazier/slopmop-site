Slop Mop for founders: a feed filter for build-in-public theatre

For startup founders: fold the build-in-public theatre on X, LinkedIn and Substack, and keep operators who share real numbers.

What a founder's feed looks like

Founders tend to follow other founders, and founder writing has its own genre wherever it shows up. On X it's the build-in-public thread and the revenue screenshot. On LinkedIn it's the lessons-learned post and the fundraising announcement. On Substack it's the founder newsletter that turns one good quarter into a philosophy, and Notes that restack the same growth advice. Then there's the inbox: cold pitches from agencies, tool vendors and "growth partners", written by the same templates. Much of it is written to build a personal brand, attract investors or sell a course on building a startup, and it borrows the same shapes over and over.

You'll recognise:

  • The "we went from 0 to £1M ARR in 9 months, here are 11 lessons" thread, where the lessons could apply to any company.
  • Fundraising announcements written as hero stories, with the round size up top and nothing about what the money is for.
  • "Unpopular opinion" posts that turn out to be popular opinions.
  • Tool lists promising that some AI product will replace a team, with no mention of what it gets wrong.
  • Cold emails that praise your "incredible journey" before asking for 15 minutes.

The posts that help you are rarer: an operator explaining exactly how they priced their first enterprise deal, a founder describing why they shut a product line, a post-mortem with the numbers left in. Those posts are often longer and duller to look at, and they lose out in feeds tuned for replies and reactions. X's own help page on the For you timeline describes how it ranks what you see.

Examples

slop

We hit $1M ARR in 9 months. No VC. No team. No marketing budget.

Here's exactly how we did it. 🧵👇

1. Obsess over your customer.
2. Ship fast.
3. Never give up.

fine

Our first enterprise contract was £18k a year. We asked for £30k, they countered at £15k, and we settled at £18k with a two-year term. In hindsight the term mattered more than the price: it got us through the next raise.

slop

AI agents will replace your entire sales team. Zero hiring, zero training, infinite scale. The founders who don't adopt this now will be left behind.

fine

We trialled an AI agent for first-touch sales emails for six weeks. Reply rates were similar to our SDR's, but two prospects complained the emails got their product wrong. We kept it for follow-ups only.

The first slop example is a classic scroll-stopper: a big number, one-line paragraphs, and a promise of a thread. The second is a no-catch promise, with a dose of the pressure described in agent FOMO. The fine versions give a number, a trade-off and a decision.

How Slop Mop helps founders

Slop Mop is a browser extension for Chrome, Edge, Firefox, Safari and Chromium browsers such as Brave and Arc, on desktop. It works where founders read: your X timeline, your LinkedIn feed, Substack Notes and posts, Reddit, Facebook, Medium and the articles you open. In Gmail and Outlook, the Slop Mop add-ons check what the sender wrote, with your personal details replaced by placeholders first; attachments and quoted earlier messages are never sent, and private, legal, health and transactional mail is never read unless you check one by hand.

As you scroll, it scores each post against nine tells of slop and three counter-signals (How it works has the detail). Two counter-signals matter a lot here. "Useful to readers" rewards specific numbers, named examples and steps someone could follow. "Reader response" counts comments and reposts for more than one-tap reactions, and discounts posts with hundreds of reactions and almost no comments, which is what an engagement pod looks like.

It judges the writing, not who wrote it. A founder who drafted a detailed post-mortem with AI help keeps their post. It never deletes, reports, mutes or blocks anything.

Setting up the feed filter for founders

Choose your plan by where you read. The free plan covers one feed you pick, with 250 checks a day: X if that's where your founder reading happens, LinkedIn if it's where your customers and investors are. Slop Mop XL, at US$5.99 a month or US$59.99 a year, covers every feed, the sites you read, and Gmail and Outlook, with a higher daily limit on up to five browsers. For a founder whose day is split between X, LinkedIn, Substack and a crowded inbox, XL is the plan that covers all of it.

Start with Hide mode on Aggressive sensitivity.

That's a stronger setup than most people need, and it suits founders for two reasons. Your time is the scarcest thing you have, and founder content is unusually heavy on hooks, so Moderate can leave a lot of it in place. Aggressive folds more posts into a one-line strip, which you can reopen with one click if the author's name catches your eye.

The trade-off is more false positives. Some genuinely useful founder posts open with a dramatic line because that's what the genre has taught people to do. Handle that two ways:

  • Click the mop on folded posts from people you rate. The panel shows the score and the strongest signs, so you can see whether it was folded for one bad habit or for several.
  • Vote No on the misses. Your vote overrides the model in your feed. When it disagrees with the verdict, a person at Artimovo reviews it. Voting isn't available on email.

If after a week you're reopening more than you're skipping, step down to Moderate.

If you post too

Founders are told to post, and most do. The posts that get read by the people you want (customers, hires, investors) usually look like the fine examples above, whether they go out on X, LinkedIn or a Substack.

  • Keep the numbers and remove the drumroll. "We hit £1M ARR" can be the first sentence of a normal paragraph. See scroll-stopper.
  • Say what it cost. Every growth story has a price: a hire that didn't work, a customer you lost, a year without salary. The no-catch promises page explains why leaving it out reads as a sales pitch.
  • Avoid lessons framed as corrections ("Fundraising isn't about the deck..."). The not X, but Y page covers this.
  • Be careful with revenue claims built on pilots and trials. Counting pilots and one-off deals as recurring revenue has drawn enough attention that one investor quoted by Fortune called it vibe revenue.

Scroll-stopper

No-catch promises

Claims that list every benefit and leave out the price, the effort, the limits and the people it won't work for.
