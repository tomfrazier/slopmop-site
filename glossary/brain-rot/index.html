What brain rot means

Brain rot is slang for the supposed decline of a person's mental or intellectual state from consuming too much trivial, low-effort material, these days mostly short videos and endless feeds. People use it about themselves ("three hours of reels, total brain rot"), about the content ("this is pure brain rot") and, among younger users, for a whole genre of deliberately nonsensical memes.

It's a description of how something feels, and it isn't a medical term. Brain rot is not a diagnosis, and there's no clinical test for it. If you're worried about your concentration, memory or mood, that's worth raising with a doctor or another qualified professional rather than a glossary.

Where brain rot came from

Oxford University Press traces the first recorded use to Henry David Thoreau's Walden, published in 1854. Thoreau used it to criticise a society that preferred simple ideas to complex ones, and asked why England tried to cure potato rot but not "brain-rot".

The modern sense grew on social media in the 2020s. Oxford University Press named it Word of the Year for 2024 after a public vote, and NPR reported that Oxford's tracking showed use of the term up 230% between 2023 and 2024. Oren Etzioni and Daniel S. Weld included it in their October 2026 GeekWire glossary of AI-era language, pairing it with dead internet theory as "the supply side" of the same problem.

Examples

A feed post built to keep you scrolling, and one that gives you something to keep:

POV: you open this app for 5 minutes and it's suddenly 2am 💀 Stop scrolling. Seriously. Part 7 of 12 👇

fine

I tracked my phone use for a week: 19 hours, 11 of them in one app. I moved it off my home screen and it dropped to 6. The other apps barely changed.

How the word tends to turn up in conversation:

example

"I spent the whole train ride watching cooking videos I'll never make. My brain is rotting." Said with a laugh, as a mild complaint about a habit.

The criticism

Some researchers think the term promises more than the evidence shows. In a 2026 paper in Computers in Human Behavior, Christian Montag and colleagues argued that media terms such as "brain rot" and "AI psychosis" lack empirical support, are sensationalist and could feed moral panics about technology. The worry itself is old, too: a Thoreau scholar told NPR that he would have included the "serious" news in newspapers among the things trivialising people's minds.

None of that means heavy scrolling is harmless or that your experience of it is imaginary. It means the word describes a widely shared feeling, and the science on what that use actually does to people is still being argued over.
