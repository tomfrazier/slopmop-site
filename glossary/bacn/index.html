Bacn

Email you asked for and never read.

since 2007 · about human content

on this page What bacn means Where the word bacn came from Examples How Slop Mop sees it

What bacn means

Bacn (pronounced "bacon") is email you asked for but don't have time to read: the airline account update, the shop's sale notice, the daily headlines, the bank's monthly balance, the social network telling you someone new has followed you.

It sits between the two kinds of email everyone already had names for. Spam is unsolicited and you never wanted it. Personal mail is from a person and you do want it. Bacn is what's left over, the stuff you signed up for and still mean to look at, which is why it piles up. One of the people behind the word called it the "middle class of e-mail".

The useful part of the term is that it makes the cost self-inflicted. Nobody forced the newsletter on you, and each one is reasonable on its own. Forty of them a day is a problem you built.

Where the word bacn came from

The word was coined at PodCamp Pittsburgh 2, a conference held in August 2007, as a pun on spam: both are processed pork, and bacon is the better one. NPR reported on it on 29 August 2007, a few weeks later, quoting Canadian blogger Tommy Vallier, who helped invent the term. "It's not spam – you signed up for it, and you actually do want that information," he said. "But yet it still feels like it's wasting your time."

Not everyone was convinced. In the same NPR piece, Swiss blogger Bruno Giussani dismissed it as a few people at a conference naming a trend, and pointed out that email filters already let you route such messages into folders to read later. Vallier himself said the word might already have had its 15 minutes. It has survived as informal internet slang.

Examples

example

A Monday-morning inbox: 3 messages from colleagues, 1 from a friend, and 46 others. A loyalty scheme points balance, two shipping updates for one parcel, a webinar reminder, five newsletters subscribed to in a burst of good intentions, a "you have new connections" digest and a weekly report from a tool nobody on the team still uses.

The three from colleagues are mail. Everything in the 46 that you opted into is bacn. If any of it came from a company you never gave your address to, that part is spam.

A quick sort helps: unsubscribe from anything you haven't opened in a month, route the rest into one folder with a filter, and read that folder once a week.

How Slop Mop sees it

Bacn is a volume problem, and a well-written newsletter can still be bacn if you never have time for it, so the pile itself is outside what Slop Mop judges. Slop Mop for Gmail and Outlook judges the writing in each email and folds the filler. Before a message is checked, personal details are replaced with placeholders in your mailbox, and only what the sender wrote is sent. Transactional mail, such as shipping updates and bank statements, is never read unless you check one by hand. It won't unsubscribe you from anything; Slop Mop vs Unroll.Me covers that side, and Slop Mop for email explains the rest.

Add Slop Mop for Gmail and Outlook, part of Slop Mop XL, and it folds the filler in the bacn you keep.
