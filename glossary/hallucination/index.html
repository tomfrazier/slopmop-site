Hallucination (AI)

Confidently wrong, with citations.

also AI hallucination, confabulation · about ai content

on this page What it means Where it came from Examples The criticism How Slop Mop sees it

What it means

In AI, a hallucination is an answer that contains false or misleading information presented as fact. The classic case is a chatbot that cites a paper, gives its authors, year and journal, and the paper doesn't exist.

What makes hallucinations awkward is their delivery. The false sentence sits among true ones, in the same confident tone, often with the right formatting. Meta's warning when it released its BlenderBot 2 chatbot in July 2021 put it well: "confident statements that are not true".

Large language models produce text by predicting likely next words. A plausible-sounding citation is a likely shape of text, whether or not the source exists, which is part of why these errors happen and why they are hard to remove.

Where it came from

The word borrows loosely from psychology, where a hallucination is perceiving something that isn't there. In computer vision it was used from the 2000s for adding plausible detail to an image, as in "face hallucination", which was treated as a useful feature.

The sense of an error came later. In 2015, Andrej Karpathy used "hallucinated" in a blog post about a language model that produced an incorrect citation link, and Google researchers used it in 2017 for machine translations unrelated to the source text. After ChatGPT's release in November 2022, news outlets took the term up for chatbots' made-up answers, and in 2023 the Cambridge Dictionary added the AI sense to its definition.

Examples

example

Asked for sources on the history of a small coastal railway, a chatbot replies with three references. Two are real books. The third, a 1987 journal article with a plausible title, author and page numbers, cannot be found in any library catalogue or database.

The safe habit is dull: check any name, number, quote or citation an AI gives you before you repeat it. Someone who passes the answer on unchecked is committing AI slap, and the hallucination becomes their error.

The criticism

Some researchers avoid the word. Critics including Usama Fayyad of Northeastern University and the computer scientist Mary Shaw argue it personifies software, as if a model had perceptions that could go wrong, and that it is vague. Alternatives in use include "confabulation", "fabrication", plain "factual error" and "mirage".

A sharper objection comes from Hicks, Humphries and Slater, who argued in Ethics and Information Technology that chatbot output is bullshit in Harry Frankfurt's sense: the models are indifferent to truth, so their true statements are as accidental as their false ones. On that view "hallucination" wrongly suggests a system that is normally trying to be accurate.

Bullshit (Frankfurt)

Harry Frankfurt's term for speech made to persuade or impress with no concern for whether it is true, now often applied to chatbots.

Glossary

Botshit

Made-up or inaccurate chatbot output that a person then uses, uncritically, in real work or communication.

Glossary

AI slap

Posting, sending or submitting AI output without reading it first, so the reader has to do the checking.

Glossary

Stochastic parrot

A description of large language models as systems that stitch together plausible language without understanding what it means.

Glossary

Model collapse

The decline of AI models trained on AI output, which lose the rare and varied parts of real data over successive generations.
