Troll farm

Outrage, staffed in shifts.

also troll factory · since 2010s · about ai and human content

on this page What it means Where it came from Examples The criticism How Slop Mop sees it

What it means

A troll farm is an organisation that pays people to post online in a coordinated way, usually to push a political line, attack critics or make a view look more popular than it is. The workers run many accounts at once: sock puppets posing as ordinary citizens, backed up by bots. To anyone scrolling past, it looks like lots of separate people who happen to agree.

What sets a troll farm apart from a crowd of real supporters is the payment, the coordination and the fake identities. Its output tends to be cheap and repetitive: the same talking points in slightly different words, replies piled on to a journalist's post, outrage timed to a news event. "Troll factory" is another name for the same thing.

Where it came from

The term became widely known in the 2010s, above all through Russia's Internet Research Agency in St Petersburg, which Wikipedia describes as using "troll armies" to spread propaganda and erode trust in American institutions, including during the 2016 US presidential election. Its former office building on Savushkina Street was dubbed the "troll factory".

The practice was never limited to one country. A 2017 Freedom House report, summarised by Wikipedia, found that 30 of the 65 governments it studied paid "keyboard armies" to spread propaganda and attack critics, and that attempts were made to influence elections in 18 of those countries. Platforms have since removed networks of accounts linked to governments and political parties in several countries under rules against "coordinated inauthentic behaviour", Meta's name for the practice.

Examples

example

Minutes after a reporter posts an investigation, the replies fill with accounts created in the past month, most with stock profile photos, making the same three accusations in similar wording.

example

A hashtag trends overnight. Most of the posts come from a few hundred accounts that tweet around the clock in shifts and go quiet on public holidays.

Neither pattern proves a troll farm on its own. Real people pile on, too, and new accounts aren't automatically fake. Researchers and platforms usually rely on evidence beyond the posts: account creation data, shared infrastructure, leaked documents.

The criticism

The term is used loosely, and that's the main complaint about it. Calling an opponent's supporters a "troll farm" is a quick way to dismiss them without engaging, and it can be wrong: a burst of similar posts can come from real people responding to the same call to action. Careful use attaches the label only where there is evidence of payment or central coordination. Without it, "coordinated" or "suspected" is the honest word.

How Slop Mop sees it

Slop Mop judges the writing in each post. It can't see who is paying for it, or that a set of accounts is coordinated. It never reports, mutes or blocks accounts. Troll-farm posts built to provoke can still show tells it scores on LinkedIn, such as engagement bait.
