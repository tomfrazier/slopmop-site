AI addiction

When the chatbot becomes your life.

What AI addiction means

"AI addiction" is an informal way of describing chatbot use that has got out of hand: hours a day with an AI companion, trouble stopping, and other parts of life such as sleep, work or friendships starting to suffer. Researchers also say "problematic AI use", which makes fewer assumptions.

It is not a recognised diagnosis. No diagnostic manual lists it, and there's no agreed test for it. It helps to separate a few things the phrase can blur together:

  • Heavy use. Using a chatbot a lot, for work or for fun.
  • Reliance. Handing it tasks or decisions you used to do yourself.
  • Distress. Feeling anxious or low when you can't use it.
  • Impairment. Use that is damaging your health, job or relationships.

Concern about addiction is usually about the last two, and even then a professional would look at the whole picture.

Where it came from

The phrase follows older debates about "internet addiction" and "social media addiction", and became more common as companion apps and general chatbots became part of daily life. A 2026 scoping review in Frontiers in Psychology looked at 37 studies of problematic AI use and found the field uses inconsistent terms and measures, which makes results hard to compare.

Oren Etzioni and Daniel S. Weld included it in their October 2026 GeekWire glossary of AI-era language, as what happens when a parasocial relationship with a chatbot takes over. They point to a 2026 study from the University of British Columbia that sorted it into three kinds: escapist roleplay, where people come to prefer the world the chatbot builds; pseudosocial companionship, where it replaces relationships; and the epistemic rabbit hole, compulsive question-asking that crowds out everything else. The same researchers blame the design, and call the chatbot an AI genie.

Examples

example

Someone says "I'm addicted to ChatGPT" because they use it for every email. That's heavy use and reliance, and it may be perfectly fine.

example

Someone skips meals and sleep to keep talking to a companion bot, and feels panicky when the app is down. That is the pattern researchers are trying to understand, and a reason to talk to someone qualified.

The criticism

Researchers disagree about whether the word "addiction" fits. A 2025 paper in Addictive Behaviors by Víctor Ciudad-Fernández, Cora von Hammerstein and Joël Billieux argued that the existing studies don't show the impaired control and real-world harm an addiction label needs, and warned that calling heavy use an addiction can stigmatise ordinary users. On the other side, the 2026 University of British Columbia study of 334 Reddit posts described patterns, such as escapist roleplay and companion relationships, that its authors mapped onto established signs of behavioural addiction. They argue that ignoring real problems carries its own risk.
