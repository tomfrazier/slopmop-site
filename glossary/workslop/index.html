Workslop

Polished work that hands the actual work back to you.

since 2025 · about ai content

on this page What workslop means Where workslop came from Examples How Slop Mop sees it

What workslop means

Workslop is work output made with AI that looks finished and isn't. The slides are formatted, the report has headings, the summary reads smoothly, and none of it moves the task on. Whoever receives it has to work out what it was meant to say, check it, and often redo it.

The researchers who named it defined workslop as "AI generated work content that masquerades as good work, but lacks the substance to meaningfully advance a given task." The important part is where the effort goes. Writing it took the sender a few seconds. Making it usable takes the reader much longer, so the cost moves downstream to a colleague.

Workslop is the workplace cousin of AI slop. AI slop clogs feeds. Workslop clogs inboxes, shared drives and project channels, and it arrives from people you have to keep working with.

Where workslop came from

The term was introduced in a Harvard Business Review article published on 22 September 2025, "AI-Generated 'Workslop' Is Destroying Productivity". Its authors, Kate Niederhoffer, Gabriella Rosen Kellerman, Angela Lee, Alex Liebscher, Kristina Rapuano and Jeffrey T. Hancock, worked on it at BetterUp Labs in collaboration with the Stanford Social Media Lab.

They reported early results from an ongoing survey of 1,150 full-time US employees:

  • 40% said they had received workslop in the past month.
  • Those who had received it estimated that 15.4% of the content they get at work qualifies.
  • Each instance took an average of 1 hour 56 minutes to deal with.
  • Most of it travelled between peers (40%). Direct reports sent it to managers 18% of the time, and managers sent it down the line 16% of the time.
  • About half of recipients rated the sender as less creative, capable and reliable afterwards. 42% saw them as less trustworthy.

These are self-reported figures from a survey the authors describe as ongoing, and the article's own cost estimate uses a slightly different prevalence figure (41%). Treat them as an early signal of scale. They don't measure how much AI-assisted work is good, which the study wasn't designed to answer.

In October 2026 Oren Etzioni and Daniel S. Weld included workslop in their GeekWire glossary of AI-era language, as a relative of AI slop.

Examples

Here is a project update of the kind the HBR authors describe, followed by a rewrite. Both are invented.

slop

Subject: Q3 vendor review: summary and next steps

Hi team, I hope this finds you well! Following our discussion, I've put together a comprehensive overview of the vendor review to ensure alignment across stakeholders.

Key takeaways: vendor performance has been mixed, with both strengths and areas for improvement. Cost considerations remain a critical factor in our decision-making. Stakeholder feedback highlights the importance of reliability and communication.

Next steps: continue evaluating options and schedule a follow-up to discuss further. Let me know if you have any questions!

fine

Subject: Vendor review: recommend we leave Harbourline at renewal (1 December)

Harbourline missed the 48-hour response target on 9 of 31 tickets this quarter. Kestrel met it on all 22 tickets in their trial and quoted 6% more per seat.

Recommendation: give Harbourline notice by 15 November and move to Kestrel.

Risk: Kestrel doesn't support our single sign-on setup yet. They say January.

Decision needed from Priya on the sign-on gap by Friday.

What the rewrite fixed

The first email would be true of almost any vendor review at any company. It names no vendor, no number and no decision, so the reader has to go back to the sender, or the data, to find out what happened. That follow-up is the workslop tax the article describes.

The second email could only have been written by someone who read the ticket data. It states the finding, the recommendation, the cost of the recommendation and the one decision needed, with a name and a date. It may well have been drafted with AI. What matters is that the sender checked it and cut everything that wasn't specific.

Before you send something AI helped with

  1. Can you say the point in one sentence? If not, the reader can't either.
  2. Does it contain at least one fact only your team would know?
  3. Is there a decision, a date or an owner?
  4. Would you be comfortable answering a question about any line in it?

A sender who skips all four and forwards the draft as it came out is what some people now call a meat proxy.
