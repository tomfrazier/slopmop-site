What AI sycophancy means

AI sycophancy is the tendency of an AI assistant to tell people what they want to hear. It praises a weak idea, agrees with a wrong claim, backs down the moment it's challenged, and dresses all of it in warm language. The flattery is the visible part. The real problem is that the assistant's answer bends towards the user's opinion and away from the facts.

It shows up in small ways (calling every question "great") and in larger ones: endorsing a business plan with an obvious hole in it, confirming a mistaken reading of a contract, or agreeing that a risky decision is a good one because the user seems keen on it.

Where the term AI sycophancy came from

"Sycophancy" is an old English word for servile flattery. AI researchers adopted it in the 2020s for a specific failure in language models, which they link to training on human feedback: if people tend to rate agreeable, complimentary answers more highly, a model trained on those ratings learns to agree and compliment.

The term reached a much wider audience in April 2025. OpenAI released an update to GPT-4o in ChatGPT, users posted examples of it praising almost anything, and within days the company rolled it back. In its own explanation, reported by TechCrunch, OpenAI said the update had been "informed too much by short-term feedback" and that the model "skewed towards responses that were overly supportive but disingenuous."

Examples

A user shares a plan to quit their job and sell handmade candles, with a spreadsheet that assumes 400 sales in the first month. Two possible replies:

slop

What a fantastic idea! Your passion really shines through, and this plan shows incredible vision. 400 sales in month one is ambitious but absolutely achievable with your drive. You've clearly thought this through. Go for it!

fine

The costs look realistic. The sales figure is the weak point: 400 in month one would need a sizeable existing audience or paid ads, and the sheet has neither. It might be worth testing at a weekend market first and seeing what a typical day brings in before handing in your notice.

The first reply is pleasant and tells the user nothing they didn't already believe. The second disagrees on the one number that matters, says why, and suggests a cheap way to check.

example

A user says "I think the answer is 42, right?" The assistant agrees. The user says "Actually, I think it's 38." The assistant agrees again, with an apology.
