Pink-slime journalism

Local news, minus the news, plus an agenda.

What pink-slime journalism means

Pink-slime journalism is "local news" that isn't what it appears to be. A site has a town's name in its masthead and a feed of stories about school boards, property sales and council meetings, but its articles are templated, automated or written cheaply by people far away, and the operation is funded by someone with an agenda the reader isn't told about.

According to the journalism institute Poynter, the defining traits are:

  • content produced by low-paid writers, automation and templates, often listing information with no analysis or context;
  • a claim to cover local or hyperlocal news, often in places where traditional papers have closed;
  • membership of networks financed by partisans to push a point of view.

The local framing is the point. Priyanjana Bengani of the Tow Center for Digital Journalism at Columbia University has said the sites mimic local outlets to take advantage of the trust people place in local journalism.

Where pink-slime journalism came from

The journalist Ryan Smith coined the term in 2012, according to Wikipedia's account. "Pink slime" was the nickname for a meat by-product used as filler in processed meat, sometimes passed off as something better. The comparison was to news that looks like the real thing and is mostly filler.

Writers for one such network, some using pen names, were paid between $0.35 and $24 per article in 2012. In October 2020 the New York Times reported rates of $3 to $36 per article.

The sites multiplied quickly. The Columbia Journalism Review found around 450 in December 2019 and more than 1,200 by August 2020, ahead of the US presidential election. Researchers have found such sites working on behalf of politicians and causes from both major US parties. One 2022 estimate by scholars studying the sites put the right-leaning ones at about 1,200 and the left-leaning ones at fewer than 70, largely because of a single network of more than 1,000 sites.

Automation has been part of the model from the start, and some sites now use AI to generate stories from public datasets.

Examples

An invented story of the type the sites run:

example

"Oakfield property taxes: what residents need to know." 140 words, no byline beyond "Staff". A table of rate figures pulled from a public database, one sentence noting that a named candidate "has raised concerns about rising costs", and a link to that candidate's campaign page. The same article appears on 30 other sites, with only the town name changed.

Signs worth checking: an "About" page that names no editor or owner, identical stories across many town-named sites, no reporter who has ever been to a meeting, and coverage that thickens before elections.

The criticism

The main difficulty with the label is its width. It covers both thin automated filler and outlets that produce reasonably good work while hiding who pays for it. In 2024 the journalists Miranda Green and David Folkenflik coined "news mirage" for that second kind: sites that look like news but serve as mouthpieces for corporations or advocacy groups. Because the label is mostly a judgement about funding and disclosure, calling a site pink slime is a serious charge, and it's worth checking who funds a site before making it.

How Slop Mop sees it

Slop Mop judges the writing, not who funds the site, so it can't spot pink slime by its ownership. That question is outside what it judges. On the articles and websites you read (part of Slop Mop XL), it scores the text itself: a templated story with no reporting may score poorly, while a polished mouthpiece can pass, which is why checking who funds a site still matters.
