Dead internet theory

Bots talking to bots.

since 2021 · about ai content

on this page What it means Where it came from Examples The criticism How Slop Mop sees it

What it means

Dead internet theory is the claim that the internet "died" some years ago, and that most of what now appears online, from posts and comments to likes and whole accounts, is generated by bots and AI, with relatively few real people left. Its strongest versions add a conspiracy: that this is being done on purpose, by governments or companies, to shape what people think.

It is a theory, and its big claims haven't been shown to be true. People use the phrase in two quite different ways:

  • The literal version: most of the internet is fake, and someone is behind it. There's no good evidence for this.
  • The loose version: a feeling that feeds are filling up with automated, recycled and low-effort material, and that it's getting harder to find a real person saying something they mean. Plenty of people who reject the conspiracy still recognise this.

Where it came from

The theory took its name from a forum thread titled "Dead Internet Theory: Most of the Internet is Fake", posted in January 2021 by an anonymous user on a small message board called Agora Road's Macintosh Cafe. Futurism, summarising the idea that September, reported that it dates the internet's death to around 2016 or 2017. It reached a much wider audience after an August 2021 article in The Atlantic by Kaitlyn Tiffany, whose headline summed up most commentators' view: the theory is wrong, but it feels true.

Interest returned with generative AI, which made large volumes of plausible text and images cheap to produce.

Examples

What a "dead internet" thread feels like, in our own invented example:

slop

Comment 1: Great insights! 🔥
Comment 2: So true, thanks for sharing!
Comment 3: This is a game-changer. Agree 100%!

fine

Comment: We tried this at a 12-person agency. It worked for proposals but slowed down invoicing, so we only use it for the first draft now.

The first three could be bots, or people clicking quickly between meetings. Nobody can tell from the text, and that ambiguity is what the theory feeds on.

The criticism

The main criticism is that there's no evidence for a coordinated plan or for "most people online are fake". The Atlantic article that made the theory famous called it wrong in its headline.

The measurable part is narrower. Thales' 2025 Imperva Bad Bot Report estimated that automated traffic made up 51% of web traffic in 2024, the first time in a decade it had passed human traffic. That figure counts all automated requests, including search crawlers and other benign bots, and comes from a security company that sells bot protection. Bot traffic means automated requests to websites, which is a long way from bots writing most of the posts people read.

How Slop Mop sees it

Slop Mop doesn't try to tell bots from people. On LinkedIn it judges the writing itself, so a hollow comment or post gets folded away whoever typed it, and a specific, useful one stays. That goes some way towards the loose version of the problem; it can't settle the literal one.
