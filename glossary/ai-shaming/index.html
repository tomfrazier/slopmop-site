AI shaming

Accusing someone of using AI.

since 2020s · about ai and human content

on this page What AI shaming means Where it came from Examples The criticism How Slop Mop avoids AI shaming

What AI shaming means

AI shaming is criticism or social pressure aimed at someone because they used AI, or because someone thinks they did. It ranges from a jokey "did ChatGPT write this?" in a comment thread to a student hauled before a misconduct panel, or a colleague whose work is quietly marked down.

It comes in a few forms:

  • The accusation. "This reads like AI", said to undermine a person or their work.
  • The comparison. "An AI could have written this", as a put-down.
  • The blame. Putting any weakness in a piece of work down to AI use.
  • The stigma. Treating anyone who admits using AI as lazy or less capable.

Where it came from

The term is newer and less settled than most in this glossary. Microsoft researcher Advait Sarkar gave it an academic footing in a paper presented at the ACM CHI conference in 2025, "AI Could Have Written This: Birth of a Classist Slur in Knowledge Work". It describes AI shaming as negative judgement attached to the use of AI, and argues it works as a way of policing who counts as a proper knowledge worker.

Oren Etzioni and Daniel S. Weld included it in their October 2026 GeekWire glossary of AI-era language, as the accusation that follows AI suspicion. Summarising Sarkar's paper, they note its argument that the accusations fall hardest on racial minorities and on writers whose first language isn't English.

Other research shows why it matters. In experiments published at CHI 2025, Kowe Kadoma, Danaë Metaxa and Mor Naaman found that suspecting a freelance writer of AI use lowered how readers rated both the work and the writer. A 2023 Stanford study found that AI-text detectors wrongly flagged many essays by non-native English writers as machine-made.

Examples

example

A team lead replies to a junior colleague's well-organised project summary with "Nice, which chatbot?" in front of the whole channel. She wrote it herself on the train.

example

A writer adds "No AI was used in this post" to the top of every article after readers accused her of it twice. The articles were never the problem.

example

A useful comparison: "This post doesn't say what the tool costs or who it's for" is a criticism of the writing. "You obviously used AI" is a claim about the person, and usually one nobody can check.

The criticism

Some people defend the instinct behind it. They see calling out AI use as a fair response to AI slop, deception or work passed off as personal effort, and argue that "AI shaming" can be used to wave away legitimate complaints. The disagreement is mostly about the target: criticising a piece of empty writing is one thing; accusing a person of how they made it, without evidence, is another.

How Slop Mop avoids AI shaming

Slop Mop judges content, never people, and it doesn't flag a post for suspected AI use. It is not an AI detector (how it compares with one). A useful AI-assisted post stays in your feed and an empty human-written one can be folded away, so its verdicts describe what the post gives the reader, whether that post is on LinkedIn, X, Reddit, Facebook, Substack or Medium. AI suspicion

AI slop

Low-quality content made in bulk, usually with generative AI. Merriam-Webster's word of the year for 2025.

AI cream

Etzioni and Weld's name for the rare opposite of slop: superb writing made with the help of AI.

Counterfeit people

AI systems or synthetic profiles presented as real humans without saying so. Philosopher Daniel Dennett's 2023 term.
