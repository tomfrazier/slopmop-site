Clickbait

A headline that writes cheques the article can't cash.

also click-bait, link bait, linkbait · since 1999 · about ai and human content

on this page What it means Where it came from Examples The criticism How Slop Mop sees it

What it means

Clickbait is a headline, thumbnail or link text designed to make you click, usually by withholding the one piece of information that would let you decide whether the click is worth it. Merriam-Webster defines it as "something designed to make readers want to click on a hyperlink especially when the link leads to content of dubious value or interest."

The usual mechanism is the curiosity gap: tell readers just enough to make them curious and not enough to satisfy them. "You won't believe what this landlord found in the loft" works because it names a surprise and hides it. The page behind it is often thinner than the headline suggested, which is why the most common working definition is a headline that overpromises and underdelivers.

People draw the line in different places:

  • Strict: the headline is dishonest about the content. In 2014 BuzzFeed's then editor-in-chief, Ben Smith, said his site avoided clickbait on this definition, arguing that an eye-catching headline is fine if the article delivers what it promised.
  • Facebook's 2014 version: a headline that "encourages people to click to see more, without telling them much information about what they will see."
  • Loose: any headline written mainly to be clicked, honest or not.

The word now covers more than headlines. A video thumbnail with a shocked face and a red arrow is clickbait. So is a social post whose first two lines exist only to make you press "see more".

Where it came from

Merriam-Webster dates the first known use of "clickbait" to 1999. The "-bait" half is a fishing image: a hook disguised as something worth swallowing. The technique is much older than the word. Sensational, exaggerated front pages were the stock in trade of yellow journalism in the 1890s, and bait-and-switch was a sales trick long before the web.

What changed was the money. Ad-funded sites earned more with every page view, and social networks showed posts that got clicks to more people. Through the early 2010s the curiosity-gap headline ("This is what happens when…", "Number 7 will shock you") became the defining complaint about online publishing.

The backlash arrived in 2014. The Onion launched ClickHole, a site parodying viral publishers. On 25 August 2014 Facebook announced it would show fewer "click-baiting" headlines in News Feed. One signal it named was time spent: if people clicked a link and came straight back, the story probably hadn't delivered. It said that in an initial survey, people preferred headlines that helped them decide whether to read the full article 80% of the time.

Examples

Two headlines for the same story, a council report on bin collections:

slop

The council just made a change to your bins, and residents are FURIOUS 😳

fine

Bin collections in Northgate move to fortnightly from 3 March; recycling stays weekly

The first hides what changed, who it affects and when. The second gives you all three, so you can skip it if you don't live in Northgate. That's the point: a fair headline lets readers decide without clicking.

The same move on LinkedIn, where the "link" is the "see more" button:

slop

I almost didn't post this.

But what happened in yesterday's board meeting changed how I think about leadership forever.

(The ending surprised everyone.) 👇

fine

Our board approved a four-day week trial for the support team, starting in January. Here's the plan, the cost and what we'll measure.

How to spot it

  1. The headline promises a reaction ("shocked", "furious", "you won't believe") instead of a fact.
  2. The key noun is hidden behind "this", "one thing" or "what happened next".
  3. There's a number with no subject: "7 reasons", reasons for what?
  4. After clicking, you could have written the headline yourself from the article's first paragraph.

The criticism

"Clickbait" gets stretched. It's sometimes used for any headline someone dislikes, including accurate ones that are simply unflattering to a person. A catchy headline that delivers what it promises fits the loose definition at most, and some editors argue it isn't clickbait at all. When the word is used as a general insult for headlines, it stops telling you anything useful.
