AI suspicion

Your bullshit detector activating.

also AI-dar, Turing tingle · about ai content

on this page What it means Where it came from Examples How Slop Mop sees it

What it means

AI suspicion is the moment you read something and think "a machine wrote this". It might be a LinkedIn post that sounds like every other LinkedIn post, a customer service reply that answers a slightly different question, or a profile photo with too-perfect skin. People also call it AI-dar or a Turing tingle.

It's a hunch. Sometimes it's right. It can also fire on plenty of things people write themselves: formal English, corporate house style, a template, or a writer who simply likes em dashes.

Where it came from

The phrase is plain description, and AI-dar (after "gaydar") and Turing tingle (after Alan Turing's imitation game) are informal jokey variants. None has a known coiner. They became common once chatbots made fluent text cheap, from late 2022.

Research suggests the hunch is shaky ground for judging people. A Stanford team tested seven AI-text detectors in 2023 and found they misclassified a large share of essays written by non-native English speakers as AI-generated, while essays by native speakers were mostly classed correctly. A 2025 CHI paper by Kowe Kadoma, Danaë Metaxa and Mor Naaman found that when readers suspected a writer had used AI, they rated the writing and the writer lower, whether or not it was true. The authors call this a "perceptual harm".

Examples

Two posts that might set off the tingle:

slop

In today's fast-paced world, communication isn't just important. It's everything. Here are 5 powerful tips to elevate your messaging. 👇

fine

Our weekly update email had a 22% open rate. We cut it from 900 words to 150 and put the one decision we needed in the subject line. Open rate went to 41%.

The first one may have been typed by a person and the second drafted by AI. The useful reaction to the first is the same either way: it's empty, so move on.

example

A reader replies to a careful, formal comment with "ChatGPT wrote this". The commenter writes English as a third language and learned it from textbooks.

How Slop Mop sees it

Slop Mop doesn't act on AI suspicion. It skips the authorship question and asks concrete ones instead, such as whether a post leans on stiff phrasing or "not X, but Y" framing, and whether it tells readers anything useful. Those are things a reader can check. Who wrote a post usually isn't.
