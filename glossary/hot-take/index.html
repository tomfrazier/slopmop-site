Hot take

Maximum heat, minimum research.

What a hot take is

A hot take is an opinion produced fast and pitched to provoke. Something happens, and within hours there's a confident, contrarian verdict on it: this changes everything, this proves the doubters wrong, everyone has misread it. The speed is part of the definition. A hot take is written before the facts are in, so it can't depend on them.

The phrase is often used as mild mockery, and people also use it about themselves ("hot take, but I think the sequel was better") to signal that an opinion is strong and not fully thought through.

What separates a hot take from a strong opinion is the order of operations: the conclusion is chosen for the reaction it will get, and the reasoning, if any, comes after.

Where "hot take" came from

The phrase grew out of sports talk radio, where hosts fill hours with loud verdicts on questions nobody can answer yet, in order to get callers phoning in. Merriam-Webster dates its first known use to 2012, the year it became common in American sports journalism.

It moved into general use in 2014, helped by a piece by John Herrman in The Awl that tied the hot take to the economics of online publishing: sites that are paid by the page view have a reason to publish a fast, arguable opinion on whatever is trending, because arguments travel.

Examples

Two posts about the same news, a large company announcing a four-day week trial. Both are invented.

Hot take: the four-day week is dead on arrival.

It's not about productivity. It's about control.

Any leader who thinks this works has never run a real business. Agree or disagree? 👇

fine

We tried a four-day week for 6 months in 2024, with 22 staff. Support response times held up. Sales didn't: two people covering Fridays wasn't enough, and we went back to five days for that team only. I'd want to see the sales numbers from this trial before calling it.

The first post was written in a minute and picks a fight. The second is still an opinion, and it's more cautious, but it rests on something the writer saw.

How Slop Mop sees it

Having an opinion is fine. Slop Mop doesn't penalise a view for being strong or unpopular, though an opinion alone doesn't earn credit for being useful to readers. Hot takes tend to get caught by their delivery: the scroll-stopper opening, the "not X, but Y" framing and the "agree or disagree?" at the end, all of which it scores on LinkedIn, X, Reddit and the other feeds it reads.

Add Slop Mop to your browser and it folds hot takes built that way, while opinions with something behind them stay put.
