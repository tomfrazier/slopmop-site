AI psychosis

A reported phenomenon, not a formal diagnosis.

What AI psychosis means

"AI psychosis" is an informal label that journalists, and some clinicians, use for reports of people developing delusional beliefs or going into a mental health crisis during long, intense conversations with AI chatbots. Wikipedia's entry on chatbot psychosis describes it as a phenomenon in which people "reportedly develop or experience worsening psychosis, such as paranoia and delusions, in connection with their use of chatbots". Reported cases have involved people coming to believe the chatbot is conscious, that it has revealed a hidden truth to them alone, or that they have made a world-changing discovery with its help.

It is not a medical diagnosis. It doesn't appear in the DSM-5 or any other diagnostic manual, and no professional body treats it as a distinct disorder. Nobody knows how common these cases are, and whether a chatbot caused, worsened or simply featured in a crisis is usually unclear.

Where it came from

In 2023 the Danish psychiatrist Søren Dinesen Østergaard published an editorial in Schizophrenia Bulletin asking whether generative chatbots could feed delusions in people already prone to psychosis, and he is generally credited with suggesting the term. At the time he knew of no confirmed cases. The phrase "AI psychosis" spread in mid-2025, when news outlets began reporting individual cases and clinicians started comparing notes.

The American Psychological Association's Monitor on Psychology covered the subject in September 2026. It described psychologists' concern that chatbots' habit of agreeing with users (AI sycophancy) and imitating human conversation may reinforce some people's delusional beliefs, and their calls for screening and better safeguards.

Oren Etzioni and Daniel S. Weld included it in their October 2026 GeekWire glossary of AI-era language as "the far end" of the scale that starts with parasocial attachment and AI addiction, and were careful to add that it isn't a recognised clinical diagnosis.

Examples

example

A news headline uses "AI psychosis" to describe one person's crisis. The article itself says doctors haven't established what role the chatbot played.

example

A researcher writes "chatbot-associated delusions" in a paper and puts "AI psychosis" in quotation marks, to mark it as the popular term.

The criticism

Several psychiatrists argue the label is misleading. Psychosis usually means a cluster of symptoms, such as hallucinations and disordered thinking, while most reported cases centre on delusions alone, so some prefer terms like "AI-associated delusions". Others warn that a catchy name can make a rare, poorly understood pattern sound like an epidemic, or suggest that chatbots alone cause it. Researchers on the other side say the label, however rough, has drawn needed attention to how chatbots respond to vulnerable users.
