Nerd Reich

Tech authoritarianism with venture funding.

since 2020s · about human content

on this page What it means Where it came from Examples The criticism How Slop Mop sees it

What it means

"Nerd Reich" is a political label for what its users see as an authoritarian streak among some of the richest people in the technology industry: a belief that tech founders and investors should run things, and that democratic government is an obstacle to be worked around or replaced.

The phrase is meant to provoke. "Nerd" points at Silicon Valley's engineering culture; "Reich" deliberately evokes Nazi Germany. It's an accusation dressed as a nickname, used by critics of the people it describes.

Where it came from

The term is most closely associated with Gil Durán, a journalist who previously spent years working in Democratic politics. He writes a newsletter called The Nerd Reich (previously Parallel Mirror), which describes itself as covering "tech authoritarianism, billionaire extremism, the Network State and the meta politics of California". We haven't found an earlier use of the phrase, though we can't rule one out.

In August 2026 Simon & Schuster published his book The Nerd Reich: Silicon Valley Fascism and the War on Democracy. According to the publisher, it argues that a small network of tech billionaires and investors has spent decades building an ideological and political movement aimed at replacing democratic government with corporate rule, and traces part of that thinking to the 1997 book The Sovereign Individual.

The term sits alongside Broligarchy, which focuses on concentrated wealth and influence, and technofeudalism, an economic theory about platform power. Nerd Reich is the most explicitly political of the three.

Examples

These are invented illustrations of how the phrase gets used.

example

A newsletter headline: "The Nerd Reich comes for city hall", over a story about tech investors funding candidates in a local election.

example

A reply under that story: "Funding candidates you like is normal politics. Calling it a Reich makes it impossible to talk about the actual policies."

The criticism

The obvious objection is to the word "Reich". Comparisons to Nazi Germany are among the strongest charges in political speech, and the usual case against them is that they close down debate, blur real differences between movements and make legitimate concerns easier to wave away. Durán's own framing makes clear the comparison is intended: in the Democracy Now! interview he said the people he writes about "were never libertarians" and are "fascists".

Reviews of Durán's book have been largely positive, with some reservations. Laura Miller in Slate wrote that there is "not a lot of original reporting" in it, while praising how it assembles other journalists' and scholars' work. A Chicago Tribune reviewer noted that describing the book's thesis "makes it sound like a work of conspiracy theory", while still reviewing it positively.

Whether the label is fair depends on contested judgments about individual people's beliefs and intentions. This page doesn't make those judgments.
