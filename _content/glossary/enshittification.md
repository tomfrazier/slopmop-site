## What it means

Enshittification is the writer Cory Doctorow's name for a specific pattern of decline in online platforms. In his words: "first, they are good to their users; then they abuse their users to make things better for their business customers; finally, they abuse those business customers to claw back all the value for themselves. Then, they die."

The three stages, roughly:

1. **Good to users.** The service is cheap or free and pleasant, and people join and stay.
2. **Good to business customers.** Once users are locked in, the platform shifts value to advertisers, sellers or publishers: more ads, more paid placement.
3. **Good to itself.** Once those businesses depend on it too, the platform squeezes them as well, through higher fees, less reach and more charges for visibility.

The engine is lock-in. It works because leaving is costly: your friends, your followers, your sales history or your audience are all on the platform. How far any particular service has gone down this path varies a lot, and changes over time.

## Where it came from

Doctorow coined the word in a 2022 blog post, and set out the full argument in a January 2023 essay for WIRED about TikTok. It caught on fast. The American Dialect Society chose it as its Digital Word of the Year for 2023, and in November 2024 Australia's Macquarie Dictionary made it word of the year, with its committee saying it "captures what many of us feel is happening to the world". Doctorow turned the idea into a book, *Enshittification: Why Everything Suddenly Got Worse and What to Do About It*, published in 2025.

## Examples

Invented examples of how the word gets used:

:::example
**Close to Doctorow's sense:** "When I joined, the feed was just people I followed. Now it's a third ads, a third 'suggested for you', and my own posts reach about 5% of the people who follow me unless I pay to boost them."
:::
:::example
**The loose sense:** "They moved the settings button again. Total enshittification."
:::

The first describes value moving from users to advertisers and then to the platform. The second is a complaint about a redesign.

## The criticism

The most common complaint is that the word has stretched to cover any change someone dislikes, which blunts its meaning. A review of Doctorow's book in Compact pointed out that he gives readers "explicit permission" to use the word loosely, and objected to exactly that. The useful test is whether value has actually moved from users to businesses and then to the platform. A redesign you don't like, or a service that has simply got worse, is a different complaint.

## How Slop Mop sees it

Slop Mop doesn't change how any platform ranks or pays for posts, and it can't undo stage three. It works on what reaches your screen: on LinkedIn it folds away the [engagement bait](/patterns/engagement-bait/) and hooks that crowd a feed, without leaving the platform or muting anyone.
