## What it means

A deepfake is an image, video or audio recording made or altered with machine learning so that it looks or sounds real. The classic case swaps one person's face onto another's body, or clones a voice, so a real person appears to say or do something they never did. The word now also covers wholly generated people and scenes.

Synthetic media isn't automatically deceptive, and the word is usually kept for the deceptive kind. A film studio de-ageing an actor with their consent, a museum animating a historical portrait and labelling it, or a person using a cloned version of their own voice after losing the ability to speak are all made the same way. What makes something a deepfake in the everyday sense is a fake presented, or likely to be taken, as real, often of someone who didn't agree to it.

## Where it came from

The word combines "deep learning", the machine-learning technique behind it, with "fake". It started as a username. In December 2017, Samantha Cole reported for Vice's Motherboard on a Reddit user called "deepfakes" who was posting pornographic videos with celebrities' faces swapped in using open-source machine-learning tools. The name spread from that account to the technique and then to the whole category.

Those origins in non-consensual sexual imagery have stayed part of the story. Legal scholars Bobby Chesney and Danielle Citron's 2019 California Law Review article set out the wider risks, to privacy, elections and national security, and also coined the [liar's dividend](/glossary/liars-dividend/): the chance for people caught on genuine recordings to claim they were faked.

## Examples

Invented examples, for illustration only:

:::example
**Deepfake:** a voice message that sounds exactly like a company's finance director, asking a junior colleague to move money urgently before a deadline. The director never recorded it.
:::
:::example
**Synthetic but consented and labelled:** a training video in which a presenter's AI avatar delivers the same course in six languages, with an on-screen note saying so.
:::

Low-tech tricks such as slowing a clip or adding a false caption are a different thing, usually called a [cheapfake](/glossary/cheapfake/).

If something seems designed to make you angry or to act fast, check for the original source, a second outlet reporting it, or a direct confirmation from the person shown before you share or act on it.

## How Slop Mop sees it

Slop Mop doesn't detect deepfakes. It reads the text of LinkedIn posts, not their images, audio or video, so it can't tell you whether a clip is real. It can flag the writing that often comes wrapped around one, such as an urgent caption asking you to share, but a calm caption on a fake video would get past it.
