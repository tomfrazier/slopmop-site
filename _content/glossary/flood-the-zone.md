## What it means

To flood the zone is to put out so much at once (announcements, claims, controversies, posts) that opponents and journalists can't respond to all of it. Each item gets less scrutiny because the next one has already arrived. Coverage scatters, attention moves on, and whatever the sender would rather nobody examined closely gets lost in the volume.

The phrase describes a volume strategy. Some of what floods the zone may be true and newsworthy; the point is the pace. It sits close to the [firehose of falsehood](/glossary/firehose-of-falsehood/), a term from propaganda research about high-volume, multi-channel messaging, and to [Brandolini's law](/glossary/brandolinis-law/), the observation that refuting nonsense takes far more effort than producing it.

## Where it came from

The phrase is attributed to Steve Bannon, the former chief strategist to Donald Trump. In 2018 Bannon told the writer Michael Lewis: "The Democrats don't matter... The real opposition is the media. And the way to deal with them is to flood the zone with shit." The full, cruder version is where the term's alias comes from.

Commentators have since used the phrase to describe the pace of announcements and executive orders early in Trump's second term. It hasn't stayed with one side. During that term, Democratic politicians, including Representative Maxwell Frost and House Democratic leader Hakeem Jeffries, called on their own party to communicate more often and more widely, with Frost using the words "flood the zone" himself.

## Examples

:::example
A company facing a damaging report announces a product launch, a partnership and a leadership change in the same 48 hours. Coverage of the report shrinks to a paragraph in a busy news cycle.
:::

:::example
A campaign publishes dozens of short claims a day across several platforms. Fact-checkers can examine three of them before the next batch arrives.
:::

## The criticism

The phrase is mostly used as a charge against opponents, and the line between flooding the zone and simply communicating a lot is blurry. A government with many policies, or a party that wants more press coverage, can be accused of it for doing what busy organisations do. The term also tends to credit a deliberate plan where the cause may be a news cycle that rewards volume on its own. Its spread across the political spectrum suggests many people now see high volume as a tactic anyone can use, which weakens it as a description of one camp.
