## What broligarchy means

Broligarchy blends "bro" and "oligarchy". Cambridge Dictionary, which added it in August 2025, defines it as "a small group of men, especially men owning or involved in a technology business, who are extremely rich and powerful, and who have or want political influence." A member is a broligarch. [Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/slang/broligarchy) has a similar slang entry: "a small group of extremely wealthy men, particularly those in technology, who wield significant political and economic power."

It's a critical term. People who use it are making an argument: that a handful of technology executives have gained too much influence over politics and public life, and that the platforms they own give them unusual power over what people see. People who reject that argument tend to reject the word too. Read it as political rhetoric: a label with a point of view about who holds power, which plenty of people dispute.

## Where broligarchy came from

Informal uses go back a long way. Wikipedia traces the modern use of "broligarch" to a Twitter post in 2009, and the word floated around slang sites for years after.

The current political sense was popularised by the British journalist Carole Cadwalladr. She writes that she first used "broligarch" in July 2024, in a piece for The Observer about tech bro oligarchs, and she later named her newsletter *How to Survive the Broligarchy*. The term spread quickly in coverage of the 2024 US presidential election and of the January 2025 presidential inauguration, which several of the best-known technology chief executives attended. Cambridge Dictionary's entry followed later that year.

Oren Etzioni and Daniel S. Weld included it in their [October 2026 GeekWire glossary of AI-era language](https://www.geekwire.com/2026/etzioni-on-ai-the-colorful-and-cranky-language-of-the-ai-era-from-slop-to-slap/), next to [the Nerd Reich](/glossary/nerd-reich/) and [technofeudalism](/glossary/technofeudalism/). Their account matches the timeline above: the word "sat on Twitter from 2009 and went nowhere" until Cadwalladr wrote about "a new super-class of broligarchs" in July 2024.

## Examples

Three typical ways the word turns up, in our own illustrations:

:::example
**Commentary:** "The broligarchy now owns the platforms where the election will be argued about." An opinion writer using the term to make a case.
:::
:::example
**Reporting about the term:** "Critics of the administration have taken to calling its tech allies a 'broligarchy'." Attributed, so the reader knows whose word it is.
:::
:::example
**Casual use:** "Another week, another broligarch with a podcast." Shorthand among people who already share the view.
:::

The second form is the one to use if you want to describe the debate without taking a side in it.

## The criticism

Critics say the word does more work as an insult than as analysis. Writing in the Daily Sceptic in December 2024, James Alexander argued that "bro" turns an argument about power into one about the sex of the people holding it, and that calling them oligarchs would be the more serious charge. He also urged critics on the left to stop lumping everything together. Some dispute Cadwalladr's claim to have coined it, given the older uses.

Cadwalladr's defence, as quoted in the same piece, is that "to name is to understand": a short, memorable word helps people see a pattern she thinks deserves attention.
