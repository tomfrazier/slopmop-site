## What it means

Content shock is what happens when the supply of content keeps growing and the amount of time people have to read it doesn't. Past that point, each new blog post, video or newsletter competes harder for the same fixed attention, so it costs more to get noticed and most of what's published is barely seen.

The idea is plain supply and demand. When content was scarce, a modest blog post could find readers. Once every niche filled up, getting the same readers took more money, more promotion or higher quality, and for some businesses it stopped being worth it.

## Where it came from

The marketing writer Mark Schaefer coined the term. He says he scribbled "The world is in content shock" in a note on a flight in the summer of 2013, then waited months before publishing the idea in a blog post on 6 January 2014. The post argued that content marketing, then sold as a reliable way to bring in leads for free, wasn't a sustainable strategy for many businesses.

Schaefer compared it with early television. When channels were hungry for programmes, anyone could make a cooking show; as the channels filled up, production costs rose and only the best-funded shows competed. He saw the same thing happening to blogs and social platforms.

Writing on the post's tenth anniversary in January 2024, he said the industry had come to use the term for the competition in a world saturated with content, and that he'd met people who described content shock to him without knowing he'd coined it.

## Examples

:::example
An invented case: a software company publishes 3 blog posts a week in 2014 and gets steady traffic. By 2018 it publishes 5 a week, pays for promotion, and gets less. Nothing about the posts got worse; there were simply more competitors for the same readers.
:::

Generative AI has made the supply side much cheaper again. A content calendar that once took a team now takes one person and a prompt, and every one of those posts asks a reader for the same few seconds.

## The criticism

Schaefer says the post was called controversial at the time, and that prominent content marketers dismissed it, including a podcast host who laughed when asked whether it had any legitimacy and described content marketing as having unlimited potential. Schaefer responded with a book, *The Content Code*, built on the premise that "the economic value of content that is not seen and shared is zero".

## How Slop Mop sees it

Content shock is the reason a filter is useful at all: there's more in the feed than anyone can read. Slop Mop reads each LinkedIn post ahead of you and folds away the ones whose writing shows the signs of slop, so the limited time you do have goes to the rest. It never deletes anything, and one click reopens a folded post.
