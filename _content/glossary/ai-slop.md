## What AI slop means

AI slop is low-quality content produced in bulk, usually with generative AI, and pushed at people who never asked for it. It covers text, images and video: the uncanny stock image, the 40-page ebook "written" in an afternoon, the LinkedIn post that opens with a hook and ends with a request to repost.

[Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/wordplay/word-of-the-year), which made it the 2025 word of the year, defines slop as "digital content of low quality that is produced usually in quantity by means of artificial intelligence". Macquarie Dictionary's version adds two useful details: AI slop often contains errors, and it is "not requested by the user".

Three things tend to mark it out:

- **Volume over value.** It's cheap to make, so a lot of it gets made.
- **Nobody checked it.** Errors, filler and invented details survive because no person read it closely before it went out.
- **It takes more attention than it gives.** You have to read some of it to find out there was nothing there.

## Where the word AI slop came from

"Slop" meant soft mud in the 1700s and pig feed in the 1800s, which tells you how people feel about the modern sense. Its AI meaning grew up as in-group slang around 2022, when image generators arrived and people on forums such as 4chan, Hacker News and YouTube comment threads started using it for the flood of odd, glossy pictures. Nobody is credited with coining it.

The programmer and blogger Simon Willison helped carry it into the mainstream. On 8 May 2024 he wrote that "slop" was becoming the name for unwanted AI-generated content, the way "spam" became the name for unwanted email. He was careful to add that not all AI output is slop: the word fits content that was generated mindlessly and put in front of someone who didn't ask for it. The Guardian and the New York Times picked the term up within weeks.

By 2025 it had spread to the dictionaries:

## Examples

A post written to fill a slot in a content calendar:

:::slop
AI isn't just a tool. It's a mindset. 🚀 In today's fast-paced world, the leaders who embrace it will unlock unprecedented growth. Agree? ♻️ Repost to help your network.
:::
:::fine
We used an AI model to draft first replies to support tickets for six weeks. Median response time fell from 9 hours to 2. Refund requests still go to a person, because the drafts kept promising refunds we don't offer.
:::

Suppose the second one was drafted with AI help too. It's still worth reading: it has numbers, a limit and something you could copy.

:::example
A recipe site where every page has the same 600-word preamble about "the joy of home cooking", an ingredient list that doesn't match the method, and a photo of a dish that was never cooked.
:::

## Human-written slop

The word grew up attached to AI, but the qualities that make something slop are older than ChatGPT. Engagement-bait posts, keyword-stuffed blog pages, press releases full of "game-changing" and the motivational story about the cleaner who taught the CEO a lesson were all around in 2015. A person typed every word.

If you take the AI part away, the definition still works: low-value content made in bulk, with nobody checking whether it helps the reader. That is why "Is this AI?" is a poor question to ask of a post. A careful writer can use AI and produce something useful; a person on autopilot can produce slop by hand. What the reader loses is the same either way.

## The criticism

Because the word is handy and insulting, it gets stretched. Some people use "slop" for any content that involved AI at all, however good. Others use it for anything they dislike. Both uses blur a useful word, and the first can land on writers who were never using AI in the first place: accusing someone of it is its own problem (see [AI shaming](/glossary/ai-shaming/) and [AI suspicion](/glossary/ai-suspicion/)).

There's also a fair point that "slop" describes a reader's reaction as much as the content. One reader's filler is another's useful reminder.
