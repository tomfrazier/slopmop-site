## What a click farm is

A click farm is a business that sells the appearance of attention. Workers are paid to click on ads or links, like pages, follow accounts, watch videos or leave reviews, all for a client who wants the numbers to go up. Some farms are rooms of people working through lists; others are racks of phones and SIM cards running the same actions over and over.

Click farms serve two kinds of customer. One wants to look popular, which shades into [astroturfing](/glossary/astroturfing/): a brand, a musician, an influencer or a product listing that benefits from a big follower count or a wall of five-star reviews. The other wants money from advertising: fake clicks on ads can earn a publisher a fraudulent payout, or drain a rival's ad budget.

Click farms are harder to catch than simple bots because the traffic comes from real devices, and often real people, behaving the way real visitors do.

## Where click farms came from

Click farms grew up alongside pay-per-click advertising and social media metrics. Once a like, a follow or a view had a market value, someone started selling them. [Wikipedia's entry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Click_farm) describes the trade as concentrated in lower-wage countries, with workers paid on average about one US dollar per thousand likes or follows, which the farms then resell at a much higher price.

The scale became visible through the platforms' own clean-ups. Facebook purged fake likes and accounts in 2012, and its 2014 financial report estimated 83 million false accounts, about 6.4% of its total at the time. Instagram's December 2014 purge was nicknamed the "Instagram Rapture". In Thailand in June 2017, police found a farm with hundreds of phones and several hundred thousand SIM cards used to boost likes and views on WeChat.

Buying fake engagement breaks the rules of every major platform. Wikipedia notes that it isn't generally illegal in itself, though lawyers have argued that it can breach consumer protection rules by misleading buyers.

## Examples

:::example
A new account with 40,000 followers, three posts, and around 12 likes on each one.
:::
:::example
A product with 900 five-star reviews posted in the same fortnight, many using the same phrases in a different order.
:::
:::example
A video whose view count jumps by 200,000 overnight while its comments stay at four.
:::

None of these proves a click farm on its own; viral luck and enthusiastic fans can produce odd numbers too. The pattern to look for is attention that doesn't behave like attention: big counts with no conversation, no history and no follow-through.

## How Slop Mop sees it

Slop Mop can't tell whether a like was bought. Its verdict rests mainly on the writing, but one of its three counter-signals, [reader response](/how-it-works.html#response), is counted from the reactions, comments and reposts under a post. That signal discounts hollow engagement (lots of reactions, few comments or reposts) and can only take part of a score away, so a post padded with cheap likes still has to get past the nine tells. Bought numbers look much the same on LinkedIn, X, Facebook and Reddit, and Slop Mop reads posts on all of them. It doesn't judge accounts, so a network of [sock puppets](/glossary/sock-puppet/) posting passable text is outside what it can see.
