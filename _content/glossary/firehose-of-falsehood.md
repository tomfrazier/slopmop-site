## What it means

The firehose of falsehood is a description of propaganda that works by volume. Instead of crafting one careful message, the source sends a constant, fast stream of claims through as many channels as it can: state TV, websites, social media accounts, paid commenters. Some claims are partly true, some are invented, and they don't have to agree with each other.

The RAND report that named it set out four features:

1. High volume, spread across many channels.
2. Rapid, continuous and repetitive.
3. No commitment to objective reality.
4. No commitment to consistency.

The model works on attention. Messages that arrive in greater volume and from more sources tend to be more persuasive, the authors noted. Repetition breeds familiarity, and, in the report's words, "familiarity leads to acceptance". And when the claims keep changing, fact-checkers are always a step behind.

## Where it came from

Christopher Paul and Miriam Matthews of the RAND Corporation, a US research organisation, used the phrase in a short 2016 paper about Russian state propaganda, "The Russian 'Firehose of Falsehood' Propaganda Model". They described its "high numbers of channels and messages" and "a shameless willingness to disseminate partial truths or outright fictions", and drew on psychology research to explain why it might work.

Their advice on countering it is the line most often quoted from the paper: "Don't expect to counter the firehose of falsehood with the squirt gun of truth." Their suggestions leaned towards warning audiences in advance, getting accurate information out early and reducing the reach of the channels carrying the falsehoods, more than rebutting each claim one by one.

The phrase soon spread beyond its original subject. In 2017 Mother Jones applied the RAND model point by point to Donald Trump's statements as US president, and commentators have since used it of other governments, politicians and campaigns. It's closely related to "[flood the zone](/glossary/flood-the-zone/)" and to [Brandolini's law](/glossary/brandolinis-law/), the observation that refuting nonsense takes far more effort than producing it.

## Examples

:::example
Within hours of an event, dozens of accounts and outlets offer conflicting explanations of who was responsible. Some contradict each other. By the time any single claim has been checked, three newer ones are circulating.
:::
:::example
A source repeats a disputed figure in every interview for a week. When it's corrected, the figure changes, and the correction becomes the next talking point.
:::

## The criticism

The RAND paper was a short "perspective" built from observation and existing psychology research. It didn't measure how well the technique worked. Its authors framed their account as why it "might work".

Since then, the main criticism has been about how widely the label is applied. Calling something a firehose of falsehood is a claim about intent: that the volume and the falsehoods are a deliberate strategy. That is hard to show, and a lot of high-volume, careless or contradictory messaging comes from disorganisation rather than design. The risk is that the phrase turns into a way to dismiss opponents without engaging with what they said. Applied carefully, it describes a pattern; applied loosely, it becomes a political insult.

## How Slop Mop sees it

Whether a statement is true, and whether it's part of a propaganda campaign, is outside what Slop Mop judges. It doesn't fact-check, and it reads each LinkedIn post on its own, so it can't see a coordinated stream of them. What it can sometimes catch is the writing that rides along: posts built for reaction, with [hype words](/patterns/hype-words/) and nothing a reader could check.
