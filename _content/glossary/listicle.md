## What it means

A listicle is an article structured as a list: a title with a number in it ("17 ways to…", "The 10 worst…") and a short section for each item. The word is a blend of "list" and "article".

The form itself is neutral. A ranked list of the year's best albums, a checklist for moving house or a set of steps to fix a bug can all be the clearest way to present the material. The word usually carries a sneer, though, because the list is so often a way to stretch one thin idea across a dozen headings and a dozen scrolls.

On social feeds the listicle has a cousin: the post that opens "7 lessons I learned from failing" and follows with seven one-line platitudes.

## Where it came from

Merriam-Webster dates the first known use of "listicle" to 2007. The habit it names is older. Magazine covers such as *Cosmopolitan*'s had carried numbered lists for years, and in 2006 the blogger Anil Dash was already disparaging list posts as the "geek equivalents of Cosmo coverlines".

Lists are quick to make. Narrative writing needs flow from one point to the next, while a list just needs items, and it can be assembled from material that already exists, such as captions under video clips. That ease is why it drew the charge of being a "kind of cheap content-creation". By 2015 sites such as BuzzFeed were publishing hundreds a day.

## Examples

Two invented LinkedIn posts on the same subject:

:::slop
7 things every great leader does (most people miss #4) 👇

1. They listen.
2. They lead by example.
3. They empower their team.
4. They embrace failure.
5. They communicate clearly.
6. They never stop learning.
7. They show up.

Which one resonates most with you? ♻️ Repost to help a leader in your network.
:::
:::fine
3 things that cut our weekly meeting from 90 minutes to 35:

1. Updates go in the doc by Monday 5pm. The meeting only covers blockers.
2. Each agenda item names an owner and the decision needed. No owner, no slot.
3. We stop at 35 minutes even if items are left. They roll to the doc.

It took three weeks before people stopped bringing status updates anyway.
:::

The first list could be pasted under any leadership post ever written. Each item is a claim with nothing behind it, and the frame around the list is a hook and a request for reposts. The second list is shorter and every item is specific enough to try, with a result and a cost.

## How Slop Mop sees it

A list on its own doesn't make a post slop to Slop Mop. The padded kind usually gives itself away through other tells, typically a [scroll-stopper](/patterns/scroll-stopper/) title, [engagement bait](/patterns/engagement-bait/) at the end and [empty praise](/patterns/empty-praise/) in between, and those are what it scores on LinkedIn.
