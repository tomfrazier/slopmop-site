## What doomscrolling means

Doomscrolling is spending far more time than you meant to scrolling through news or posts that make you feel worse. [Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/doomscrolling) defines doomscroll as "to spend excessive time scrolling through online content (especially news) that makes one feel sad, anxious, angry, etc."

The usual picture is late at night, phone in hand, reading one more update about a crisis you can do nothing about tonight. The content is often real news. The problem is the amount, and that each upsetting item tends to send you looking for the next one.

Doomscrolling is an everyday word for a habit. It isn't a medical diagnosis, and having done it says nothing on its own about anyone's mental health.

## Where doomscrolling came from

The word joins "doom" to "scrolling", the way people once talked about "surfing" the web. Its origins are unclear. The first documented use is a tweet from 2018, and a separate tweet the same day is often credited as the coinage.

It spread fast in 2020, during the COVID-19 pandemic, when many people spent long stretches reading bad news on their phones. Dictionary.com picked it as its top monthly trend in August 2020, and the Macquarie Dictionary named it its 2020 Committee's Choice Word of the Year. Merriam-Webster kept it on its watch list for three years before adding it in September 2023, and dates its first known use in that sense to 2020.

People have pointed to several things that make it easier. Infinite scrolling removes the natural stopping points that a page or a newspaper had. Feeds that rank posts for engagement often push emotionally charged material up, including [rage bait](/glossary/rage-bait/). An older idea, "mean world syndrome" from the 1970s, described heavy television viewers coming to believe the world was more dangerous than it is.

## Examples

:::example
It's 12:40am. You opened your phone to check tomorrow's weather. Forty minutes later you have read six posts about a flood in another country, three furious replies to a politician and a thread speculating about what might happen next. You still haven't checked the weather, and you feel worse than when you started.
:::

Some habits people report helping: a fixed time for the news, notifications off for news apps, a chronological feed where the platform offers one, and the phone outside the bedroom.
