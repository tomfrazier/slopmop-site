## What it means

A humblebrag is a boast wrapped in something that sounds modest: a complaint, a confession, a show of gratitude. "I can't believe they asked me to keynote again" is about being asked to keynote. The disguise lets the writer share the good news while appearing not to.

It works through a few familiar moves: complaining about a privilege, asking a question that isn't really a question, or presenting the boast as a neutral fact. The tell is that the real subject of the sentence is the achievement, and the modesty is there to excuse mentioning it.

## Where it came from

The comedian and writer Harris Wittels coined it. In 2010 he used it as the name of his Twitter account, @Humblebrag, where he retweeted examples he spotted, and the idea later became a column on Grantland. In 2012 he published a book, *Humblebrag: The Art of False Modesty*. The American Dialect Society named it the most useful word of 2011.

Researchers have since looked at how humblebrags land. A 2017 paper in the *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* by Ovul Sezer and colleagues found humblebragging less effective than plain bragging: because it comes across as insincere, people judged humblebraggers as less likeable and less competent. Wittels himself said humblebrags "can only serve to make people jealous of the posters and/or hate them".

## Examples

Two invented posts about the same news:

:::slop
Honestly so humbled and a little overwhelmed 🙏 Never in a million years did I think I'd be named one of the top 40 under 40. Still processing. Grateful doesn't even begin to cover it.
:::
:::fine
I'm on this year's 40 under 40 list, mostly for the clinic scheduling tool we shipped in May. The credit is shared with Dev and Ana, who built the booking engine.
:::

The first is all reaction and no information; the reader learns how the writer feels about the award but not what it was for. The second just says it, then gives the reader something to know.

## How Slop Mop sees it

Slop Mop has no question for humblebrags as such. They often travel with tells it does score, though: a [scroll-stopper](/patterns/scroll-stopper/) opening, [empty praise](/patterns/empty-praise/) and the [too-tidy story](/patterns/too-tidy-story/) that ends with a lesson. On LinkedIn a post that stacks several of those can be flagged; a plain announcement with real detail usually isn't.
