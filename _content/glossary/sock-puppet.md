## What it means

A sock puppet is a false online identity that pretends to be an independent person while someone else pulls the strings. The image is the hand puppet made from a sock: it seems to talk, and everyone can see whose hand is inside, except online they can't.

People use sock puppets to:

- praise or defend themselves, their product or their book;
- make one view look like the majority, by having several "different" accounts agree;
- vote more than once in polls or flood review sites;
- get round a ban by coming back under a new name.

The deception is what makes it a sock puppet. A second account that is openly yours, for a hobby or for work, is usually called an "alt", and a pseudonym isn't a sock puppet just for hiding a real name. The line is crossed when the extra account pretends to be someone else.

## Where it came from

The practice is older than the internet. Walt Whitman, Anthony Burgess and Benjamin Franklin all wrote pseudonymous reviews or praise of their own work. The Oxford English Dictionary defines sock puppet without reference to the internet, as "a person whose actions are controlled by another; a minion", with a citation from 2000.

Online, the term has long been used in forums and on Wikipedia, which has fought sock puppets for years. In October 2013 the Wikimedia Foundation condemned paid-advocacy sock puppetry and banned the firm Wiki-PR from editing. In 2015 it uncovered another group of accounts doing undisclosed paid editing.

Several variants have their own names. A "strawman sock puppet" pretends to support a view and argues for it badly, so the view looks foolish. A "meatpuppet" is a real second person recruited to act like a sock puppet. In network theory, one actor creating many identities to game a reputation system is a Sybil attack. Sock puppets are also a building block of [astroturfing](/glossary/astroturfing/) and [troll farms](/glossary/troll-farm/).

## Examples

:::example
A self-published thriller has 40 five-star reviews in its first week. Nine of the reviewers joined that week, have reviewed nothing else, and describe the twist with the same unusual phrase. One defends the book in a reply to a critical review, using the author's own pet word.
:::

:::example
A local planning debate on a forum has four new accounts that all oppose the proposal, all post within the same hour and all sign off with the same misspelling.
:::

Neither pattern proves anything alone. Investigators use clues such as shared IP addresses and similar writing style, and an accusation of sock puppetry is serious enough to need that evidence.
