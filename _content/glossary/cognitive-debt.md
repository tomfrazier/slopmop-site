## What it means

Cognitive debt is the idea that handing your thinking to an AI assistant works like borrowing. You get the essay, the summary or the plan now, without the effort. The cost arrives later, as weaker engagement with the material, worse recall of what you produced and less sense that it's yours.

The metaphor echoes "technical debt" in software, where a quick fix today makes tomorrow's changes harder. Applied to people, the claim is that skipped effort doesn't vanish. You pay it back the next time you have to think about the subject unaided.

The term describes a risk from a particular kind of use. Drafting with AI and then working hard on the draft is a different activity from accepting the first answer.

## Where it came from

The phrase was popularised by a study from the MIT Media Lab, posted to arXiv on 10 June 2025 as "Your Brain on ChatGPT: Accumulation of Cognitive Debt when Using an AI Assistant for Essay Writing Task". Its authors, with Nataliya Kosmyna as first author and Pattie Maes among the team, split participants into three groups to write essays: one using a large language model, one using a search engine, and one using no tools.

What they reported:

- 54 people took part in the first three sessions; 18 completed a fourth.
- Brain activity was measured with EEG. The no-tools group showed the strongest and most widespread connectivity, the search group came next, and the LLM group showed the weakest.
- LLM users reported the lowest sense of ownership of their essays and struggled to quote accurately from what they had just written.
- In the fourth session the groups swapped. People who moved from the LLM to no tools showed signs of under-engagement.
- Over four months, the authors write, LLM users "consistently underperformed at neural, linguistic, and behavioral levels".

## Examples

:::example
Two students write the same 600-word essay on urban planning. One prompts a chatbot, tidies the formatting and submits it in 20 minutes. The other spends 2 hours reading and drafting. A week later, both are asked to explain their essay's main argument without notes. The question cognitive debt raises is which of them can.
:::

That is an illustration of the idea, not a result from the study.

## The criticism

The main reasons for caution are in the paper's own description. The sample is small: 54 people, and only 18 in the session where the groups swapped. The task was essay writing under lab conditions, so the findings may not carry over to other kinds of AI use. EEG connectivity is a measure of brain activity, and lower activity isn't the same thing as lasting harm. The paper first appeared as a preprint, and the authors present their results as raising concerns that call for "deeper inquiry".

## How Slop Mop sees it

Cognitive debt is about what AI use does to the writer, which is outside what Slop Mop judges. It scores the post in front of you, and it doesn't penalise AI involvement, so a post drafted with a chatbot and then properly worked on is judged like any other.
