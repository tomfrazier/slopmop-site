## What it means

Broetry is a way of writing LinkedIn posts: one short sentence per line, a blank line after each, building to a business or self-help lesson. A single post is a broem. The name blends "bro" and "poetry", and the joke is that the layout looks like verse while the content is a pep talk.

The usual broem has three parts. It opens with a line that promises a story ("I was fired on a Tuesday."). It tells a short anecdote in which the writer comes off well, often after a setback. It closes on a moral that would have fitted in one sentence, sometimes followed by "Agree?" and a pointing emoji.

The line breaks do two jobs. They make a thin idea look weightier than it is, because every sentence gets a line of its own. They also push the text below LinkedIn's "see more" cut-off, so a reader has to click to finish the post, and that click tells the feed the post is interesting. Fenwick's long write-up on the style calls the breaks "typographic mic drops for insights that aren't particularly insightful".

Plenty of good posts use short sentences and white space. A broem adds the drip-fed hook, the tidy anecdote and the lesson nobody needed spelling out.

## Where it came from

The style spread on LinkedIn in late 2017. BuzzFeed News reporters looked into it that year, tracing much of it to one self-described growth strategist whose posts were drawing unusual engagement and copycats, and suggested LinkedIn might soon crack down. According to Fenwick, LinkedIn did change its feed ranking afterwards to give more weight to posts from people's close connections and less to viral writers. The format survived anyway.

Who coined the word is disputed. Fenwick credits Bloomberg writer Lorcan Roche Kelly and says outlets including BuzzFeed and Reuters popularised it. The Business of Business, writing in November 2020, credits BuzzFeed. Both agree on the year.

By 2020 the format had moved well beyond its origins: that Business of Business piece rounded up broems from well-known authors, investors and executives with millions of followers. Fenwick's updated article notes that large language models have since made broetry easier to produce than ever, which fits what readers see now: the same shape, generated in seconds.

Fenwick's author also observed that most of the examples he collected were written by men, though not all of them. The label is about the format, and anyone can write one.

## Examples

Both of these are invented. The first is a broem; the second tells the same story as a post.

:::slop
Last year I lost our biggest client.

$400K. Gone.

In one phone call.

I didn't sleep for a week.

Then I did something nobody expected.

I called them back.

And said thank you.

Today they're our biggest client again.

Gratitude is a strategy.

Agree? 👇
:::
:::fine
In April we lost a client worth about a third of our revenue. They said our reporting was late and hard to read, which was fair. We rebuilt the monthly report around the three numbers their CFO asked about, and sent it on the 3rd of every month instead of the 15th. They came back in October on a smaller contract. We're still a quarter down on last year.
:::

The broem hides every useful detail behind the drumroll: what went wrong, what changed, what it cost. The rewrite gives the reason, the fix and the remaining damage, and it ends when the story does.

:::slop
My intern asked me a question today.

"Why do you still reply to every cold email?"

I smiled.

Because 10 years ago

someone replied to mine.

That reply changed my life.

Be the reply.
:::
:::fine
I reply to most cold emails with one line, even when the answer is no. It takes me about 20 minutes a day. Two of our last five hires came from people I'd told "not now" a year earlier.
:::

The first is a [too-tidy story](/patterns/too-tidy-story/): an anecdote that exists to deliver its moral, complete with a line break in the middle of a sentence for effect. The second states the habit, its cost and the evidence it works, and leaves the reader to decide.

### How to spot a broem

- Almost every sentence sits on its own line, including fragments.
- The first line withholds something ("Then everything changed.").
- The story has a turn, a reveal and a moral, in that order.
- The writer is the hero, or the humble student of someone wiser.
- It ends with a one-line lesson, a question, or both.

## How Slop Mop sees it

Broetry is one of the most common shapes in the LinkedIn feed, and Slop Mop has no rule against line breaks. What it scores are the habits broetry tends to carry: the [scroll-stopper](/patterns/scroll-stopper/) opening, the too-tidy story and the closing [engagement bait](/patterns/engagement-bait/). A short-lined post with a real number and a real cost in it can pass; a broem with nothing behind the drumroll usually shows several tells at once, which is what gets it folded.
