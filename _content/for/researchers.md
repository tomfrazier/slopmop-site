## What your feed looks like

If you work in a lab, a university department, a think tank or an industry research team, LinkedIn is where a lot of your field now announces things. New preprints, accepted papers, open datasets, postdoc vacancies and conference calls all turn up there, often before they reach a mailing list.

So does a great deal of commentary about research by people who haven't read it. A single paper becomes "BREAKING: scientists prove remote work kills productivity". A chart with no axis labels circulates with "this changes everything". Summaries of AI papers appear within hours of release, written as numbered lists of claims the paper doesn't make. Then there are the motivational posts about the PhD, the "10 tools every researcher needs" lists, and the announcements of awards that describe the honour and never the work.

What you want is posts by people close to the work: an author explaining what the result does and doesn't show, a methods note, a dataset release with a licence and a link, a group looking for collaborators with a specific skill.

## Examples

:::slop
🚨 New study just dropped and it's HUGE.

Researchers found sleep is the ultimate productivity hack. This will revolutionise how we work.

Save this post and share with someone who needs to hear it.
:::
:::fine
Our preprint on shift work and sleep is out. 1,140 hospital nurses, wrist actigraphy for 14 days each. Night-shift staff slept 41 minutes less on average, but only in their first two years. We can't separate adaptation from people leaving, which is the obvious next study.
:::

:::slop
Honoured and humbled to receive this prestigious award. Grateful to my incredible team for this truly meaningful recognition. The best is yet to come!
:::
:::fine
We're releasing the annotated survey data from the 2025 rural broadband project: 3,600 households, CC BY 4.0, with the codebook. Two questions were badly worded and we've flagged them in the readme.
:::

The slop is a scroll-stopper with hype words and a request to save and share, and an award post made entirely of empty praise. The fine examples give the sample, the method, the limit and how to get the data.

## How Slop Mop helps

Slop Mop is a browser extension for Chrome and other Chromium browsers. It reads each post in your LinkedIn feed before you reach it and asks twelve questions: nine about tells of slop and three counter-signals, including whether the post is useful to readers. A post with specific numbers, named examples or a method a reader could follow earns a large share off its slop score, though never all of it.

It judges the writing and doesn't try to detect AI. A clear summary that an author wrote with help from a language model will usually pass; a hand-typed hype post won't. It also can't tell you whether a study is any good. It reads the text of the post and never sees the paper behind it.

It works on the main feed of desktop LinkedIn today. X, Reddit, Facebook, Substack, Medium, websites and email are coming soon, so it won't help with the research discussion that happens on other networks yet. The free version covers one feed with 250 checks a day.

## Setting it up for researchers

Start with **Hide** mode at **Mild** sensitivity.

Hide suits researchers because most of what you'd skip is easy to recognise and you lose little by folding it. Folded posts become a one-line strip that opens again with one click, so a mis-fold costs you a second.

Mild is the cautious choice for a specific reason. Academic writing has habits that overlap with one of the tells, stiff phrasing: hedges like "may suggest" and "further work is needed", and stock transitions. Good researchers hedge on purpose. On Mild, a post needs a higher slop score before it folds, so a careful, hedged results post is less likely to be caught. If your feed still feels noisy after a week, move to **Moderate**.

When you open the panel on a folded post, look at the Slopprint. If stiff phrasing is the only spike, the model has probably mistaken caution for filler; vote **No** and the post comes back in your feed. A single standing-out tell is already discounted, but your vote settles it. Votes that disagree with the verdict go to a person at Artimovo for review.

## If you post too

Researchers promoting their own work tend to slide into two registers, both of which trip the tells:

- **Press-release voice**: "groundbreaking", "first-ever", "transforms our understanding". State the finding and the size of the effect. See [hype words](/patterns/hype-words/).
- **Paper voice on social media**: "Furthermore, these results may potentially indicate…" reads as padding in a feed. Keep one honest hedge and drop the rest. See [stiff phrasing](/patterns/stiff-phrasing/).
- **The dramatic opener** ("We were wrong about everything") over a modest result. See [scroll-stopper](/patterns/scroll-stopper/).

A good research post names the sample, the main number, the biggest limitation and where to read it. That usually fits in four sentences.
