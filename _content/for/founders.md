## What your feed looks like

Founders tend to follow other founders, and founder LinkedIn has its own genre. Much of it is written to build a personal brand, attract investors or sell a course on building a startup, and it borrows the same shapes over and over.

You'll recognise:

- The "we went from 0 to £1M ARR in 9 months, here are 11 lessons" thread, where the lessons could apply to any company.
- Fundraising announcements written as hero stories, with the round size up top and nothing about what the money is for.
- "Unpopular opinion" posts that turn out to be popular opinions.
- Tool lists promising that some AI product will replace a team, with no mention of what it gets wrong.

The posts that help you are rarer: an operator explaining exactly how they priced their first enterprise deal, a founder describing why they shut a product line, a post-mortem with the numbers left in. Those posts are often longer and duller to look at, and they lose out in a feed tuned for reactions.

## Examples

:::slop
We hit $1M ARR in 9 months. No VC. No team. No marketing budget.

Here's exactly how we did it. 🧵👇

1. Obsess over your customer.
2. Ship fast.
3. Never give up.
:::
:::fine
Our first enterprise contract was £18k a year. We asked for £30k, they countered at £15k, and we settled at £18k with a two-year term. In hindsight the term mattered more than the price: it got us through the next raise.
:::

:::slop
AI agents will replace your entire sales team. Zero hiring, zero training, infinite scale. The founders who don't adopt this now will be left behind.
:::
:::fine
We trialled an AI agent for first-touch sales emails for six weeks. Reply rates were similar to our SDR's, but two prospects complained the emails got their product wrong. We kept it for follow-ups only.
:::

The first slop example is a classic [scroll-stopper](/patterns/scroll-stopper/): a big number, one-line paragraphs, and a promise of a thread. The second is a [no-catch promise](/patterns/no-catch-promises/), with a dose of the pressure described in [agent FOMO](/glossary/agent-fomo/). The fine versions give a number, a trade-off and a decision.

## How Slop Mop helps

Slop Mop is a browser extension for Chrome and other Chromium browsers, live on LinkedIn today. X, Reddit, Facebook, Substack and Medium are coming soon, along with email, so if most of your founder reading happens on X, it can't help with that yet.

As you scroll, it scores each post against nine tells of slop and three counter-signals. Two counter-signals matter a lot here. "Useful to readers" rewards specific numbers, named examples and steps someone could follow. "Reader response" counts comments and reposts for more than one-tap reactions, and discounts posts with hundreds of reactions and almost no comments, which is what an engagement pod looks like.

It judges the writing, not who wrote it. A founder who drafted a detailed post-mortem with AI help keeps their post. It never deletes, reports, mutes or blocks anything.

## Setting it up for founders

Start with **Hide** mode on **Aggressive** sensitivity.

That's a stronger setup than most people need, and it suits founders for two reasons. Your time is the scarcest thing you have, and founder content is unusually heavy on hooks, so Moderate can leave a lot of it in place. Aggressive folds more posts into a one-line strip, which you can reopen with one click if the author's name catches your eye.

The trade-off is more false positives. Some genuinely useful founder posts open with a dramatic line because that's what the genre has taught people to do. Handle that two ways:

- **Click the mop on folded posts from people you rate.** The panel shows the score and the strongest signs, so you can see whether it was folded for one bad habit or for several.
- **Vote No on the misses.** Your vote overrides the model in your feed. When it disagrees with the verdict, a person at Artimovo reviews it.

If after a week you're reopening more than you're skipping, step down to Moderate.

## If you post too

Founders are told to post, and most do. The posts that get read by the people you want (customers, hires, investors) usually look like the fine examples above.

- Keep the numbers and remove the drumroll. "We hit £1M ARR" can be the first sentence of a normal paragraph. See [scroll-stopper](/patterns/scroll-stopper/).
- Say what it cost. Every growth story has a price: a hire that didn't work, a customer you lost, a year without salary. The [no-catch promises](/patterns/no-catch-promises/) page explains why leaving it out reads as a sales pitch.
- Avoid lessons framed as corrections ("Fundraising isn't about the deck..."). The [not X, but Y](/patterns/not-x-but-y/) page covers this.
- Be careful with revenue claims built on pilots and trials. Counting pilots and one-off deals as recurring revenue has drawn enough attention that one investor quoted by [Fortune](https://fortune.com/2025/09/28/how-is-arr-calculated-startups-venture/) called it [vibe revenue](/glossary/vibe-revenue/).
