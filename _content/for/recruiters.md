## What your feed looks like

A recruiter's LinkedIn feed is a work tool. You use it to see who just left a company, who is quietly open to a move, which clients are hiring and which have gone silent. The trouble is that recruiting itself produces a lot of the noise.

The usual crowd:

- Other recruiters posting "hot jobs" with a row of fire emojis and a request to comment "interested".
- Talent leaders posting the same three opinions about ghosting, counter-offers and culture fit, each with a dramatic opening line.
- Candidate-advice accounts promising a "secret" CV format if you comment a keyword.
- Hiring stories where a candidate did one surprising thing in an interview and got the job on the spot.

Underneath all that are the posts you actually came for: an engineer explaining why they left, a hiring manager describing what the team is building, a company posting a plain update about a new office. Those are often short and unpolished, and they get buried by posts built to win the algorithm.

## Examples

:::slop
🔥 HIRING 🔥 Senior Backend Engineer, remote, amazing culture, rockstar team! Comment "ME" below and I'll reach out! ♻️ Repost to help someone in your network land their dream role!
:::
:::fine
We're hiring a backend engineer for the payments team in Leeds, hybrid two days a week. The stack is Go and Postgres. Salary band is £70k to £85k. The hiring manager is Priya, and she'll do the first call herself.
:::

:::slop
A candidate once showed up 10 minutes late to an interview. Everyone wrote her off. Then she said something I'll never forget. That day I learned that resilience beats punctuality every time.
:::
:::fine
Three of our last five offers were declined because the final round was scheduled more than a week after the first. We've cut the process to two stages and now book the final round on the same call.
:::

The first pair shows [engagement bait](/patterns/engagement-bait/) dressed as a job ad. The second shows a [too-tidy story](/patterns/too-tidy-story/), the anecdote that exists to deliver a moral. The fine versions give a reader something to act on: a salary band, a named manager, a change to a process.

## How Slop Mop helps

Slop Mop is a browser extension for Chrome and other Chromium browsers. On LinkedIn, which is live today, it reads each post before you reach it and scores the writing against nine tells of slop and three counter-signals. X, Reddit, Facebook and email are coming soon, so for now it only works on your LinkedIn feed.

It judges the writing, not the person or whether AI was involved. That matters in your job. A candidate who used AI to tidy up an "I'm looking" post still gets a fair read if the post says what they do, where and for whom. Slop Mop doesn't detect AI and doesn't touch anyone's profile. It never deletes, reports, mutes or blocks anything.

## Setting it up for recruiters

Start with **Highlight** mode on **Moderate** sensitivity.

Highlight hides nothing. It tints each post's mop yellow for Possibly slop and red for Likely slop. For a recruiter that's the safer start, because some of the people you most want to find write badly. A nervous job-seeker might open with a hook because they've been told hooks work. You still want to see that post. A red mop just tells you to read past the opening line.

Moderate is a sensible middle. Mild flags only the most obvious bait, which leaves most "hot jobs" posts unmarked. Aggressive flags more, which is fine in Highlight mode since nothing disappears, but the colour starts to lose meaning if half your feed is yellow.

After a week, if the red posts are almost all recruiter spam and influencer advice, switch to **Hide**. Folded posts sit in a one-line strip you can reopen with one click, so you can still check anything that looks like it might be a candidate.

Use your vote. Click the mop on a post and vote No if Slop Mop got a candidate's post wrong. Your vote overrides the score in your own feed, and when it disagrees with the verdict a person at Artimovo reviews it.

The free plan covers one feed with 250 checks a day, resetting at midnight UTC. If you scroll LinkedIn for hours, you may hit that limit in the afternoon.

## If you post too

Your job ads and market updates are read by candidates who see dozens a day. The ones that get replies tend to say the salary, the location, the stack and who the hiring manager is.

- Drop the comment-a-keyword request. A plain "apply here" link or "message me" is enough. See [engagement bait](/patterns/engagement-bait/).
- Skip "rockstar", "fast-paced" and "exciting opportunity". They read as [hype words](/patterns/hype-words/) and say nothing about the role.
- If you share a hiring story, keep the detail and lose the moral. A real number from your own process is more useful than a candidate who said something unforgettable.
- Avoid opening with a single dramatic line and a pause. The [scroll-stopper](/patterns/scroll-stopper/) page shows what that looks like.

If you worry that candidates will assume your outreach came from a chatbot (the habit described in [AI suspicion](/glossary/ai-suspicion/)), specific detail is the quickest way to show a person wrote it.
