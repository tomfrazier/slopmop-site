## What your feed looks like

Marketers have an awkward relationship with slop, because many of its formats came out of marketing. The hook-and-line-break post, the carousel of "7 lessons", the comment-to-get-the-guide giveaway, the X thread that starts "Most people don't know this 🧵", the blog post written for a keyword: these are lead-generation tactics, and you've probably been asked to run some of them.

So every feed you read for work is full of your own trade's output:

- **LinkedIn**: "Marketing is dead" and "SEO is dead" posts, roughly monthly, each followed by a pitch for what replaces it. Agency case studies with percentage uplifts and no baseline. AI-generated carousels that restate a well-known framework with new colours.
- **X**: growth threads that promise a playbook and deliver a list of tools.
- **Reddit**: marketing subreddits where a "genuine question" turns out to be a product plug, and brands posing as customers in buying-advice threads.
- **Facebook**: brand pages and groups running "comment YES if you agree" posts for reach.
- **Substack and Medium**: newsletters that rewrite last week's trend piece.
- **Websites and email**: competitor blogs padded for search, and newsletters whose brand voice sounds like every other brand voice: "We're not just a company. We're a movement."

What's useful is the reverse: a marketer showing a campaign that didn't work and why, an analyst explaining an attribution problem with real numbers, a founder describing which channel actually brought their first 100 customers. Those posts teach you something about your job, and they're the ones the formula posts crowd out.

## Examples

:::slop
We're not just a software company. We're a movement. It's not about features, it's about feelings. Join the revolution. 💜
:::
:::fine
We changed our homepage headline from a product description to a customer quote in July. Demo requests went up for about three weeks and then settled back to where they were. We kept the quote, but it wasn't the win it looked like in week one.
:::

:::slop
SEO is DEAD. 💀 AI search changed everything overnight. If you're still writing blog posts, you're already behind. Here's the new playbook nobody's talking about 👇
:::
:::fine
Organic traffic to our help centre fell by about a fifth this year, but sign-ups from organic stayed flat. Our guess is that people who just wanted a quick answer stopped clicking through, and the ones who needed the product still did.
:::

The first slop example is [not X, but Y](/patterns/not-x-but-y/) framing stacked into a brand slogan. The second is a [scroll-stopper](/patterns/scroll-stopper/) that leans on [hype words](/patterns/hype-words/). The fine versions report a result, admit what they don't know, and give a reader something to test.

## How Slop Mop helps marketers

Slop Mop is a browser extension for Chrome, Edge, Firefox, Safari and Chromium browsers such as Brave, Opera and Arc, on desktop. It reads posts on LinkedIn, X, Reddit (posts and comments), Facebook, Substack (Notes and posts) and Medium before you reach them, reads the articles and blogs you open, and with Slop Mop for Gmail and Outlook it checks what the sender wrote in your newsletters. Each item is scored against nine tells of slop and three counter-signals. [How it works](/how-it-works.html) explains the scoring.

For marketers, the most useful part is probably the panel. Click the mop on any post and you see the score, the strongest signs and the Slopprint, a chart of all nine tells. The shape shows what kind of slop a post is. A slogan-heavy brand post might spike on hype words and "not X, but Y", while a committee-written thought-leadership post might spike on stiff phrasing and empty praise. Seeing the pattern on other people's posts makes it easier to catch in your own drafts.

The same reasoning sits behind search. Google's [spam policies](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies) target pages made mainly to rank and not to help readers, and the padded competitor blog is often exactly that. Slop Mop marks it as you read, which is quicker than reading it to the end to find out.

It judges the writing, not whether AI was involved. A clear, specific post drafted with AI keeps its score. It never deletes, reports, mutes or blocks anything. On email, personal details are replaced with placeholders before anything is checked, and attachments and quoted earlier messages are never sent.

## Setting up Slop Mop for marketers

Start with **Highlight** mode on **Moderate** sensitivity.

Marketers have a reason to see slop that most readers don't: it's market research. You want to know what competitors are posting and which formats are spreading, even when the posts are empty. Highlight hides nothing. Each post's mop turns yellow for Possibly slop or red for Likely slop, so you can skim the feed and still see what's out there.

Moderate gives a useful spread of colour without marking everything. If you find almost every agency post coming up red, that's information too.

Once you've seen enough examples:

- **Switch to Hide** for your everyday scroll, and back to Highlight when you're doing competitor research. Hide folds posts into a one-line strip you can reopen.
- **Vote when you disagree.** Your vote overrides the model in your feed, and when it disagrees with the verdict, a person at Artimovo reviews that post. Voting isn't available for email.

The free plan covers the one feed you pick, with 250 checks a day. Pick the network your audience lives on, since that's where you most need to see what's spreading. Slop Mop XL, at US$5.99 a month or US$59.99 a year, covers every feed, the sites you read, Gmail and Outlook, with a higher daily limit, on up to five browsers. A marketer watching competitors on four networks will want XL.

## If you post too

You almost certainly do, for yourself or a brand. Your own posts get a mop in your feed like everyone else's. You can't vote on them, but the panel still shows the Slopprint, so you can see which tells a published post picked up.

- Cut the slogans built on contrast. Say what the product does and for whom. See [not X, but Y](/patterns/not-x-but-y/).
- Replace the vocabulary list ("game-changing", "seamless", "next-level") with the specific result. The [hype words](/patterns/hype-words/) page has the full set.
- Give a baseline with every uplift. A percentage without a starting number is an [empty promise](/patterns/no-catch-promises/) dressed as data.
- Lose the comment-for-the-guide ask, even if it lifts reach. Readers recognise [engagement bait](/patterns/engagement-bait/) and some fold it away.
- Treat newsletters and blog posts the same way. A subscriber who sees the tells in your email learns to skim the next one.

If your audience seems tired of AI-flavoured marketing, that mood has a name: [AI fatigue](/glossary/ai-fatigue/). [Add Slop Mop to your browser](/) and it marks the formula posts in your feeds, and shows you which tells your own posts picked up.
