## What a teacher's feed looks like

Teachers, lecturers, trainers and school leaders read in several places for practical reasons. LinkedIn is for jobs, professional development and keeping up with people they trained alongside. Facebook groups are where subject teachers swap resources and ask each other for help. X still has a busy education crowd, Reddit has its teaching subreddits, and Medium and Substack carry the longer pieces and newsletters. The feeds they get back are shaped by who else posts about education, and much of that is people selling to schools.

So you see the edtech pitch dressed as a reflection ("I asked my students what they really wanted…"), the AI tool that will "personalise learning for every child" with no word on cost, setup or data, and the stream of inspirational posts about the teacher who changed someone's life, shared from page to page on Facebook. There are also infographics of "21 prompts every teacher needs", most of which you could write in five minutes, and conference recaps full of words like "empowering" and "future-ready".

What you're actually looking for is smaller and plainer: a head of maths sharing the homework policy they dropped and why, a lecturer describing how they changed an assessment after students started using chatbots, a colleague in a Facebook group posting a free resource with a link, or a job you'd want.

## Examples

:::slop
A shy student once handed me a note on the last day of term.

I read it in my car and cried.

That's when I realised: we don't teach subjects. We teach hearts. ❤️

Tag a teacher who needs to hear this.
:::
:::fine
Year 9 stopped getting weekly homework in our department this term. We swapped it for a 10-minute retrieval quiz at the start of Monday lessons. Quiz scores are up on last year's cohort; parents of two students have complained, and we're still working out what to do about revision.
:::

:::slop
Our AI tutor delivers personalised learning for every learner, saves teachers hours every week, and closes attainment gaps at scale. The future of education is here.
:::
:::fine
We piloted an AI feedback tool on Year 12 essays for six weeks. It was fast and decent on structure, poor on argument, and it cost us a full INSET afternoon to set up. We're keeping it for first drafts only.
:::

The first slop post is a [too-tidy story](/patterns/too-tidy-story/) with a "not X, but Y" moral and a tagging request. The second is a [no-catch promise](/patterns/no-catch-promises/): every benefit, no cost. The fine examples name a year group, a time period and a downside, which is what lets another teacher decide whether to try it.

## How Slop Mop helps teachers

Slop Mop is a browser extension for Chrome, Edge, Firefox, Safari and Chromium browsers such as Brave and Opera, on desktop. It reads each post before you reach it in your LinkedIn feed, Facebook feed and groups, X timeline, Reddit, Substack and Medium, and in the articles you open on other sites. It checks the writing for nine tells of slop and three counter-signals, and folds or marks the posts that score as likely slop. Click the mop beside a post to see why; [How it works](/how-it-works.html) explains the scoring.

Because it judges the writing and not whether AI was involved, it won't punish a colleague who used a chatbot to tidy up a genuinely useful post about their marking policy. It isn't an AI detector, and it isn't meant for checking student work.

It runs on desktop browsers only, so it won't run on a phone or tablet. It never deletes, reports, mutes or blocks anything; to tell LinkedIn itself that you don't want a post, use its own controls to [hide content in your feed](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/13325).

## Setting up Slop Mop for teachers

Pick your feed. The free plan covers one feed you choose, with 250 checks a day: LinkedIn if you're job-hunting, Facebook if your subject groups are where the useful posts are. Slop Mop XL covers every feed, the sites you read, and Gmail and Outlook, with a higher daily limit, on up to five browsers, for US$5.99 a month or US$59.99 a year. For email it checks only what the sender wrote, with personal details replaced by placeholders first. Check your school's or university's rules before adding anything to a work mailbox.

Start with **Hide** mode at **Moderate** sensitivity.

Most teachers check their feeds in short gaps, and Hide gives the shortest scroll. Likely slop folds into a one-line strip that opens again with one click, so nothing is lost. Moderate catches the obvious inspiration and sales posts while leaving most practice posts alone.

If you work somewhere that requires you to follow edtech companies or your trust's central accounts, consider **Highlight** instead. Nothing is hidden, and announcement posts that you're expected to read stay visible with a yellow or red mop.

Use the vote freely. If a colleague's plain post about timetabling gets folded, vote **No** and it stays open in your feed. If an inspiration post slips past, vote **Probably**. Votes that disagree with the verdict are reviewed by a person at Artimovo. Voting isn't available for email.

There's a side use too. The panel shows a Slopprint, a chart of all nine tells for the post. If you teach writing, media literacy or computing, it can be a quick way to show a class what the tells look like in a real feed. Use posts from organisations or public accounts, not private individuals.

## If you post too

Education posts often slip into the tells because teaching attracts strong feeling:

- **The student-who-changed-me story.** It may be true, but if it ends in a moral it reads as constructed. Give the details and leave out the lesson. See [too-tidy story](/patterns/too-tidy-story/).
- **Lyrical language about learning** ("a garden where curiosity blooms"). Say what you did in the lesson. See [flowery prose](/patterns/flowery-prose/).
- **Tool recommendations with no downside.** Fellow teachers trust a post that says what went wrong. See [no-catch promises](/patterns/no-catch-promises/).

Keep students anonymous, and ask before sharing their work. [Add Slop Mop to your browser](/) and it folds posts like these for you.
