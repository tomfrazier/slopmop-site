## What your feed looks like

Teachers, lecturers, trainers and school leaders end up on LinkedIn for practical reasons: jobs, professional development, and keeping up with people they trained alongside. The feed they get back is shaped by who else posts about education, and much of that is people selling to schools.

So you see the edtech pitch dressed as a reflection ("I asked my students what they really wanted…"), the AI tool that will "personalise learning for every child" with no word on cost, setup or data, and the stream of inspirational posts about the teacher who changed someone's life. There are also infographics of "21 prompts every teacher needs", most of which you could write in five minutes, and conference recaps full of words like "empowering" and "future-ready".

What you're actually looking for is smaller and plainer: a head of maths sharing the homework policy they dropped and why, a lecturer describing how they changed an assessment after students started using chatbots, a colleague posting a free resource with a link, or a job you'd want.

## Examples

:::slop
A shy student once handed me a note on the last day of term.

I read it in my car and cried.

That's when I realised: we don't teach subjects. We teach hearts. ❤️

Tag a teacher who needs to hear this.
:::
:::fine
Year 9 stopped getting weekly homework in our department this term. We swapped it for a 10-minute retrieval quiz at the start of Monday lessons. Quiz scores are up on last year's cohort; parents of two students have complained, and we're still working out what to do about revision.
:::

:::slop
Our AI tutor delivers personalised learning for every learner, saves teachers hours every week, and closes attainment gaps at scale. The future of education is here.
:::
:::fine
We piloted an AI feedback tool on Year 12 essays for six weeks. It was fast and decent on structure, poor on argument, and it cost us a full INSET afternoon to set up. We're keeping it for first drafts only.
:::

The first slop post is a too-tidy story with a "not X, but Y" moral and a tagging request. The second is a no-catch promise: every benefit, no cost. The fine examples name a year group, a time period and a downside, which is what lets another teacher decide whether to try it.

## How Slop Mop helps

Slop Mop is a browser extension for Chrome and other Chromium browsers. It reads each post in your LinkedIn feed before you reach it, checks the writing for nine tells of slop and three counter-signals, and folds or marks the posts that score as likely slop. Click the mop beside a post to see why.

Because it judges the writing and not whether AI was involved, it won't punish a colleague who used a chatbot to tidy up a genuinely useful post about their marking policy. It isn't an AI detector, and it isn't meant for checking student work.

It works on the main feed of desktop LinkedIn now. X, Reddit, Facebook, Substack, Medium, websites and email are coming soon, so it won't yet help with education newsletters or school email. It's free for one feed with 250 checks a day. It needs a desktop Chrome or Chromium browser, so it won't run on a phone or tablet.

## Setting it up for educators

Start with **Hide** mode at **Moderate** sensitivity.

Most teachers check LinkedIn in short gaps, and Hide gives the shortest scroll. Likely slop folds into a one-line strip that opens again with one click, so nothing is lost. Moderate catches the obvious inspiration and sales posts while leaving most practice posts alone.

If you work somewhere that requires you to follow edtech companies or your trust's central accounts, consider **Highlight** instead. Nothing is hidden, and announcement posts that you're expected to read stay visible with a yellow or red mop.

Use the vote freely. If a colleague's plain post about timetabling gets folded, vote **No** and it stays open in your feed. If an inspiration post slips past, vote **Probably**. Votes that disagree with the verdict are reviewed by a person at Artimovo.

There's a side use too. The panel shows a Slopprint, a chart of all nine tells for the post. If you teach writing, media literacy or computing, it can be a quick way to show a class what the tells look like in a real feed. Use posts from organisations or public accounts, not private individuals.

## If you post too

Education posts often slip into the tells because teaching attracts strong feeling:

- **The student-who-changed-me story.** It may be true, but if it ends in a moral it reads as constructed. Give the details and leave out the lesson. See [too-tidy story](/patterns/too-tidy-story/).
- **Lyrical language about learning** ("a garden where curiosity blooms"). Say what you did in the lesson. See [flowery prose](/patterns/flowery-prose/).
- **Tool recommendations with no downside.** Fellow teachers trust a post that says what went wrong. See [no-catch promises](/patterns/no-catch-promises/).

Keep students anonymous, and ask before sharing their work.
