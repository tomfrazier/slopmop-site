## What your feed looks like

When you're looking for work, LinkedIn changes. The feed starts showing you more career advice, more "I'm hiring" posts and more people selling help with the search. Some of it is useful. A lot of it is written to make you anxious enough to comment, follow or buy.

The common types:

- CV and interview "secrets" offered in exchange for commenting a keyword.
- Courses and coaching that promise interviews within weeks, with no mention of the cost or who it didn't work for.
- Stories of someone who sent 300 applications, got nothing, then changed one thing and got five offers.
- Motivational posts about rejection that end with a call to like if you agree.

What you need is narrower and less exciting: actual job posts with a salary and a location, hiring managers saying what their team is building, people in your field describing how their company's interview process works, and the occasional honest post about a search that went badly.

## Examples

:::slop
I applied to 312 jobs and heard nothing. Then I changed ONE line on my CV. 5 interviews in 2 weeks. 🤯 Comment "CV" and I'll send you the exact template. 👇
:::
:::fine
Our interview loop for product designers is a 30-minute chat with the recruiter, a portfolio review with two designers, and a final with the head of design. No take-home. We aim to give feedback within five working days.
:::

:::slop
Land your dream job in 30 days. No experience? No problem. No network? No problem. My method works for everyone, in any industry, at any level.
:::
:::fine
The career sessions I run are £60 for an hour. They work best for people moving between similar roles. If you're changing field entirely, a course or a contact in that field will probably help you more.
:::

The first slop example stacks a [scroll-stopper](/patterns/scroll-stopper/) on top of engagement bait. The second is a [no-catch promise](/patterns/no-catch-promises/): all benefit, no cost, no limit. The fine versions tell you something you can check or act on, including the limits.

## How Slop Mop helps

Slop Mop is a browser extension for Chrome and other Chromium browsers. It works on LinkedIn today. X, Reddit, Facebook, Substack, Medium and email are coming soon, so if your search also runs through Gmail or other networks, Slop Mop can't help there yet.

It reads each post before you reach it and scores the writing against nine tells of slop and three counter-signals. One of the counter-signals is "useful to readers": specific facts, named examples, a method someone could follow. A job post with a salary band scores well on that, however plain it is.

It judges the writing, not who wrote it, and it isn't an AI detector. A recruiter who used AI to draft a clear, specific job ad isn't penalised for it. It never deletes, reports, mutes or blocks anything.

## Setting it up for job-seekers

Start with **Highlight** mode on **Mild** sensitivity.

This is the gentlest setup, and it suits a search. Job ads are often written in hype-heavy language ("fast-paced", "rockstar", "exciting opportunity"), but a badly written ad can still be a good job. In Highlight mode nothing is hidden. Each post's mop turns yellow for Possibly slop or red for Likely slop, and you decide what to read. Mild flags only the clearest cases, so you'll mostly see the keyword-bait and the miracle-method posts marked.

Once you know what the colours mean in your feed, two changes are worth trying:

- **Move to Moderate** if the career-advice bait is still getting through unmarked.
- **Switch to Hide** if you find yourself never opening the red posts. Folded posts sit behind a one-line strip, and one click reopens them.

If Slop Mop marks a real job post, click its mop and vote No. Your vote overrides the score in your own feed.

The free plan covers one feed with 250 checks a day. Slop Mop XL, coming soon, will cost US$5.99 a month and adds other feeds, email and a higher daily limit. Since LinkedIn is the only network live today, the free plan is a reasonable place to stay during a search unless you regularly hit the 250-check limit.

## If you post too

Plenty of job-seekers post an "open to work" update or a write-up of a project. Those posts are read by recruiters and hiring managers who scroll past many of them a day.

- Lead with the facts: the role you want, where, and one thing you've done that proves you can do it. Skip the dramatic opening line; the [scroll-stopper](/patterns/scroll-stopper/) page shows the pattern.
- Avoid asking people to "like for reach". A clear request ("If you know a team in Bristol hiring data engineers, I'd like an introduction") works better and isn't [engagement bait](/patterns/engagement-bait/).
- Describe what you did in plain words. "Cut month-end close from 9 days to 6" says more than "drove transformative process improvements". See [hype words](/patterns/hype-words/).
- If you used AI to help draft it, read it back for stock phrases. The [stiff phrasing](/patterns/stiff-phrasing/) page lists the ones that give it away.

A long search on a noisy feed is tiring. If that's where you are, the [AI fatigue](/glossary/ai-fatigue/) entry might be familiar reading.
