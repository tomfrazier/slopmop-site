A too-tidy story is an anecdote shaped backwards from its lesson. A taxi driver, a janitor, a child or a junior employee says something simple; the narrator is changed for ever; the post ends with the moral in bold. Nothing in it can be checked, nothing goes wrong afterwards, and the details are exactly the ones the lesson needs.

## What it looks like

:::slop
A security guard once told me something I'll never forget. "I don't guard the building. I guard the people in it." That day, I realised every role matters.
:::
:::fine
Our office cleaner, Dave, pointed out in June that the fire door on the third floor had been propped open for weeks. Nobody else had noticed. We fitted a door alarm, and facilities now asks the cleaning team for a monthly list of anything that looks wrong.
:::

:::slop
Yesterday a 7-year-old asked me why I work so much. I had no answer. I closed my laptop and went outside. Your kids won't remember your promotions.
:::
:::fine
I've stopped checking email after 6pm on weekdays since September. Two clients complained in the first month; one moved their weekly call earlier to suit. I'm still working most Sunday evenings, which I'd like to fix next.
:::

:::slop
I interviewed a candidate who picked up a piece of litter on the way in. I hired him on the spot. Character beats CVs every time.
:::
:::fine
We added a short paid work trial to our hiring in 2025. It caught two candidates who interviewed well but couldn't do the task, and it put off one strong candidate who didn't have time for it.
:::

How to recognise one:

- **An unnamed, symbolic character:** a stranger, a cleaner, a child, an old man on a bench, a junior hire.
- **A single line of dialogue** that happens to state the moral perfectly.
- **An instant transformation:** "That day, I realised", "I've never been the same".
- **No date, place or consequence** beyond the lesson.
- **A moral that's broader than the story.** One litter-picking candidate becomes a rule about all hiring.
- **Often a borrowed plot.** Many circulate for years with the details swapped.

## Why it wastes your time

A true story is evidence. It shows you what happened in a particular case, with the mess left in, and lets you decide what it means. A too-tidy story gives you the conclusion first and a scene invented or trimmed to fit, so the "evidence" is only the moral again in costume.

It also asks you to trust something you can't check. You can't tell whether the janitor existed, whether the conversation happened or what the narrator did the following week. Readers who notice that tend to stop believing the writer's real stories as well.

And the lessons are usually ones you already knew. Every role matters; spend time with your children; character counts. A story that ends there has spent your attention on a greeting card.

## When it's fine

Stories are one of the best ways to explain anything, and some are meant to be tidy.

- **Fables, parables and children's books.** Everyone knows they're constructed to teach, and nobody presents them as something that happened last Tuesday.
- **Clearly labelled hypotheticals.** "Imagine a customer who orders on Friday and needs it Monday" sets up an example honestly.
- **Case studies with real details.** Names (with permission), dates, numbers and what didn't work make a story useful even if it ends well.
- **Personal stories with loose ends.** A real experience told with its odd details, its mixed outcome and no grand lesson is good writing.
- **Jokes and speeches,** where the audience understands the story has been polished.

The test: could someone else check this happened, or at least picture it differently from you? If the story has a date, a place and a consequence that doesn't flatter the moral, it's probably real enough.

## How Slop Mop scores it

Too-tidy story is one of nine questions the model asks about every post, and one spoke on the Slopprint. It asks whether the anecdote exists to prove the moral, which is a different matter from whether the post contains a story at all. It can't know whether something really happened; it reads how the story is built.

The model rates its own confidence, and unsure answers count for less. When this is the only tell that stands out, the score is cut, because one neat story in an otherwise useful post is an ordinary habit. Too-tidy stories usually sit inside a [scroll-stopper](/patterns/scroll-stopper/) layout, with a [“not X, but Y”](/patterns/not-x-but-y/) moral at the bottom, and several tells together is what gets flagged.

Three counter-signals can only lower the score. Specific personal detail raises "sounds like a person", and a story with numbers and a trade-off scores well on "useful to readers", so a real, messy anecdote gets a share taken off. Some tells count for more than others, and the weights aren't published. Your sensitivity setting places the verdict, and your vote overrides it in your own feed.

## How to fix it in your own writing

1. **Start from what happened.** Write the events in order before you decide what they mean.
2. **Add the checkable details.** When, where, who (with permission), how many.
3. **Keep one thing that doesn't fit.** The part that went badly, or the question you still haven't answered.
4. **Say what changed afterwards.** A process, a habit, a number. If nothing changed, say so.
5. **Shrink the moral to the size of the story.** "This worked for us" in place of "this is the secret to leadership". Or leave the moral out entirely.

:::slop
A customer once called me in tears. Our product had saved her business. I hung up and cried too. That's when I knew: we're in the business of changing lives.
:::
:::fine
In August a bakery owner in Bristol emailed to say our scheduling tool had cut her weekly rota time from 3 hours to 40 minutes. She also said the app crashed every time she added a seventh staff member. We fixed that bug in September, and she's still a customer.
:::

## Where it shows up

LinkedIn is full of them, often under a scroll-stopper hook, and LinkedIn is where Slop Mop reads posts today. They also travel widely as chain posts on Facebook, in motivational talks, in sales emails ("one of our clients was in exactly your position") and in wedding speeches, where they're forgiven.
