Flowery prose dresses a simple claim in imagery until the claim disappears. Growth becomes a tapestry, teamwork a symphony, a hard year a journey through the storm. The images are rarely fresh, and they rarely explain anything: they're there to make the sentence feel important.

## What flowery prose looks like

:::slop
Our company culture is a vibrant tapestry woven from countless threads of passion, purpose and possibility.
:::
:::fine
Most of our staff have been here more than five years, and about a third of our managers started in junior roles.
:::

:::slop
Product management is a delicate dance between the symphony of user needs and the ever-shifting tides of the market.
:::
:::fine
Most of my week goes on two things: talking to customers who are about to cancel, and deciding which of their requests to say no to.
:::

:::slop
In the quiet stillness of dawn, as the first light kissed the horizon, I realised that resilience is a garden we tend each day.
:::
:::fine
I went back to work three weeks after surgery. Starting with half days for the first fortnight was what made it manageable.
:::

Signs to look for:

- **Craft and art metaphors:** tapestry, symphony, mosaic, canvas, masterpiece, woven, orchestrated.
- **Movement metaphors:** dance, journey, voyage, tides, winds of change.
- **Nature as decoration:** gardens, seeds, storms, sunrises that have nothing to do with the topic.
- **Abstract nouns doing the work:** passion, purpose, possibility, resilience, all at once.
- **A sentence you can't picture.** If you try to imagine a "symphony of user needs", nothing specific appears.

## Why it wastes your time

A metaphor is a shortcut: it lets you understand something new by comparing it with something you already know. Flowery prose uses the form without the function. Knowing that a culture is a tapestry tells you nothing about the people, the pay or what a working day is like.

It's also slow. You have to translate the image back into a claim, and often discover there wasn't one. "Growth is a delicate dance between vision and humility" takes a moment to parse and leaves you with nothing to agree or disagree with.

George Orwell made the same complaint in 1946. In ["Politics and the English Language"](https://www.orwellfoundation.com/the-orwell-foundation/orwell/essays-and-other-works/politics-and-the-english-language/) he named "staleness of imagery" as one of the two failings common to the bad prose he quoted, and his first rule was never to use a metaphor or simile you are used to seeing in print. Tapestries and journeys have been in print a great deal since then.

## When it's fine

Figurative language is a large part of what makes writing good. It belongs:

- **In fiction, poetry and song.** Imagery is the point there, and readers come for it.
- **When it explains.** "A database index works like the index at the back of a book" makes a technical idea clear in one line. The comparison is specific and carries information.
- **When it's fresh and exact.** A writer who says a delayed train "sat outside Peterborough like it was thinking about its life choices" is being funny and precise about a real experience.
- **In eulogies, toasts and speeches,** where readers expect a warmer register and the occasion supplies the facts.
- **In idioms everyone uses without noticing,** such as "a steep learning curve" or "the bottom line". These are worn smooth enough to read as plain speech.

The test: if you replace the image with a plain sentence, does the reader lose anything? If they lose a joke, a picture or an explanation, keep it. If they lose only the decoration, cut it.

## How to fix it in your own writing

1. **Underline every image.** Tapestries, journeys, dances, gardens, storms.
2. **Ask what each one is standing in for.** Write that underneath in the plainest words you can.
3. **Check whether the plain version is a claim.** If it is, use it. If it's vague ("we care about lots of things"), find the specific fact.
4. **Keep at most one image,** and only if it explains something or makes the reader smile.
5. **Read the result aloud.** If it sounds like something you'd say to a colleague, it's done.

:::slop
This year has been a transformative journey, a rich tapestry of challenges and triumphs, as our team danced through the storms of change to emerge stronger.
:::
:::fine
This year we lost our biggest client in March, cut two roles in May, and by November had replaced the lost revenue with four smaller clients. The team is smaller and busier, and we're hiring one person in January.
:::

## Where flowery prose shows up

End-of-year reflections and culture posts on LinkedIn lean on it heavily. It turns up in Facebook memorial and milestone posts, Medium and Substack personal essays, and the "About us" pages of the websites you read, especially companies that sell something ordinary. Brand manifestos, wedding-venue websites, wine labels and travel copy use it too.
