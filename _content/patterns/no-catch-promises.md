A no-catch promise describes a result with every cost taken out. More output without more hours, growth without hiring, fitness without effort, savings without trade-offs. Real changes nearly always cost something: money, time, a skill to learn, a feature lost along the way. A post that names none of them is showing you half the ledger.

## What it looks like

:::slop
Double your revenue without spending more on ads, without hiring and without working weekends. Anyone can do it.
:::
:::fine
We grew revenue by about a third last year without raising ad spend. Most of it came from putting prices up 15%, which cost us roughly 1 in 10 customers. It worked because most of our clients had been with us for years.
:::

:::slop
Learn to code in 30 days, with no experience, no maths and no stress. Land a six-figure job by spring.
:::
:::fine
I learnt enough Python to automate our weekly report in about three months of evenings. It saves me two hours a week. I'm nowhere near job-ready as a developer, and the course I used was the third one I tried.
:::

:::slop
Our app helps you sleep better, focus more and feel calmer, effortlessly. Just press play.
:::
:::fine
The app plays a 20-minute wind-down recording. Some of our users report falling asleep faster; others find it does nothing. It isn't a treatment for insomnia, and if poor sleep is affecting your days, a GP is a better first step.
:::

The signs:

- **A string of "without"s.** Without hiring, without burnout, without sacrificing quality.
- **"Effortlessly", "instantly", "anyone can".** Words that remove the work or the conditions.
- **A result with no starting point.** "Double your revenue" from what, over how long?
- **No mention of who it didn't work for.**
- **The price, the time or the catch kept for the DM, the call or the sales page.**

## Why it wastes your time

A promise is only useful if you can compare it with your own situation. To decide whether to try something, you need the cost, the effort and the conditions it needs. A no-catch promise withholds exactly those, so the only way to evaluate it is to click, book a call or buy.

It can also cost more than time. A reader who takes "without hiring" at face value might take on a workload that needed another person. The missing catch still exists, and it lands on whoever believed the post.

Regulators have long expected advertisers to put the conditions next to the claim. The US Federal Trade Commission's guidance on digital advertising disclosures, published in 2013, says a qualifying disclosure works best close to the claim it qualifies. Readers of ordinary posts are owed the same courtesy, and this page isn't legal advice on when the rules apply to you.

## When it's fine

Some things genuinely have no catch worth mentioning, and some contexts make the catch obvious.

- **Things that really are free and simple.** "Your library card gets you free ebooks" is all benefit, and it's true. A borrowing limit is a detail nobody needs in the headline.
- **The catch is common knowledge.** "Booking early gets cheaper flights" doesn't need to explain that you give up flexibility.
- **The cost is stated in the same post.** A headline promise with the price, the time and the limits a line or two below is honest, even if the headline is upbeat.
- **Jokes and obvious exaggeration.** "This sandwich will change your life" under a photo of a sandwich isn't misleading anyone.

The test: could a reader act on the post and later say, "Nobody told me about that"? If yes, the catch belonged in the post.

## How Slop Mop scores it

No-catch promises is one of nine questions the model asks about every post, one spoke on the Slopprint. It looks for benefits presented with no cost, limit or condition anywhere in the post.

The model gives its confidence with each answer, and unsure answers count for less. When no-catch promises is the only tell that stands out, the score is cut. An upbeat launch post with one big claim is common and often fine. The pattern usually comes bundled with [hype words](/patterns/hype-words/), [empty praise](/patterns/empty-praise/) and an [engagement bait](/patterns/engagement-bait/) ending ("comment PLAN for the full method"), and that cluster is what gets flagged.

Counter-signals can only bring a score down. A post that states its own trade-offs, gives numbers and reads like a particular person gets a share taken off. Some tells count for more than others, and the weights aren't published, so nobody can add a token caveat and slip through. Your sensitivity setting decides the verdict; your vote overrides it in your feed.

## How to fix it in your own writing

1. **Write down what it cost you.** Money, hours, weeks, the thing you stopped doing to make room.
2. **Say where you started.** "From 40 customers to 60" lets readers judge whether "50% growth" applies to them.
3. **Name one condition it depended on.** A team that already knew the tool, a market that was growing anyway, a lucky hire.
4. **Say who it won't suit.** One sentence is enough.
5. **Put all of that in the post.** Keep nothing important back for the DM or the call.

:::slop
I cut my email time in half without missing anything important, and you can too. No apps, no rules, no stress. 🚀
:::
:::fine
I cut my email time from about 2 hours a day to 1 by checking twice a day instead of constantly. It took three weeks to stop reaching for my phone, and I did miss one urgent request in the first week, so I now give my team a phone number for anything that can't wait. It would be harder in a client-facing role.
:::

## Where it shows up

Course launches, coaching offers and productivity tips on LinkedIn are frequent sources, and LinkedIn is where Slop Mop reads posts today. The same promises run through sponsored posts on every network, landing pages, webinar invitations and cold sales email.
