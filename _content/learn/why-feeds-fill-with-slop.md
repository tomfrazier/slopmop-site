Slop isn't an accident, and it isn't only an AI problem. Feeds fill with it because of how they're ranked, what it costs to produce a post and what posting earns. Each of those has changed in slop's favour.

## Why feeds fill with slop: ranking by engagement

Most feeds don't show posts in the order they were written. They rank them by how likely you are to react, comment, share or stop scrolling. That's a reasonable goal, but it rewards a post's ability to provoke a response, not its usefulness. A [scroll-stopper hook](/patterns/scroll-stopper/) holds your thumb for a second; a question with "comment YES below" collects replies; a post that annoys people collects even more.

Writers notice what gets reach and copy it. That's how formats spread: the one-line paragraphs of broetry, the "a thread 🧵" opener, the too-tidy story with a moral. Each is a structure that ranks well, applied to whatever topic is to hand.

## Slop is cheap and pays

Two costs have fallen at once.

**Producing a post** has never been cheaper. A chatbot will write a plausible inspirational story or a list of ten tips in seconds. The glossary entry on [AI slop](/glossary/ai-slop/) traces how quickly that output became visible; Shrimp Jesus is its emblem.

**The rewards for reach** have grown. Some platforms pay creators directly. In October 2024 X changed its creator payouts to [depend on engagement](https://techcrunch.com/2024/10/09/x-changes-creator-payouts-to-depend-on-engagement-not-ads/), which gives anyone who can farm replies a reason to post more. Others reward reach indirectly, through followers, leads and job offers.

When producing a post costs nothing and attention can be turned into money, the volume of low-value posts goes up. That's the supply side the [dead internet theory](/glossary/dead-internet-theory/) exaggerates, and the business-model side Cory Doctorow calls [enshittification](/glossary/enshittification/).

## Platforms push back, partly

Platforms know engagement bait harms the experience. Facebook announced in 2017 that it would [demote posts that bait engagement](https://about.fb.com/news/2017/12/news-feed-fyi-fighting-engagement-bait-on-facebook/), such as "comment YES" and "tag a friend" requests. LinkedIn said in 2022 that it would [down-rank engagement-baiting posts and polls](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/linkedin-updates-feed-algorithm-to-downrank-engagement-baiting-posts-and-po/623313/). Those rules catch the most obvious formulas.

They don't catch the rest: the hook with nothing behind it, the [empty praise](/patterns/empty-praise/), the [no-catch promise](/patterns/no-catch-promises/). Those depend on reading what a post says, and a platform that earns money from time on site has mixed incentives to filter it.

## What readers can do about feeds filling with slop

You can't change how a platform ranks, but you can change what reaches you:

- **Follow fewer, better sources,** and use chronological views where they exist. The [guides](/guides/) cover each network's controls.
- **Don't reward the formats.** A reply to [engagement bait](/patterns/engagement-bait/), even an angry one, tells the ranking that it worked. That's the mechanism behind [rage bait](/glossary/rage-bait/).
- **Learn the tells.** Recognising the [nine tells](/patterns/) makes each post quicker to dismiss.
- **Filter on the reader's side.** Slop Mop reads posts ahead of you on LinkedIn, X, Reddit, Facebook, Substack and Medium, and on the articles and email you read, and folds likely slop with its reasons shown. It doesn't change the platform's ranking; it changes what you have to read. [How it works](/how-it-works.html) explains the twelve questions behind each score.
