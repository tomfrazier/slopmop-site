A feed reader is the classic answer to a noisy feed: stop letting a platform decide what you see, and subscribe to the sources you trust instead. It's a good answer for blogs, news sites and newsletters. It can't do much about a LinkedIn home feed, which doesn't come out as RSS, and that's where Slop Mop works today.

## What each one does

**RSS and feed readers**, such as Feedly, collect new articles from sites, blogs and newsletters you subscribe to and show them in one list, usually newest first and outside any platform's algorithm. Some add AI features on top. Feedly, for example, has a Feedly AI menu that includes [Mute Filters](https://docs.feedly.com/article/109-how-can-i-add-create-a-mute-filter), which hide articles containing a keyword you pick; as of October 2026 those need a Pro+ or Enterprise plan. Most readers have a free tier, with paid plans for extras.

**Slop Mop** is a browser extension that reads posts in your LinkedIn feed before you reach them. It rates nine tells of slop, from [hype words](/patterns/hype-words/) to [flowery prose](/patterns/flowery-prose/), and three counter-signals, then gives a score out of 100. Likely slop folds into a one-line strip you can reopen, or, in Highlight mode, stays visible with a coloured mop icon.

## Where they differ

**Leaving versus staying.** A feed reader replaces the platform's feed with one you build. Slop Mop leaves you on the platform and cleans up what it shows. If you've had enough of algorithmic feeds, a reader gets you out. If you need to be on LinkedIn for work, hiring or keeping up with contacts, a reader can't stand in for it.

**What they can see.** A feed reader only sees sources that publish a feed. Your LinkedIn home feed isn't one of them, so a reader has nothing to offer there. Slop Mop works directly in that feed.

**How they filter.** Feed readers filter mostly by source: you chose to subscribe, so everything from that source arrives. Some add keyword filters. Few judge individual articles on how well they're written. Slop Mop judges every post on its writing, including counter-signals such as whether it's useful to readers, and shows why. Click the mop for the score and the strongest signs, and vote if you disagree; your vote overrides the model in your own feed.

**Curation is the reader's strength.** A well-chosen list of sources often has little slop to begin with, because you picked writers who don't do it. That's a quieter result than any filter can give, at the cost of the time it takes to find and add sources, and of missing whatever you didn't subscribe to.

**Coverage.** Feed readers already handle blogs and newsletters well. As of October 2026, Slop Mop is live only on LinkedIn. Substack, Medium, websites, X, Reddit, Facebook and email are coming soon. Until then, a reader is the better tool for the long-form writing you follow.

**Privacy.** A reader fetches public feeds. Slop Mop sends the text of each new post (never your name or the author's) to its scoring service, which stores a one-way hash and the scores, not the text.

## When to use a feed reader

- You'd rather follow a hand-picked set of blogs, sites and newsletters than any social feed.
- You want a chronological list with no ranking algorithm.
- You want your newsletters out of your inbox and in one place.
- You read mostly long-form writing, which Slop Mop doesn't cover yet.

## When to use Slop Mop

- You still scroll LinkedIn, for work or for people, and want the slop folded.
- You want each post judged on its writing, with the reasons shown.
- You don't want to rebuild your reading from scratch.

## Using both

They fit together. Move the blogs and newsletters you value into a reader, where you control the list. Keep LinkedIn for what only LinkedIn has, and let Slop Mop handle the posts there.
