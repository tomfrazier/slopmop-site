Keep a newsletter if, most weeks, it tells you something specific you'd act on or repeat to someone, in less time than it takes to read. To check, open the last three issues side by side and count the concrete things in each: dates, numbers, names, links to original sources, a method you could use. Padded issues have lots of words and few of those.

This is the step after the big clear-out. If you haven't done that yet, [Gmail's Manage subscriptions page](/guides/email/email-gmail-manage-subscriptions/) or [Outlook's Subscriptions page](/guides/email/email-outlook-clean-up-newsletters/) will take most of the junk off your list first.

## Before you start

- **Pick 10 to 15 newsletters.** The ones you've kept on purpose, or still feel guilty about not reading.
- **Use the last three issues of each.** One issue can be an off week. Three show the habit.
- **Give yourself about a minute per newsletter.** Skim each one; don't read it. If an issue can't show you its value in a minute, that's part of the answer.

## What to look for in an issue

### Signs it's worth your time

- **The subject line says what's inside.** "Issue 48: the new VAT threshold, and a cheaper payroll tool" is a table of contents. You can decide without opening it.
- **Facts you can check.** Dates, prices, figures, named people and organisations, with links to where they came from.
- **Something only this writer would know.** First-hand testing, an interview, local knowledge, an opinion with reasons attached.
- **It admits limits.** "We haven't tested this on Windows." "This only applies in England." A writer who tells you what they don't know is usually careful about what they do.
- **It ends when it's done.** No sign-off paragraph that restates everything.

### Signs it's padding

- **A long warm-up.** Three paragraphs on the weather, the writer's week or "what a time to be alive" before the first piece of information.
- **The roundup that says nothing.** Ten links, each with a one-line summary that repeats the headline. You could have got the same from a news site.
- **Big framing, small content.** "We're entering a new era." "Everything is changing." Followed by one news item everyone already covered.
- **The tells from social feeds.** Newsletters drafted fast pick up the same habits as posts: [flowery prose](/patterns/flowery-prose/) about tapestries and journeys, ["not X, but Y"](/patterns/not-x-but-y/) slogans in place of arguments, and [too-tidy stories](/patterns/too-tidy-story/) with a moral in the last line.
- **More sponsor than substance.** Count the paragraphs. If the ads and "upgrade to paid" blocks outnumber the paragraphs of actual content, the free tier is a sales funnel.
- **No sources.** Claims about studies, statistics or "experts say" with no link. Either the writer didn't check, or they don't want you to.

The same news, two ways:

:::slop
What a week it's been, friends! 🌊 The world of work is shifting beneath our feet, and honestly, it's not about working harder anymore, it's about working smarter. This week's big story? Remote work. Let's dive in…
:::
:::fine
The county council published its staff working-pattern survey on Tuesday. Hybrid working is flat at 61%. The bit worth reading is page 9: office attendance rose among under-30s and fell among over-50s. Link and our summary below.
:::

Both are invented. The first could be AI-drafted or typed at speed, and it makes no difference to you. The second gives you the source, the date, a number and where to look, in fewer words.

## The scoring

For each newsletter, look at its last three issues and give it one point for each issue that:

1. Had at least three specific facts you didn't know.
2. Had something you'd act on, forward or mention to someone.
3. Took less time to read than it saved you, or was simply a pleasure to read.

That's a score out of 9. Then:

| Score | Do this |
|---|---|
| 7–9 | **Keep in the inbox.** This one has earned it. |
| 4–6 | **Demote.** Send it to a reading label or folder with a filter or rule, and read it when you have time. |
| 0–3 | **Cut.** Unsubscribe. If you miss it in a month, you can sign up again. |

The third point is there on purpose. A newsletter you read for the writing, the jokes or one person's taste is doing its job even if it's light on facts. The audit is for the issues you skim out of a faint sense of obligation. Leave the ones you enjoy alone.

For the demote pile, [Gmail filters](/guides/email/email-gmail-filters-for-newsletters/) can send them to a label automatically. In Outlook, an inbox rule does the same job.

## Things that look like padding but aren't

- **A long issue.** A 3,000-word investigation with sources is dense. A 600-word issue built from filler is padded. Count what you learned per minute of reading.
- **Personal writing.** A writer telling you what happened to them, with the details, is first-hand experience. The warning sign is an anecdote with no date, place or names that exists only to land a lesson.
- **Plain repetition.** A weekly newsletter on a slow beat will sometimes say "nothing changed this week". That's useful, and honest.
- **AI help.** A writer who uses AI to tidy up their notes can still send you something worth reading. Judge the issue by what's in it.

## Run it again in three months

Newsletters change. A good one gets sold, gets a sponsor, or runs out of steam; a new one you signed up for at a conference turns out to be excellent. Put a reminder in your calendar for three months from now and redo the audit on whatever has crept back in.
