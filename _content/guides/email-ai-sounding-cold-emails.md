You can't tell for certain whether AI wrote a cold email, and you don't need to. What you can see is whether the sender knows anything about you: a real reason for writing, one specific detail that took effort to find, and an offer with a price or a limit attached. Mass-produced pitches fail all three, whoever or whatever typed them.

## Why "was this AI?" is the wrong question

Plenty of good sales emails are drafted with AI and then checked by someone who did the research. Plenty of bad ones were typed by hand from a template in 2015. Guessing at authorship leads to [AI suspicion](/glossary/ai-suspicion/), which feels useful and proves nothing. The question that saves you time is narrower: did anyone think about *you* before pressing send?

## What to look for in an AI-written cold email

### 1. Personalisation that could fit anyone

The opening line names your company, your job title or a post you wrote, then says something that would be equally true of any company, title or post. That's a mail-merge field with a compliment wrapped round it.

:::slop
Hi Sam, I came across Northgate Supplies and was really impressed by your commitment to innovation and customer success.
:::
:::fine
Hi Sam, your returns page says orders over 20 kg can't be returned by post. We run a collection service for heavy returns in the North West.
:::

The second sender had to read a page on Sam's website. The first one only needed the company name.

### 2. Praise with nothing behind it

"Impressed by your work", "love what you're building", "your incredible journey". This is [empty praise](/patterns/empty-praise/): a verdict with no evidence. If the sender liked something, they'd say what it was.

### 3. A problem they've assumed you have

"Many companies like yours struggle with…" followed by a problem chosen because the sender sells the fix. No evidence you have it. A good cold email either points at something visible (a slow page, a missing feature, a job advert) or admits it's guessing.

### 4. An offer with no catch

"Double your pipeline without adding headcount." "Save 40% with zero disruption." Every benefit, no price, no setup time, no list of who it won't suit. These are [no-catch promises](/patterns/no-catch-promises/), and the missing cost is usually large.

### 5. Stock phrasing in the middle

"I hope this email finds you well." "I wanted to reach out." "Additionally, our solution may help to drive meaningful outcomes." The [stiff phrasing](/patterns/stiff-phrasing/) is filler between the merge fields. Count the sentences you could delete without losing information. In a mass pitch it's most of them.

### 6. The soft ask

"Would you be open to a quick 15-minute chat?" "Worth a conversation?" The ask is vague because the offer is vague. A sender with something specific tends to ask something specific: "Can I send you a quote for your Leeds warehouse?"

### 7. The follow-up chain

"Just bumping this to the top of your inbox." "Did you get a chance to look at my last email?" "Should I close your file?" Three or four automated nudges, each shorter and more guilt-flavoured than the last, are a sign of a sequence tool. A person following up usually adds something new.

## A 30-second test

Run any cold email through four questions. If it fails three, archive it.

1. **Could this email be sent, unchanged, to 500 other people?** Swap your name and company for a competitor's. If it still reads fine, it was written for a list.
2. **Is there one fact only someone who looked would know?** A product name, a page on your site, a recent change you made.
3. **Is there a cost, a limit or a price?** Even "from £400 a month" or "only works if you use Shopify" counts.
4. **Is the ask small and specific?** A yes or no question you could answer in one line.

Here's one that passes, with all four in a few lines:

:::fine
Subject: Your Leeds warehouse job advert
Hi Sam, I saw you're hiring two pickers for the Leeds site. We supply temporary pickers from £14.20 an hour, minimum two-week booking, and can usually start people within five working days. Want me to send rates for November?
:::

You might still say no. But you know what's on offer, what it costs and what they want from you, and you can answer in one line.

## When to be careful

Some cold emails are worse than generic. If a message asks you to log in, pay an invoice you don't recognise, open an attachment or move fast before something bad happens, treat it as possible phishing. [Google's phishing guidance](https://support.google.com/mail/answer/8253) is to avoid clicking links, downloading files or entering personal information in messages from untrustworthy or unknown senders, and to check that the sender's name and email address match. Report it, don't reply to it.

Generic isn't the same as malicious. A dull pitch from a real company is a waste of time; a convincing pitch with a dodgy link is a risk. Treat them differently.

## What to do with the ones that fail

- **Archive, don't reply.** A "no thanks" tells the sender your address is read, and can restart the follow-up chain.
- **Unsubscribe if there's a link and the sender is a real company.** Most legitimate senders include one. If the email looks suspicious, block instead of clicking.

## If you send cold email yourself

The same test works in reverse. Before you send, delete every sentence that would survive being sent to a stranger, add the one fact you found, state the price or the limit, and ask a question someone can answer in one line. If your sales team drafts with AI, that edit is the part that can't be skipped.
