Look past the article to the site behind it. A content farm publishes far more than any real team could, on topics with nothing in common, under authors you can't find anywhere else, with an About page that says nothing about who runs it. If the homepage shows dozens of new posts a day spanning crypto, gardening and celebrity news, you have your answer.

## What a content farm is

A content farm is a site that produces large numbers of pages mainly to attract search traffic and show ads, with little care for whether the pages are accurate or useful. The idea is older than chatbots, but generative AI made it far cheaper.

NewsGuard, which rates news sites, keeps a running count of what it calls "AI Content Farm" sites. As of its [update on 23 June 2026](https://www.newsguardtech.com/special-reports/ai-tracking-center/), it had identified 3,749 of them in 16 languages. It describes them as sites that "tend to churn out dozens of articles a day", often funded by programmatic advertising that places ads "without regard to the nature or quality of the website".

Search engines have rules about this. Google's [spam policies](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies) define "scaled content abuse" as many pages "generated for the primary purpose of manipulating search rankings and not helping users", whether made by AI, by people or both, and Google [announced](https://blog.google/products/search/google-search-update-march-2024/) a crackdown on it in March 2024. Kagi's [SlopStop](https://help.kagi.com/kagi/features/slopstop.html) downranks domains it finds are mostly AI-generated, "typically more than 80% across its pages". Plenty still reach the results, so it helps to recognise them yourself.

## Before you start

This check is about whole sites. To judge a single article, use [How to spot an AI slop article](/guides/websites/web-spot-ai-slop-articles/). A good site can publish a bad article, and a farm can occasionally publish a decent one. What marks a farm is the pattern across the site.

## The five-minute site check

### 1. Open the homepage, or the latest posts

Count what was published in the last day or two. A small specialist blog might post weekly. A real newsroom posts a lot, but you'll see named reporters, sections and stories with original reporting. A farm posts dozens of near-identical articles a day with no sign of reporting.

### 2. Look at the spread of topics

Real publications have a focus, or clear sections with editors. Farms chase whatever is searched. One homepage covering mortgage rates, dog breeds, a film release and "how to clean grout" with the same tone and template is a strong signal.

### 3. Check the authors

Click a byline. On a genuine site, it leads to an author page with a short bio and other work on related subjects, and often links to that person elsewhere. On a farm you'll find "Admin", "Staff", a first name only, or an author who has written 400 articles this month on every subject imaginable. Google's [guidance for publishers](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content) asks whether pages carry a byline "where one might be expected" and whether it's "self-evident" who wrote them. A missing or meaningless byline fails both.

Profile photos can be stock images or generated faces. If an author seems too good to be true, search the name with the site's topic and see whether they exist anywhere else. Invented people presented as real writers are what the term [counterfeit people](/glossary/counterfeit-people/) is about.

### 4. Read the About and Contact pages

A real publisher says who owns it, where it is and how to reach a person. Farms tend to offer a paragraph of generic mission text and a contact form.

:::slop
Our mission is to provide our readers with high-quality, informative content across a wide range of topics. We are passionate about delivering value and helping you stay informed.
:::
:::fine
We're three former bike mechanics in Bristol. We test every product we write about for at least a month. Corrections go to the email below and are logged on our corrections page.
:::

The first could belong to any site on the internet. The second names people, a place, a method and a way to hold them to account.

### 5. Look at the page itself

Farms are built for ad impressions. Signs include ads between every paragraph, autoplay video, pop-ups before you've read a line, and articles split across several pages. Reading the page in [reader mode](/guides/websites/web-reader-mode/) shows how little text is left once the furniture is removed.

## What the writing looks like

Farm articles share a house style whatever the topic: a long warm-up, headings that restate the question, and [stiff phrasing](/patterns/stiff-phrasing/) holding it together. They rarely have dates for events, quotes from named people, or anything the writer could only know by being there.

:::slop
In conclusion, choosing the right lawn care routine is an important decision that requires careful consideration of various factors. It is essential to consider your specific needs to achieve the best results.
:::
:::fine
Mow less often in a dry spell and raise the blades to the highest setting. Longer grass shades its own roots and browns more slowly.
:::

The fine example is short and general, but it gives you something to do this weekend. The farm version never does.

## Content farm signs at a glance

| Signal | Farm | Real publisher |
|---|---|---|
| Output | Dozens a day, every topic | Matches the size of the team |
| Topics | Anything being searched | A clear focus or proper sections |
| Authors | "Admin", no bio, or one name on everything | Named people with related work |
| About page | Generic mission text | Owners, location, contact, corrections |
| Ads | Between every paragraph | Present, but the article comes first |
| Sources | Few or none, or other farms | Links to originals |

No single row settles it. Three or four together usually do.

## Why it matters beyond one bad click

Farms are part of why people talk about the [dead internet theory](/glossary/dead-internet-theory/), the idea that much of what you meet online is automated. You don't need to accept the theory to see the practical cost: farms copy each other, so the same mistake can appear on 20 sites and look like agreement. When several pages say the same thing in similar words, trace the claim to its origin with the steps in [How to check a blog post's sources](/guides/websites/web-check-blog-post-sources/).

## What to do once you've spotted one

Stop clicking it and keep it out of future searches. DuckDuckGo and Kagi let you block a domain, and Kagi lets you report it to SlopStop; on Google, use `-site:` or an extension. [How to block a site from your search results](/guides/websites/web-block-sites-search-results/) has the steps.
