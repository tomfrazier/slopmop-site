A reply guy is an account, usually a person, that replies to large accounts constantly with comments that add nothing: agreement, a joke that repeats the post, or a correction nobody needed. A reply bot does the same job automatically, often with generic praise or a tidy restatement of the original post. You spot both the same way: the reply would fit under almost any post, and the account's replies tab is full of the same thing.

## Why the replies are crowded

Replies under a big post get seen by that post's audience, which makes them free reach. On X there can be money in it as well: since October 2024, creator payouts have depended on engagement from Premium subscribers ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2024/10/09/x-changes-creator-payouts-to-depend-on-engagement-not-ads/)), so an account that replies early and often under popular posts can draw attention back to its own. Not every reply guy is chasing money; some just like being in the conversation. The effect on readers is the same.

## The human reply guy

Reply guys are people, and the term describes a habit. It has a few recognisable forms:

- **The echo.** Restates the post with more emphasis. "This. So much this."
- **The unrequested expert.** Explains the post's own subject back to its author.
- **The first-responder.** Replies within seconds to every post from the same large accounts, whatever the topic.
- **The self-promoter.** A loosely related reply that ends with a pointer to their own thread, product or newsletter.

:::slop
This is so true. People don't realise how important this is. 💯
:::
:::fine
Same thing happened when we switched payroll providers in April: the first run double-counted holiday pay for part-timers. Worth checking before your first live run.
:::

The first reply could sit under a post about tax, football or bread. The second could only sit under one post, and it gives the next reader something to check.

## The reply bot

Automated replies have become easier to produce, and they tend to read differently from human ones. They're often polite, complete and empty. Look for:

- **[Empty praise](/patterns/empty-praise/).** "What a powerful insight! Thanks for sharing this valuable perspective."
- **The summary reply.** A neat paraphrase of the post, sometimes with a question bolted on: "Great point about remote work. How do you see this evolving?"
- **[Stiff phrasing](/patterns/stiff-phrasing/).** "Indeed, this highlights the importance of…" in a reply section where everyone else writes casually.
- **Mismatched tone.** Cheerful congratulations under a post about a bereavement or a layoff. A bot reads the words; it often misses the situation.
- **Emoji confetti.** 🚀🔥🙌 with a one-line compliment, repeated across dozens of posts.

:::slop
Absolutely fascinating perspective! This really highlights the importance of innovation in today's world. What are your thoughts on the future of this? 🚀
:::

That reply mentions nothing specific to any post. It would work under a rocket launch, a recipe or a resignation letter, which is how you know.

## Check the account as well as the reply

One empty reply doesn't make anyone a bot. Before you write an account off, open its profile and look at the **Replies** tab.

- **Volume.** Dozens of replies an hour, around the clock, suggests automation or a team.
- **Sameness.** If the replies all follow one template, the account is running a script, human or otherwise.
- **Targets.** Replies only under the largest accounts, never under small ones, suggests the goal is visibility.
- **Original posts.** A real reply guy usually has posts of their own with some personality. Accounts built for replies often have few or none.

Be careful about calling people bots in public. Plenty of real people write stiffly, use a lot of emoji or are new to a language. The [counterfeit people](/glossary/counterfeit-people/) and [dead internet theory](/glossary/dead-internet-theory/) pages cover why the line between bot and person has become hard to draw.

## How to read a reply section without the filler

1. **Skip the first screen.** The earliest replies under a big post are where farming accounts aim, because they're seen first. Useful replies often sit further down, after the first rush.
2. **Look for replies with detail.** A number, a place, a link to a source or a first-hand account. Read those and skip the rest.
3. **Check whether the author replied.** Replies the original poster answered are more likely to be part of a real conversation.
4. **Look for corrections with sources.** A correction with a link is worth more than ten agreements.

5. **Sort by usefulness yourself.** If one reply answers the question you had, stop there. A reply section doesn't need reading to the end.

## What to do about it

- **Don't reply to the reply guy.** Arguing under a reply gives it the attention it was after.
- **Mute or block the account** if it keeps showing up. X offers both from the account's profile and from the post menu; the wording varies between the app and the web.
- **Mute the phrases bots overuse.** X's muted words can hide replies that contain a word or phrase ([X Help Center](https://help.x.com/en/using-x/advanced-x-mute-options)). "Thanks for sharing" and "valuable insight" are reasonable entries; [how to use muted words on X](/guides/x/x-muted-words/) has the steps.
- **Keep your own replies specific.** If you reply, add something the post didn't have.

## If that doesn't work

On very large accounts the reply section may be a lost cause; read the post and close it. For the posts that attract this kind of reply in the first place, see [engagement farming on X](/guides/x/x-engagement-farming/).
