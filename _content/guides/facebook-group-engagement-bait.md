Engagement bait in a group is a post that asks members to comment for the sake of commenting: a question nobody needs answered, a "drop a photo of your dog" thread, or a giveaway that only counts comments. It's harder to spot than on a Page because groups are built on questions, and most questions are real. The test is the same one that works everywhere: would the answers change anything for the person asking?

All the examples here are made up. We don't reproduce posts from real groups, private or public.

## Why groups attract it

Groups reward activity. Facebook says the number of comments, likes and reactions a post gets can make it more likely to appear higher in Feed ([Facebook Help](https://www.facebook.com/help/166738576721085)), so a busy thread is worth something to whoever started it. Meta's own definition of engagement bait covers posts that explicitly ask for comments, votes, tags, shares or reactions for any purpose other than a specific call to action, such as finding a missing person or raising money ([Meta Transparency Centre](https://transparency.meta.com/en-gb/features/approach-to-ranking/content-distribution-guidelines/engagement-bait/)). Inside a group, the people posting it are often:

- **Members building a profile,** so a later promotional post or friend request looks familiar.
- **Admins trying to make the group look busy,** to attract members or sponsors.
- **Ordinary members who copy what gets attention,** which is most of it, and not malicious.

## What it looks like in a group

### The question with no stakes

:::slop
Quick poll! Tea or coffee? ☕ Let's get to 500 comments!
:::
:::fine
Has anyone used the new recycling collection since they changed it to fortnightly? Our green bin wasn't emptied on Tuesday and I can't tell if it's the wrong week.
:::

The first sets a comment target, which is the giveaway. The second has a problem behind it, and the replies would solve it.

### "Drop a photo" and "introduce yourself" threads

A new member welcome thread run by the admins is part of how a group works. The same post repeated weekly by a member, with "the more comments the better!", is bait. Look at who's asking and how often.

### Advice that ends in a pitch

"What's the one thing you wish you'd known before starting a business?" collects hundreds of thoughtful replies, then the poster comments under each one with a link to their course. Check the poster's earlier posts in the group if you can see them. A string of open questions followed by an offer is a pattern, and the offer usually leaves out the cost (a [no-catch promise](/patterns/no-catch-promises/)).

### Giveaways that count comments

:::slop
🎁 GIVEAWAY 🎁 Comment "ME" and tag 3 friends to win a £200 voucher! Winner picked Friday!
:::
:::fine
We've got six spare tickets for the junior football club's Christmas party. Message me if your child plays for the under-9s and hasn't got one yet.
:::

Real giveaways by a group's own members happen, but comment-and-tag mechanics are what bait looks like. If a "winner" is then asked to pay a fee to claim the prize, it's a scam: Facebook's scam guidance lists advance fees for prizes as a warning sign ([Facebook Help](https://www.facebook.com/help/1674717642789671)).

### Comments that are all the same

A post where dozens of replies say "Interested", "Following" or a single emoji, with no one asking anything, is either bait or coordinated. Meta said in April 2025 it would show detected coordinated fake engagement in comments less ([Meta](https://about.fb.com/news/2025/04/cracking-down-spammy-content-facebook/)), but within a group you'll still see plenty.

## Bait, spam or just low quality?

These need different responses, and admins handle them differently.

| What you're seeing | Example | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Low-quality but harmless | A tea-or-coffee poll | Scroll past |
| Engagement bait | "Comment YES and tag 3 friends" | Scroll past; tell admins if it's constant |
| Breaks the group's rules | Promotion in a no-selling group | Report to group admins |
| Scam or fraud | A prize that needs a fee | Report to Facebook |

Admins can set up to 10 rules for their group ([Facebook Help](https://www.facebook.com/help/462230500886400)), and the right response depends on them. A buy-and-sell group expects selling. A support group probably doesn't.

## When to report to the admins

You can report a post or comment to the group's admins from its three-dot menu (**Report post to group admins**, or **Report comment to group admins** for a comment). In the app, tap a comment to see the option. You pick a reason and the admins get a notification ([Facebook Help](https://www.facebook.com/help/1765429060199160)).

Know three things first:

1. **The admins will know it was you.** Facebook says so plainly ([Facebook Help](https://www.facebook.com/help/1765429060199160)). In a small local group, that can matter.
2. **It doesn't go to Facebook.** Reporting to admins sends nothing to Facebook, and the admins may or may not act. For scams or anything that breaks Facebook's Community Standards, use the separate report to Facebook in the same menu.
3. **Admins can't fix taste.** Report rule-breaking. A boring poll isn't a breach.

## Making a busy group readable

- **Sort the posts.** At the top of the group's posts, choose **Recent activity**, **New posts** or **Most relevant** ([Facebook Help](https://www.facebook.com/help/792969828344532)). **New posts** is useful in groups where bait threads keep getting pushed up by their comment counts. It resets to the admins' default each visit, and Facebook notes the feature may not be available to you yet.
- **Read the Groups filter in Feeds** to catch the groups you care about in date order. The [Feeds and Favorites guide](/guides/facebook/facebook-feeds-tab-favourites/) has the steps.
- **Unfollow a group you want to keep but not see.** The [snooze and unfollow guide](/guides/facebook/facebook-snooze-unfollow-not-interested/) covers it. Next week's guide deals with group notifications.
