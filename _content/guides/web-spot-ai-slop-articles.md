Look for specifics. A low-value article, whether a model or a person wrote it, makes broad claims that would fit any page on the topic, names no sources you can check and has nothing the writer saw or did themselves. If three minutes of skimming turns up no numbers, names, dates or steps you could follow, close the tab.

## Why "was it written by AI?" is the wrong question

You can't reliably tell, and it matters less than you'd think. Wikipedia's volunteer [guide to signs of AI writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing) warns editors not to rely solely on AI detection tools, which have "non-trivial error rates", and notes that people are poor at telling model text from human text too. Google says much the same from the other side: its [2023 guidance on AI-generated content](https://developers.google.com/search/blog/2023/02/google-search-and-ai-content) rewards "high-quality content, however it is produced", and its [spam policies](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies) target pages made in bulk to rank "no matter how it's created".

So the useful question is whether the page gives you something. A careful writer can use a model and produce a good guide. A tired copywriter can produce [AI slop](/glossary/ai-slop/) by hand. Judging the writing also keeps you clear of [AI suspicion](/glossary/ai-suspicion/), the habit of dismissing anything polished as machine-made.

## The three-minute check

### 1. Read the first two paragraphs

A useful article answers the question in the headline early, or at least tells you what it will cover. A padded one warms up: the topic "has never been more important", "in this article we will explore", a definition of a word you already searched for.

:::slop
In today's digital age, choosing the right standing desk has become more important than ever. Whether you're a remote worker or a busy professional, the desk you choose can have a significant impact on your productivity and well-being.
:::
:::fine
Get a desk that goes at least 10cm below your seated elbow height. Most cheap frames stop at 72cm, which is too tall for anyone under about 170cm.
:::

The first could open any desk article on the web. The second gives you a test you can use in a shop.

### 2. Jump to the middle and count concrete things

Scroll to a section halfway down and read one paragraph properly. Count the items you could check or act on: a number with a unit, a product or version name, a date, a named source, a step. Two or more per paragraph is good. Paragraph after paragraph with none means the page is mostly framing.

Wikipedia's editors describe why model text drifts this way: it tends toward the most statistically likely wording, so specific, unusual facts get smoothed into generic praise. Their example is a person described as "the inventor of the first train-coupling device" turning into "a revolutionary titan of industry". The second phrase sounds bigger and tells you less.

### 3. Check two links

Pick two claims that have links and open them. Do the linked pages exist, and do they say what the article says they say? Pages built to rank often link to a homepage, a vague "study" with no author, or another page on the same site. Claims like "experts agree" and "research shows" with no link at all are the weakest version. Our week 4 guide covers this step in more depth.

### 4. Read the last section

Generic articles end with a summary of what you just read, or with an outline of "challenges and future prospects" that says nothing new. Wikipedia lists those outline-style endings as a common tell. A useful article ends with a decision, a next step or a limitation.

## What to look for, at a glance

| Sign | What it looks like | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Throat-clearing intro | "In today's fast-paced world…" | The answer is being delayed to fill space |
| [Hype words](/patterns/hype-words/) | "game-changing", "cutting-edge", "unlock" | Size is claimed instead of shown |
| [Stiff phrasing](/patterns/stiff-phrasing/) | "Furthermore", "It's worth noting", "Ultimately" | Stock links standing in for an argument |
| ["Not X, but Y"](/patterns/not-x-but-y/) | "It's not just a desk, it's an investment" | A slogan where a fact should be |
| Rule of three, everywhere | Every list has exactly three adjectives | Rhythm instead of content |
| Vague attribution | "Many experts believe" | Nobody you can check |
| No first-hand detail | No photos, measurements or "when I tried it" | The writer may never have touched the subject |
| Summary ending | Repeats the headings | Nothing left to say |

One sign on its own proves little. Plenty of good writers say "furthermore". Several together, and no specifics to make up for them, is the pattern to act on.

## Things that tell you less than you'd think

- **Length.** Long articles can be thorough or padded. Count specifics per section, not words.
- **Polish.** Clean grammar says nothing about whether the content is useful.
- **Em dashes.** Wikipedia's editors list heavy em dash use as one sign among many, but people used them long before chatbots did. Treat it as a weak hint at most.
- **A byline.** Names are cheap. A byline means more when it links to an author page with other work on the same subject.
- **The date.** "Updated October 2026" can mean someone changed one sentence. Look at whether the content mentions anything recent.
- **AI detector scores.** See above. They make mistakes in both directions.

## A quick example

:::slop
When it comes to cleaning a cast iron pan, there are several key factors to consider. Proper care is essential for maintaining its longevity. Furthermore, using the right techniques can make all the difference. Ultimately, a well-maintained pan is a valuable kitchen companion.
:::
:::fine
Rinse it in hot water while it's still warm and scrub with a stiff brush. A drop of washing-up liquid is fine; modern soap won't strip polymerised seasoning. Dry it on the hob for two minutes, then wipe on a few drops of oil.
:::

The first paragraph has four sentences and zero instructions. The second has five steps and one myth corrected.

## What to do with a bad one

Close it and go back to the results.
