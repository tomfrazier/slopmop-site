Follow a few narrow topics to discover new writers, and follow individual writers once they've proved themselves; then read the Following feed when you want only the people you chose. Topics bring in volume and variety, including the filler. Writers you've vetted bring in less, but almost all of it is worth opening.

## How each follow reaches your feed

Medium uses all three kinds of follow, in different ways.

- **Writers and publications.** Medium's [help page on the homepage](https://help.medium.com/hc/en-us/articles/115012586467-Your-homepage) says stories from writers and publications you follow appear on your homepage and in your email digests. Every story that follows Medium's rules is shown to the writer's own followers, which Medium calls Network distribution in its [distribution rules](https://help.medium.com/hc/en-us/articles/360006362473). So if you follow a writer, you'll see their work whether or not anyone else rates it.
- **Topics.** A topic follow tells Medium what subjects you care about. Stories placed in General distribution go to readers who follow related topics, writers or publications. That's how you meet writers you've never heard of, and it's also how a single topic can deliver a dozen near-identical stories in a morning.
- **Boost.** Stories that Medium's human curators pick for Boost get higher priority in matching. You can't follow Boost directly, but it affects which topic stories reach you first.

In short, a writer follow is a guarantee and a topic follow is a request.

## Which to use when

| You want | Follow | Why |
|---|---|---|
| To find new voices on a subject | 2–4 narrow topics | Topics bring in writers you'd never search for |
| A feed you trust | Individual writers | You've already judged their work |
| A curated mix with an editor | A publication with clear standards | Someone else has done the first filter |
| Less noise from one subject | Unfollow or mute the topic | Topic follows are the main source of volume |

Broad topics are where most filler arrives. "Productivity", "Self Improvement" and "Artificial Intelligence" attract huge numbers of stories, and many are written to match what people search for. Narrower topics tend to have fewer, more specialised writers.

## 1. Look at what you follow now

1. On desktop, click your profile picture in the top right corner.
2. Open Settings, then Refine recommendations.
3. Choose the Following tab. It lists the writers, publications and topics you follow.

In the app, the same page is under your profile and Settings, though the wording may differ slightly. Medium changes its interface from time to time, so labels may not match exactly.

Go down the list and unfollow anything you followed during sign-up and never chose, any topic that mostly gives you stories you skip, and any writer you haven't opened in months.

## 2. Follow fewer, narrower topics

1. Open a story in your field and scroll to the topic buttons, which Medium shows on the story page.
2. Click a topic to open its page.
3. Look at what the topic page shows you. If the first screen is mostly headlines you'd skip, don't follow it.
4. Follow it only if the stories are close to what you want.

Medium's homepage help page says you can also add topics from the Following area of the homepage sidebar with the plus button. If you followed a broad topic and regret it, unfollow it. If you still get stories from it through other follows, mute it: Medium's [writer newsletter](https://medium.com/medium-handbook/writer-newsletter-better-repost-stats-and-topic-muting-5fa6a3c400d4) says you can mute a topic from its page.

## 3. Promote writers who pass the check

When a story is good enough to finish and you'd recommend it, follow the writer from the story page or their profile. Use a simple rule so your list doesn't grow to hundreds again: follow after two good stories, not one. A single good piece can be luck or a lucky prompt; two in a row is a habit.

A useful writer usually shows the same signs each time: specific numbers, first-hand detail, sources you can check and an ending with a caveat or next step. The [two-minute check](/guides/medium/medium-spot-low-quality-articles/) covers these.

## 4. Use the Following feed

Medium's homepage has more than one feed. For you mixes in recommendations. The Following feed, which the help page says you open from the homepage sidebar, lists stories from writers and publications you follow in reverse chronological order. When the recommended feed is noisy, switch to Following and read only what you chose.

Treat the two feeds differently: Following for reading, For you for scouting. Spend a few minutes a week in For you looking for new writers, then go back to the feed you trust.

## 5. Follow publications with care

A publication follow is only as good as its editors. Some publications check facts, edit drafts and turn down most submissions. Others accept almost anything that fits their tag. Before following, open the publication's homepage and read the submission guidelines if they're published; then read three recent stories. If they read like they were written by one person in a hurry, the editing isn't doing much.

## Signs your follows need another pass

- Your feed has many stories on the same subject with near-identical headlines.
- You skip most of the stories from one topic.
- You haven't opened anything from a writer you follow in three months.
- Your email digest has nothing you'd forward.

Any one of these means a topic is too broad or a follow has gone stale. Use the Following tab to cut, and the [muting guide](/guides/medium/medium-mute-writers-publications/) for sources you never want back.

## If that doesn't work

If your Following feed is good but For you stays full of filler, prune your reading history as described in the [recommendations clean-up guide](/guides/medium/medium-clean-up-recommendations/). Recommendations draw on what you read as well as what you follow, so a run of curiosity clicks can outweigh a careful follow list.
