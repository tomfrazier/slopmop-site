[Rage bait](/glossary/rage-bait/) is a post written to make you angry enough to reply, quote or repost it, because angry engagement counts as engagement. On X it usually looks like a sweeping claim about a group of people, a screenshot of a stranger held up for mockery, or an outrage with no source. Before you reply, ask whether the post contains anything you could check. If it doesn't, the anger is the product, and the best response is to mark it Not interested and move on.

## Why rage bait works on X

A reply is a reply, whatever it says. Since October 2024, X's creator payouts have depended on engagement from Premium subscribers, and TechCrunch noted at the time that the change seemed to reward anything that drew replies, for whatever reason ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2024/10/09/x-changes-creator-payouts-to-depend-on-engagement-not-ads/)). A post that makes a thousand people furious can earn more attention than one that makes a hundred people nod.

There's a second effect. X says its recommendations take your feedback into account ([X Help Center](https://help.x.com/en/rules-and-policies/recommendations)), and replying to something is a strong kind of feedback. Arguing with rage bait tends to bring you more of it.

This guide is about a technique, and every side of every argument uses it. The examples below are deliberately invented and politically neutral.

## Five shapes to recognise

### 1. The sweeping generalisation

A claim about everyone in a group, phrased so that members of the group will want to object.

:::slop
Nobody under 30 knows how to make a phone call anymore. Embarrassing.
:::
:::fine
Our support line's call volume from customers under 30 fell by about a third this year while chat went up. We're moving two agents to chat in January.
:::

The first invites hundreds of "actually, I…" replies. The second describes something specific, with a number and a decision attached, and nobody needs to argue with it.

### 2. The stranger in the stocks

A screenshot of an ordinary person's post, often with the name visible, captioned for mockery: "Imagine thinking this." The original poster is usually not a public figure and had no say in being shown to a new audience. Quote-posting it to pile on extends the pile-on.

### 3. The sourceless outrage

"They're banning X now." "Apparently this is illegal in some country." No link, no name, no date. The vagueness is useful to the poster: there's nothing to check, so it can't be shown false, and readers fill the gap with whatever annoys them most. Doctored or miscaptioned media is a close cousin; the [cheapfake](/glossary/cheapfake/) page covers that.

### 4. The deliberate wrong take

A claim the poster may not believe, designed to collect corrections. Food and money are favourite subjects.

:::slop
Putting milk in before the water is the only correct way to make tea. If you disagree, you're wrong.
:::

Every correction is a reply. The post is working exactly as intended.

### 5. The us-and-them frame

"People like us do this; people like them do that." It's a quick way to make readers feel part of a side, and replies from the other side confirm the frame. When this is done at scale for persuasion, the [slopaganda](/glossary/slopaganda/) page describes the pattern.

## The check before you reply

Run through these in the few seconds before you tap reply or quote:

1. **Is there anything here I could verify?** A name, a date, a link, a number. If not, there's nothing to correct.
2. **Who is the target?** If it's a private person, quoting it widens the harm.
3. **Would my reply inform anyone?** If it would only express that I'm annoyed, it's engagement for the poster.
4. **Does this account post this shape a lot?** Open the profile. A timeline of outrage is a strategy.
5. **Will I care about this tomorrow?** Most rage bait has a half-life of an afternoon.

If the answers point to bait, skip it. If the post makes a checkable claim that's wrong and matters, a short reply with a source is a fair response, though a separate post of your own with the source avoids sending traffic to the original.

## What to do instead

- **Mark it Not interested.** Use the **•••** menu on the post. X uses this as a signal for recommendations ([X Help Center](https://help.x.com/en/rules-and-policies/recommendations)).
- **Don't quote it to dunk.** A quote-post shows the original to your followers too.
- **Mute the opener phrases.** "Imagine thinking", "unpopular opinion" and "nobody under" are reasonable entries; [how to use muted words on X](/guides/x/x-muted-words/) has the steps.
- **Retreat to a quieter tab.** The Following tab or a pinned List cuts out most recommended outrage; see [Following vs For You on X](/guides/x/x-following-vs-for-you/) and [how to use X Lists as a cleaner feed](/guides/x/x-lists-cleaner-feed/).

It judges the writing, not the topic or the side, so a news post that names who, what and when stays visible however angry it makes you; formula outrage built on a dramatic hook and a call for replies tends to score on several tells at once. - **Look after yourself.** If an afternoon of arguing on X leaves you wound up, closing the app is a legitimate choice.

## How to tell rage bait from real news

Real reporting can make you angry too, and sometimes it should. The difference is in what the post gives you:

- Real news names who, what, where and when, and links to a source.
- Rage bait leaves those out and leans on how you'll feel.
- Real news is usually posted by someone who'll correct it if it's wrong.
- Rage bait is usually followed by another rage bait an hour later.

If a post makes you angry and also tells you something you can check, read the source before reacting. If it only makes you angry, you've learned everything it had to offer.
