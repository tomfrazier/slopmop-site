Workslop is a work email or document that looks finished and doesn't move anything on: smooth sentences, headings, a "next steps" section, and no decision, number or owner anywhere in it. You can usually spot it by trying to say what it asks of you in one sentence and failing. The fix is a short reply that asks for the specific thing that's missing, sent without accusing anyone of using AI.

The term comes from a [Harvard Business Review article](https://hbr.org/2025/09/ai-generated-workslop-is-destroying-productivity) published on 22 September 2025 by researchers at BetterUp Labs and the Stanford Social Media Lab. The [workslop glossary entry](/glossary/workslop/) covers their findings. This guide is about the email that's sitting in your inbox now.

## What to look for in a workslop email

Read the email once, then check it against these. Three or more and you're probably holding workslop.

- **The subject line names only a topic.** "Q3 review: summary and next steps" tells you what it's about. "Q3 review: we're over budget by £18k" tells you what happened.
- **It would be true at any company.** Swap your team's name for another team's. If nothing breaks, nobody wrote it with your situation in mind.
- **The verbs are soft.** "Explore", "align", "consider", "continue to evaluate". Nobody is doing anything by any date.
- **Stock transitions hold it together.** "Additionally", "it's important to note", "moving forward". This is [stiff phrasing](/patterns/stiff-phrasing/), and in a work email it's often a sign the sentences were produced in a batch and not edited.
- **Praise without evidence.** "Great progress across workstreams" with no workstream named is [empty praise](/patterns/empty-praise/).
- **Bullet points that are labels.** "Stakeholder engagement", "Risk considerations", "Resourcing". Headings with no content under them.
- **No ask, or an ask for everything.** "Let me know your thoughts" on a 900-word document gives you nothing to answer.

Here's the shape, with a version that does the job:

:::slop
Subject: Onboarding improvements: key insights
Hi all, following our recent discussions, I've compiled key insights on the onboarding experience. Overall, feedback has been positive, with some opportunities for improvement. Moving forward, it will be important to align on priorities and continue iterating. Let me know your thoughts!
:::
:::fine
Subject: Onboarding: 3 of 12 new starters had no laptop on day one
All three were in the Bristol office, where IT orders on Mondays only. Proposal: order on the day the contract is signed. Ana, can IT do that from December? I need a yes or no by Thursday.
:::

The second email could have been drafted with AI too. It works because someone put in the one fact, the one proposal and the one question.

## What to reply to a workslop email

The aim is to get the missing piece without a row. Ask for the thing a useful version would contain. Pick the one that fits:

- **When there's no point:** "Thanks. What's the one thing you need from me here?"
- **When there's no decision:** "Is there a decision you want me to make? If so, what are the options?"
- **When there's no number:** "Do you have the figures behind 'mixed performance'? Even rough ones would help."
- **When there's no owner or date:** "Who's doing the next step, and by when?"
- **When it's a long document:** "Could you send me the three lines you'd want me to read if I only read three?"

Keep it to one question. A reply that lists everything wrong with the email will read as a telling-off, and the next one will arrive with the same gaps and more padding.

## What not to say

"Did you just paste this from ChatGPT?" is tempting and almost never helps. You may be wrong, it puts the sender on the defensive, and it makes the conversation about the tool. The problem is what the email leaves out, and that's what your reply should ask for. Someone who forwards AI output unread is acting as a [meat proxy](/glossary/meat-proxy/); the way to stop it is to make the gaps visible, one question at a time.

If it's a pattern from one person, raise it in a one-to-one with an example and what you'd have needed instead. That works better than reacting to every message.

## Triage: when to just skip it

Not every empty email needs a reply. A quick rule:

| The email | Do this |
|---|---|
| Asks you for something but it's unclear what | Reply with one question |
| A status update with nothing you need to act on | Archive it |
| A long summary of a meeting you were in | Skim for your name, then archive |
| Addressed to a big list, no ask | Archive; if it matters, someone will chase |
| From your manager or a client, no ask | Reply asking what they need from you |

## How not to send it yourself

Most workslop comes from people short of time. Before you send an email that AI helped draft:

1. **Write the subject as the point.** If you can't, you haven't worked out the point yet.
2. **Put one fact in the first two lines** that only your team would know: a number, a name, a date.
3. **End with one question** addressed to one person, with a deadline.
4. **Delete every sentence that would survive being sent to a different team.** In a padded draft that can be a third of it.
5. **Read it as the person receiving it.** Could they act on it without writing back?

A few minutes on that saves your colleague a round of emails asking what you meant.

## If it keeps happening

If workslop is common across a team, it's often a process problem: people are being asked for updates nobody reads, or for documents of a set length. Shorter templates help. So does asking for "the decision and the evidence" in place of a "summary". Agreeing that a three-line email is acceptable gives people permission to stop padding.
