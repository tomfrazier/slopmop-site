Start with one specific thing you know: a number, a decision, a mistake and what it cost. Write it in ordinary paragraphs, say who it's useful for, and stop when you've said it. Then cut the three things readers have learned to skip: the dramatic hook, the moral at the end and the request for comments.

## Before you write

Answer two questions in a sentence each. If you can't, the post isn't ready.

1. **What's the one thing a reader will know afterwards that they didn't before?** One thing. A post that teaches three lessons usually teaches none.
2. **Who is it for?** "Founders" is too broad. "Founders hiring their first salesperson" lets you pick what to include.

This also helps with LinkedIn's own ranking. Among the content signals LinkedIn lists for its feed are what a post is about, "whether it provides knowledge or advice", and "how constructive or professional the conversation is" ([LinkedIn Help](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a1339724)). LinkedIn doesn't publish how those are weighted, so treat that as a rough direction.

## 1. Put the fact first

The first line is the one people see before "see more". Use it for the most interesting true thing in the post, said plainly.

:::slop
Nobody talks about this.

But it's quietly killing your pipeline.
:::
:::fine
We stopped offering free trials in June and asked every prospect for a 20-minute call instead. Conversions doubled; sign-ups fell by 70%.
:::

The fine version is a better hook. It gives a surprising result and makes you want the details. The [scroll-stopper](/patterns/scroll-stopper/) page has more openers to avoid.

## 2. Say what it cost

Real experience has a downside, and leaving it out is what makes a post read like an advert. Add the price, the effort, the time it took, or the people it won't work for.

:::slop
This one change will double your conversions without any extra work.
:::
:::fine
It doubled conversions for us, but we sell a product that costs £30,000 a year. At £15 a month, a call per prospect wouldn't pay for itself.
:::

See [no-catch promises](/patterns/no-catch-promises/).

## 3. Write in paragraphs

Group related sentences together. Use line breaks where the subject changes, and lists where you have real items to list. A post with one sentence per line reads as a format before it reads as an idea.

## 4. Swap the big words for the plain claim

Go through the draft and underline every word that could describe anything: *transformative*, *powerful*, *game-changing*, *unlock*, *seamless*. Replace each with what actually happened, or delete it. If nothing happened that you can name, the sentence can go too. See [hype words](/patterns/hype-words/).

## 5. Cut the moral

If your last line explains what the post meant, delete it and reread. Readers who got this far understood. A closing line that's a "not X, but Y" slogan ("Hiring isn't about skills. It's about fit.") is the most common version.

## 6. Ask only questions you want answered

LinkedIn's spam page lists emoji or reaction polls that artificially boost engagement, and chain-letter posts requesting likes and shares, among content it may remove or limit ([LinkedIn Help](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/137372)). In 2022 it also said it would stop promoting posts that ask for reactions purely to boost reach ([Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/linkedin-updates-feed-algorithm-to-downrank-engagement-baiting-posts-and-po/623313/)).

So drop "Agree?", "Comment YES" and "Follow for more". If you're offering a document, link it. If you'd genuinely use replies, narrow the question to people who can answer it and say what you'll do with the answers.

And don't arrange for friends to pile into the comments as soon as you post. LinkedIn's community policies ask members not to agree ahead of time to like or re-share each other's content ([LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/legal/professional-community-policies)).

## 7. Read it as a stranger

Read the draft aloud, or the next morning. Then check:

- Could someone else in your job have posted this word for word? If yes, add what only you know.
- Is there at least one name, date, number or place?
- Would you send it to a colleague in an email? If it would feel odd there, it's probably performing.

## A full rewrite

Here's a whole post before and after, on the same facts.

:::slop
I almost gave up on hiring.

Then I tried something radical.

I stopped reading CVs. 🤯

The result? Game-changing.

Hiring isn't about the past. It's about potential.

Agree? 👇 Comment "HIRE" and I'll send you my framework.
:::
:::fine
For our last two engineering roles we stopped screening on CVs and sent every applicant the same 45-minute paid exercise (we paid £40 each). We hired two people we'd probably have rejected on paper: one had no degree, one had been out of work for two years.

The cost: 140 applicants, so about £5,600 and a lot of marking time. We won't do it for senior roles, where the pool is smaller and CVs tell you more.

The exercise is in a public doc linked in the first comment. If you've tried paid exercises at a company under 50 people, I'd like to know how you handled the marking.
:::

The rewrite is longer and may get fewer comments in its first hour. The comments it does get will be from people who hire, which is presumably who it was for.

## The nine-tell checklist

These are the nine habits Slop Mop asks about when it reads a post in a LinkedIn feed. They make a decent editing checklist whether or not your readers use it.

| Tell | Check your draft for |
|---|---|
| [Scroll-stopper](/patterns/scroll-stopper/) | A first line that hides the point; one-line paragraphs |
| [Engagement bait](/patterns/engagement-bait/) | Comment, repost, follow or tag requests you don't need |
| [Hype words](/patterns/hype-words/) | Words that would fit any product or idea |
| [Empty praise](/patterns/empty-praise/) | "Incredible", "powerful" with no detail behind them |
| [No-catch promises](/patterns/no-catch-promises/) | Benefits with no cost, effort or limit |
| ["Not X, but Y"](/patterns/not-x-but-y/) | Defining your point against something nobody said |
| [Flowery prose](/patterns/flowery-prose/) | Journeys, tapestries and dances in place of what happened |
| [Stiff phrasing](/patterns/stiff-phrasing/) | Furthermore, moreover, ultimately, it's worth noting |
| [Too-tidy story](/patterns/too-tidy-story/) | An unnamed character delivering a perfect lesson |

One tell on its own is rarely a problem; a post that ticks three or four is the kind readers learn to skip.

## If you draft with AI

That's fine, as long as the facts are yours and you've read every line. A model can tidy your notes, but it can't supply the number, the cost or the detail, and if you ask it to, it may invent them. Posting the output unread is what the glossary calls [AI slap](/glossary/ai-slap/): it hands the checking to your readers. The earlier guide on [spotting AI-written posts](/guides/linkedin/linkedin-spot-ai-written-posts/) covers what readers notice.
