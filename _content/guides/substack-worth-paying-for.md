A paid Substack is worth it when the paid posts give you something you'd otherwise have to find, work out or pay for elsewhere, and when the writer publishes often enough to justify the price. Read at least three recent free posts and the free preview of a paid one before you decide, and take a free trial if the publication offers one. Know the rules on renewals and refunds before you enter a card: Substack's own refund window is 7 days from payment, with exceptions.

## Before you pay: what to read

Most publications give away enough to judge them. Spend 15 minutes here before spending any money.

1. **Open the archive and sort by newest.** Check how often the writer actually published in the last three months, and how many of those posts were paid. A publication that promises weekly essays and managed four since summer is telling you something.
2. **Read three recent free posts in full.** Free posts are the writer's advert. If they're padded, the paid ones are unlikely to be better. Our [guide to spotting padded posts](/guides/substack/substack-spot-padded-ai-posts/) has a two-minute test.
3. **Read the preview of a paid post.** Many paid posts show the opening before the paywall. Judge whether the opening is doing work or just warming up.
4. **Look at the comments on a paid post.** Where they're visible, check whether there are any and whether they say anything. A paid community that's meant to be the main benefit should show signs of life.
5. **Read the About page and the paid pitch.** Note exactly what's promised: how many posts, what kind, any extras such as chat or archives.

## What a paid pitch should tell you

Good pitches are specific. Watch for [no-catch promises](/patterns/no-catch-promises/), where every benefit is listed and nothing is said about frequency, scope or limits.

:::slop
Join our inner circle and get exclusive, game-changing insights that will transform how you think. Everything you need, all in one place.
:::
:::fine
Paid subscribers get one long post a month on UK housing data, with the spreadsheets. Free subscribers get a short summary of each. I skip August.
:::

The first could be on any publication. The second tells you the subject, the frequency, what you get on top and when you won't get it, which is everything you need to price it.

## The worth-it checklist

Answer each of these with yes or no. Four or more yeses is a reasonable case for paying; two or fewer is a reasonable case for staying free.

- **Original material.** Does the paid tier include reporting, data, analysis or experience you won't find in the writer's free posts or anywhere else?
- **Reliable schedule.** Did they publish what they promise, roughly when they promise it, over the last three months?
- **Specifics.** Do the posts you've read contain names, numbers, sources or worked examples?
- **Useful to you.** Would you have acted differently on something in the last month if you'd read it?
- **Not a duplicate.** Are you already paying for something that covers the same ground?
- **The price per post makes sense.** Divide the annual price by the number of paid posts you'd realistically read. Compare that with what else you pay for reading.

[Empty praise](/patterns/empty-praise/) from other readers doesn't count towards any of these. "Essential reading" in a recommendation tells you someone liked it, not what's in it.

A note on loyalty: plenty of people pay for a writer to support them, regardless of how much they read. That's a perfectly good reason. Just make it a deliberate choice, since the feeling of knowing a writer through their Notes and chats can make cancelling feel personal. That pull has a name, [parasocial](/glossary/parasocial/) attachment, and it helps to spot it when you review your subscriptions.

## Try before you buy

- **Free trials.** Some publications offer a 7-day free trial. You have to enter payment details to start one, and you're charged automatically when it ends unless you cancel first. You can only redeem a trial once per publication per account. If you cancel a trial that ends within 10 days, Substack says it ends straight away, so set a reminder for the day before rather than cancelling on day one.
- **Teaser posts.** Some publications let free subscribers read one paid post through the Substack app. Substack limits this to free subscribers who don't have the app installed and have never opened a paid post this way, and you may be asked to verify your phone number. It's one post, once.

## Renewal, pausing and cancelling

- **Renewal is automatic.** Monthly and annual plans renew on their own. Substack says annual subscribers get a reminder email about a week before renewal.
- **Cancelling.** On the web, go to substack.com/settings, choose the publication under **Subscriptions**, and on its **Manage subscription** page use the link in **Account actions**. You keep paid access until the end of the period you've paid for, and cancelling doesn't refund it. Subscriptions bought in the iOS app are cancelled through the Apple App Store.
- **Pausing.** Instead of cancelling, you can pause for 1, 3 or 6 months, once every 6 months. Charges and paid access both stop, and your remaining time is restored when it resumes. In-app iOS subscriptions can't be paused.

## When you can get money back

Refunds are mainly up to the writer, and Substack asks you to contact them first. Substack itself says it will refund in three cases:

1. **Within 7 days of payment**, unless you've claimed a subscriber perk or the writer has marked the subscription as non-refundable.
2. **A dormant publication**: no posts for at least 6 months for an annual subscriber, or at least 1 month from payment for a monthly one.
3. **Payment errors**, such as a duplicate charge on the same account at the same time.

Refunds can take up to 10 business days to show. App Store purchases are refunded at Apple's discretion. These are Substack's published policies as of 18 October 2026; check the current page before relying on them.

## If you're on the fence

Pay monthly for two months instead of annually. That's long enough to see whether the writer keeps their schedule, and short enough that a disappointing run costs little. If you finish the two months having read most of what arrived, switch to annual if it's cheaper. If you haven't, cancel and stay on the free list.
