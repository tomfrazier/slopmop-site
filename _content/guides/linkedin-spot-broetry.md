Broetry is the nickname for LinkedIn posts written as a column of one-sentence lines, usually telling a short story that ends in a business lesson. You can spot it from the preview: a hook that withholds something, line breaks after every sentence, and a "see more" that promises the payoff. If the visible lines contain no names, dates or numbers, the rest of the post almost never does either.

## What [broetry](/glossary/broetry/) looks like

The format has a fixed order. A dramatic opening line, a pause, a turn, a character, a lesson, and often an instruction to "read that again".

:::slop
I got fired on a Monday.

No warning.

No severance.

That night my daughter asked me one question.

"Daddy, are you still you?"

That's when I realised.

Your job is what you do. It isn't who you are.

Read that again.
:::
:::fine
I was made redundant in March along with 40 others in our product org. The two things that helped most: a former manager who sent my CV to three people the same week, and a spreadsheet of every role I applied for with the date and outcome. I started a new job in July.
:::

The slop version could be true. The problem is that nothing in it is checkable or useful, and its layout is built to make you click "see more" before you realise that.

## What to look for

Each habit on its own is common in ordinary writing. [Broetry](/glossary/broetry/) stacks most of them in one post.

### One sentence per line

Line breaks after every sentence stretch a 60-word idea down the screen, so the post fills more of your feed and the preview ends on a cliffhanger. Short paragraphs are fine. Every line being its own paragraph is the tell.

### A hook that withholds

"I almost quit." "Nobody talks about this." "Then something changed." The first line names an outcome and hides the content. The [scroll-stopper](/patterns/scroll-stopper/) page lists the common openers.

### An unnamed supporting character

A taxi driver, a cleaner, a six-year-old, an intern, an old mentor. They have no name, the place has no name, and they speak in perfectly formed lessons. Real conversations are messier and rarely end on a quotable line.

### A moral in the last lines

The story stops, and the writer explains what it meant. Often the moral is a contrast: "Your network isn't your net worth. Your character is." The anecdote was there to deliver this line. See [too-tidy story](/patterns/too-tidy-story/).

### Emotional words standing in for detail

"I was shaking." "Tears in my eyes." "The most powerful lesson of my career." Strong feelings with nothing specific around them are a form of [empty praise](/patterns/empty-praise/), aimed at the writer's own story.

## A two-step test

You can run this on the preview, before you expand anything.

1. **Join the lines up in your head.** Read the visible part as one paragraph. If it's two sentences long and says nothing yet, the format is doing the work.
2. **Delete the moral.** Imagine the post without its final lesson. If what's left is a vague scene with no names, dates or numbers, there was no story, only a frame for the lesson.

If the post passes, expand it. Some short-line posts are fine, and the next section covers those.

## When short lines are fine

Line breaks are a layout choice, and plenty of good posts use them. These usually earn the space:

- **Lists of real items.** Five tools, with what each one costs. Three changes to a process, with the dates.
- **A story with checkable detail.** A named company (yours), a month, a figure, an outcome that wasn't neat.
- **Writers with a real voice.** Some people simply write in short bursts, and the asides and odd word choices show it. Slop Mop's counter-signal "sounds like a person" exists because voice is a fair reason to give a post the benefit of the doubt.

:::fine
Three things I changed in our hiring process this year:

Cut the take-home test from 6 hours to 90 minutes. Completion rate went up; quality didn't drop.

Put the salary range in the first message.

Stopped asking "where do you see yourself in five years". Nobody had a useful answer, including me.
:::

That post uses short lines too. Each one carries a fact.

## What to do about it

1. **Don't click "see more" to check.** LinkedIn says the content you spend the most time viewing is one of the signals it uses ([LinkedIn Help](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a1339724)). Decide from the preview.
2. **Use Not interested.** Click the **More** icon (three dots) at the top right of the post, or tap it in the app, and choose **Not interested**. The author isn't told ([LinkedIn Help](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/13325)).
3. **Unfollow the habitual writers.** If someone posts a parable every morning, **Unfollow [name]** in the same menu keeps you connected without the posts ([LinkedIn Help](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/72150)).
4. **Don't quote-post it to mock it.** A sarcastic repost spreads the original to your network and gives it more engagement.

Slop Mop, the Chrome extension that's live on LinkedIn, asks about scroll-stopper hooks and too-tidy stories separately, so a post built on both is likely to fold. It needs more than one tell to flag a post, which means a short-line post full of real detail stays visible.

## If you write this way yourself

Some people write this way simply because the format is everywhere, and the preview cuts long posts off after a few lines, so the opening does matter. You can keep a strong opening and drop the rest. Open with the most interesting fact, write in normal paragraphs, name what you can, and stop when you've said it. The [engagement bait guide](/guides/linkedin/linkedin-filter-engagement-bait/) covers the ending that usually comes with this format, the "agree?" or the comment-for-a-PDF offer, and why to drop it.
