Look for a **Jump to recipe** link near the top, or open the page in your browser's reader mode, or use the recipe card's **Print** button, which often shows only the ingredients and method. Once you're at the recipe, spend 30 seconds checking whether anyone cooked it: real recipes have quantities you can measure, times with cues ("until the edges brown") and comments from people who made them.

## Why recipe pages are so long

Some of the length is personal writing that the cook's regular readers enjoy, and some is advertising space, since each extra screen of text is room for more ads. There's also a legal reason that's often mentioned. The U.S. Copyright Office [says](https://www.copyright.gov/help/faq/faq-protect.html) that "a mere listing of ingredients is not protected under copyright law", while a recipe with "substantial literary expression" in its explanation or directions may be. A bare list of ingredients is easy to copy; the writing around it belongs to the author.

That writing isn't the problem. The problem is the generated version: pages that wrap a recipe nobody tested in paragraphs nobody needed, made in bulk to rank. Google's [spam policies](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials/spam-policies) call that "scaled content abuse" when many unoriginal pages are made mainly to manipulate rankings. You'll still meet plenty of them.

## Three ways to get to the recipe

### 1. Use the jump link

Many recipe blogs put a **Jump to recipe** button or link near the top, often next to **Print recipe**. It scrolls straight to the recipe card. If you can't see one, look just under the headline or the first photo. The label varies from site to site.

### 2. Use reader mode

Reader views strip ads and pop-ups, which on recipe sites can halve the scroll. In Chrome on a computer, right-click and choose **Open in reading mode**; on Android, tap **More** and then **Show Reading mode**. In Safari, use **Show Reader**. [How to use reader mode](/guides/websites/web-reader-mode/) has every step for each browser.

One caution: reader views sometimes drop the recipe card itself, because it's built differently from the article text. If the ingredients are missing, switch back.

On iPhone, Safari can open a site in Reader automatically: tap the page menu in the address bar, choose **Website Settings** and turn on **Use Reader Automatically**, per [Apple's guide](https://support.apple.com/guide/iphone/hide-ads-and-distractions-iphdc30e3b86/ios). That's handy for a recipe site you use every week.

### 3. Use the print view

On many sites, the recipe card's **Print** button opens a clean page with just the ingredients, method and notes. You don't have to print it. It's often the easiest view to keep open on a tablet while you cook.

## Is the recipe real? A 30-second check

Getting past the padding is half the job. The other half is knowing whether the recipe works. Generated recipe pages often look complete and have never been cooked.

### Quantities you can measure

Good recipes give amounts you can put on a scale or in a spoon, and for baking, usually weights. Vague amounts for the ingredients that matter most are a warning.

:::slop
Add some flour and a good amount of butter, then mix until it feels right.
:::
:::fine
Rub 60g cold butter into 120g plain flour until no pieces bigger than a pea remain.
:::

### Times with cues

Ovens vary, so careful cooks give a time and a sign to look for: a colour, a texture, a sound, a temperature. A time alone, or no time at all, suggests nobody watched it happen.

:::slop
Bake until perfectly golden and delicious.
:::
:::fine
Bake for 25 to 30 minutes, until the top is deep brown and a skewer comes out with a few moist crumbs.
:::

### Steps in a sensible order

Read the method once before you shop. Look for steps that can't work: an ingredient used before it's prepared, an oven never preheated, a pan size that doesn't fit the quantities, or an ingredient in the method that isn't in the list. Generated recipes often slip on exactly these joins.

### Comments from people who made it

Scroll to the comments if there are any. "Looks delicious!" tells you nothing. Useful comments mention what happened: "I used a 20cm tin and it took 10 minutes longer", "halve the salt if you use salted butter". Several of those, and replies from the author, are good evidence that people have cooked it. A page with dozens of five-star ratings and no detailed comments is weaker. That's [empty praise](/patterns/empty-praise/) at scale.

## What the intro can tell you

You don't have to read the life story, but a quick skim says something about the recipe.

- **Useful intros** explain choices: why this flour, what went wrong in earlier versions, what to substitute. They read like notes from someone who stood at the stove.
- **Padded intros** describe the dish in [flowery prose](/patterns/flowery-prose/) ("a symphony of autumn flavours"), repeat the recipe name over and over, or tell a neat story about a grandmother that could sit on any recipe. The last one is the [too-tidy story](/patterns/too-tidy-story/) tell.

:::slop
There's nothing quite like the warm embrace of a homemade apple crumble on a crisp autumn evening. This timeless classic brings families together like nothing else.
:::
:::fine
I've cut the sugar in the filling to 40g because Bramleys collapse into a sharp purée, and the topping is sweet enough to balance it. Eating apples need even less.
:::

## If the recipe fails the check

Close it and search again, adding a detail that padded pages rarely include, such as a weight or a specific technique: `apple crumble 120g flour` will bring back different pages from `best apple crumble recipe`. If one site keeps serving untested recipes, leave it out with `-site:` or block it, as described in [How to block a site from your search results](/guides/websites/web-block-sites-search-results/).
