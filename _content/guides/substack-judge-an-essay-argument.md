Find the one sentence the essay most wants you to believe, then check what holds it up: evidence you could look up, reasoning you could follow, or only confident writing. A strong essay tells you where its claims come from and what would change the writer's mind. A weak one leans on stories, big framing and other people's agreement. You can run this check in about 10 minutes.

This guide is about the argument. If the problem is that the post is long and empty, start with [how to spot a padded or AI-written post](/guides/substack/substack-spot-padded-ai-posts/) instead.

## Before you start

- **Pick essays that matter to you.** This is worth doing for a post you might act on, share or pay for. Not for every issue.
- **Keep a scrap of paper or a note open.** Writing down the main claim in your own words is most of the work.
- **Judge the essay, not the writer.** Someone you admire can write a weak argument, and someone you dislike can be right.

## 1. Find the main claim

Read the title, the first two paragraphs and the last one. Then write one sentence: "The writer wants me to believe that…"

If you can't finish that sentence, the essay may not have a claim at all, only a mood. That's common in posts built on [flowery prose](/patterns/flowery-prose/) or big framing:

:::slop
We are living through a great unravelling. The old certainties are dissolving, and those who cling to them will be swept away.
:::
:::fine
Since the March rate change, three of the five lenders I track have stopped offering 10-year fixed mortgages. If the other two follow, buyers lose the only product that lasts past the next election.
:::

The second makes a claim you could test: three of five lenders, a specific product, a consequence. The first gives you a feeling and nothing to check.

## 2. List what holds it up

Go through the essay and mark each reason offered for the main claim. Sort them into four kinds:

| Kind | What it looks like | How much weight it carries |
|---|---|---|
| **Evidence** | Numbers, documents, data, quotes with a named source and a date | Most, if you can check it |
| **First-hand experience** | "I ran this for six months, and here's what happened" | Real, but limited to one case |
| **Reasoning** | "If A, then B, because C" | Depends on whether each step holds |
| **Atmosphere** | Anecdotes, rhetorical questions, "everyone knows", appeals to how things feel | Little on its own |

An essay that's all atmosphere can still be a good read. It just hasn't argued anything.

## 3. Check two sources

You don't need to check everything. Pick the two claims the argument leans on most and look them up.

1. **Click the link if there is one.** Does the linked page say what the essay says it says? Writers sometimes cite a source that makes a narrower point, or a headline rather than the study behind it.
2. **If there's no link, search for the claim.** Look for where the number or quote first appeared, and who else reports it. A figure that only appears in other newsletters is weaker than one from an original report or dataset.
3. **Check the date.** A source from several years ago may still be right, but the essay should say so if things have changed since.

Phrases such as "studies show", "research proves" and "experts agree" with nothing attached are a reason for caution, not a source.

## 4. Look for the moves that only sound strong

Some patterns make an argument feel finished without doing the work. When you spot one, ask what's underneath it.

- **The redefinition.** ["It's not X, it's Y"](/patterns/not-x-but-y/) can rename a problem without showing anything new about it.
- **The single story.** One vivid case, presented as typical. If the essay's only support is a [too-tidy story](/patterns/too-tidy-story/), ask how often that actually happens.
- **The straw opponent.** The other side is described in a form nobody actually holds. A fair essay states the best version of the view it disagrees with.
- **The certainty jump.** "This may suggest" in paragraph three becomes "this proves" by paragraph eight, with no new evidence in between.
- **The missing cost.** Every recommendation has upsides and no downsides. Real proposals have trade-offs.

## 5. Ask what would change the writer's mind

Good essays answer this themselves. They say what evidence would make them wrong, or what they're unsure about. If an essay treats every objection as bad faith, or never mentions one, it's closer to persuasion than analysis. On political subjects, researchers use the word [slopaganda](/glossary/slopaganda/) for AI-generated content pushed out in volume to shift beliefs. The test here is the same whichever side an essay is on.

## 6. Decide what to do with it

Once you've done the steps, you'll usually land in one of three places:

- **Holds up.** The claim is clear, the main sources say what the essay says, and the writer admits limits. Worth sharing or acting on.
- **Interesting but unproven.** A good question or a plausible idea without the evidence yet. Worth reading, not worth repeating as fact.
- **Doesn't hold.** The sources don't back it, or there's nothing but atmosphere. Skip it, and if it happens often with one writer, it's a reason to rethink the subscription.

## Sharing what you found

If you restack an essay on Notes, Substack lets you highlight a snippet and restack it as a quote with your own text. Quote the actual claim and say what you think of the evidence, instead of just "Must read." It helps the next reader do the same check faster.

## If you're short of time

Do steps 1 and 3 only: write down the main claim, and check the single source it depends on most. That catches most weak arguments in under five minutes.
