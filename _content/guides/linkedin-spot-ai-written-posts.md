You can't reliably tell from the words alone whether a LinkedIn post was written by AI, and detection tools can't either. What you can judge in a few seconds is whether the post contains anything specific: a number with context, a decision, a cost, a detail only the writer would know. Posts that fail that test are worth skipping whoever wrote them.

## Why authorship is the wrong first question

There's a lot of AI writing on LinkedIn. In November 2024 [WIRED reported](https://www.wired.com/story/linkedin-ai-generated-influencers/) an analysis by the detection company Originality AI of 8,795 public English-language posts over 100 words, which estimated that over 54% were likely AI-generated. WIRED also noted that LinkedIn offers its own AI writing tools to Premium subscribers. Treat the figure as an estimate: it comes from a detector, and detectors make mistakes.

Some of those mistakes fall unevenly. A 2023 study found that several widely used GPT detectors "consistently misclassify non-native English writing samples as AI-generated", and that simple prompting could get AI text past them ([Liang et al., arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02819)). On a network full of people writing professionally in their second or third language, that matters.

There's a practical problem too. Plenty of useful posts are drafted with AI and then edited by someone who knows the subject, and plenty of empty posts are typed by hand. If your aim is a feed worth reading, sorting by author type gets you the wrong posts on both sides. The glossary entry on [AI suspicion](/glossary/ai-suspicion/) covers why the gut feeling is common, often useful, and still not proof.

## What to look for instead

Unedited AI drafts do have habits. So do tired humans writing to a formula. The habits are the same either way, and they're the reason the post wastes your time.

### Vocabulary that could describe anything

You'll see words like *transformative*, *game-changing*, *unlock* and *elevate* in unedited model drafts and in marketing copy alike. Their real problem is that each one stands where a fact should be. See [hype words](/patterns/hype-words/).

:::slop
AI is transforming the way we work. Leaders who embrace this game-changing shift will unlock unprecedented value for their teams.
:::
:::fine
We gave our support team a model that drafts replies from our help docs. First-response time fell from 6 hours to 2. Agents still edit every reply, and about one in five drafts gets thrown away.
:::

### Contrasts against a claim nobody made

"It's not about the tools. It's about the people." The shape sounds wise and commits to nothing. See ["not X, but Y"](/patterns/not-x-but-y/).

### Stock transitions and hedges

*Furthermore*, *moreover*, *it's important to note*, *ultimately*. These are signs of filler being stitched together. See [stiff phrasing](/patterns/stiff-phrasing/).

### Lists of three with nothing inside

"Clarity, consistency and courage." Three abstract nouns in a row, often in bold, often repeated as headings in a carousel. Ask what any of them would look like on a Tuesday.

### A shape that fits every topic

Hook, short paragraphs, three lessons, a question at the end. Swap the subject from hiring to cloud costs and the post barely changes. That interchangeability is the strongest single sign, and it says nothing certain about authorship.

## A 30-second test to spot AI-written LinkedIn posts

Read the post, then answer four questions:

1. **Is there one detail that couldn't be made up in general?** A team size, a month, a figure, a tool name, a place.
2. **Does it say what something cost or where it failed?** Real experience nearly always has a downside attached. See [no-catch promises](/patterns/no-catch-promises/).
3. **Could you act on it tomorrow?** Even a small, specific idea counts.
4. **Would it read differently if a different person had posted it?** If not, it's a template.

Two or more "no" answers and the post probably isn't worth finishing. That holds whether a model, a ghostwriter or the named author wrote it.

:::slop
Here's the truth about leadership in 2026:

The best leaders listen.
The best leaders adapt.
The best leaders inspire.

Which one are you working on? 👇
:::
:::fine
I've run weekly one-to-ones with seven engineers for two years. The change that helped most was letting them set the agenda in a shared doc the day before. Sessions got shorter, and two people raised problems they'd sat on for months.
:::

The fine version might have been drafted with AI. It passes all four questions, so it doesn't matter.

## What not to do

- **Don't accuse people in the comments.** "This is ChatGPT" is usually unprovable, sometimes wrong, and lands hardest on people writing in a second language. The [AI shaming](/glossary/ai-shaming/) entry explains how accusations stick even when they're mistaken.
- **Don't rely on a detector score.** If you paste a post into one, read the result as a guess about style. It tells you nothing reliable about the person.
- **Don't treat polish as guilt.** Clean grammar and tidy structure are what good editors produce too.

## What to do with the posts that fail

Scroll on, or tell LinkedIn. Click the **More** icon (three dots) at the top right of the post, or tap it in the app, and choose **Not interested** ([LinkedIn Help](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/13325)). LinkedIn says its feed weighs, among other things, whether a post "provides knowledge or advice" ([LinkedIn Help](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a1339724)), so feedback on empty posts points the same way.
