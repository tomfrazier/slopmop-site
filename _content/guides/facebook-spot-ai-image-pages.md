An AI image page is a Facebook Page that posts a steady stream of generated pictures (cabins, carvings, cakes, soldiers, babies) to collect reactions, then uses the audience for clicks or sales. You can usually tell from the Page rather than the picture: captions copied across unrelated Pages, a history of name changes, and a link waiting in the first comment. Checking takes about a minute.

## Why the picture alone won't tell you

Image generators have got good enough that hunting for a sixth finger is unreliable, and some real photos look odd. Meta labels some AI content with **AI info**, but only when it detects industry-standard signals in the file or the uploader discloses it. In September 2024 Meta said that for images only edited with AI, it would move the label into the post's menu ([Meta](https://about.fb.com/news/2024/04/metas-approach-to-labeling-ai-generated-content-and-manipulated-media/)). A missing label proves nothing.

The behaviour around the picture is harder to fake. The best public study of these Pages, by Renee DiResta and Josh Goldstein, followed 120 Facebook Pages that had each posted at least 50 AI-generated images. Their mean follower count was about 129,000, and one post with an AI image was among the 20 most viewed pieces of content on Facebook in the third quarter of 2023, with 40 million views ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.12838)). The signs below come from what that study found and from how these posts are built.

## Signs of an AI image page on Facebook

### 1. A caption that's doing the asking

The caption usually asks for a reaction or a kind word, and often has nothing to do with the image. The study found the same phrases repeated across Pages that seemed unconnected: "Made it with my own hands", "No one ever blessed me" and "This is my first cake! will be glad for your marks" ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.12838)). One AI image of a crab-shaped Jesus carried the "Made it with my own hands" caption and still collected 209,000 engagements.

:::slop
I'm 92 and made this table from one oak tree. My family says it's ugly 😔 Do you like it?
:::
:::fine
Finished the oak table for my sister's kitchen. Took about six weekends. The top's slightly bowed at one end because I didn't let the boards dry long enough.
:::

The first gives you a sad backstory and a question. The second gives you details the writer would have no reason to invent, including a mistake.

If you suspect a caption, copy a distinctive phrase and search for it on Facebook. When the same words turn up under different pictures on several Pages, you've found a template.

### 2. A Page whose history doesn't match its posts

Every Facebook Page has a **Page transparency** section showing when it was created and its previous names, among other details ([Facebook Help](https://www.facebook.com/help/323314944866264)). To open it:

1. Go to the Page by tapping or clicking its name on the post.
2. Tap or click the Page's name under the cover photo.
3. Choose **Transparency** (Facebook's help page lists it as "Transparency and privacy policy"; the label may differ slightly on your device).

Look at the creation date and the name history. In the DiResta and Goldstein sample, 46 of the Pages had changed their names, often from a completely different subject. One had been a touring band's Page since 2011, then renamed itself "Life Nature" in December 2023 and started posting AI images ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.12838)). Others had been a church and a windmill seller. A nature Page that used to sell windmills was bought, borrowed or taken over.

### 3. A link in the first comment

Spam Pages in the study often posted the image with a copied caption and left a link to an outside website in the first comment ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.12838)). The sites tended to be ad-heavy content farms, some full of AI-written text. A Page about tiny cabins that links to "plans" on a site you've never heard of is selling your click.

### 4. A posting rate no person could keep up

Open the Page and scroll. Ten flawless "handmade" sculptures a day, each by a different elderly maker, from a Page that also posts celebrity babies and soldiers, isn't a hobby.

### 5. Comments that never touch the picture

Read a few comments. Rows of "Amen", "Beautiful" and "Happy birthday" with no one asking how anything was made suggest people reacting to the caption, not the image. The study found many commenters seemed unaware the images were synthetic, while a smaller number left warnings ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.12838)). Those warning comments are a useful clue in their own right.

### The picture itself, as a last check

When the Page signs are mixed, look closely at the image. Text in the background that isn't quite letters, hands and tools that merge, and repeated faces in a crowd are common tells. Treat them as supporting evidence. Some real photos are blurry, and the best generated ones have none of these faults.

## What it means when you find one

A Page posting AI pictures isn't automatically a scam. The study sorted its 120 Pages into spam, scam and "other creator" ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.12838)), and plenty of people enjoy AI art that says what it is. The problem is the combination: unlabelled pictures presented as real, a caption fishing for sympathy, and a business model that depends on you not noticing. That's [AI slop](/glossary/ai-slop/) in its plainest form, and the reaction-fishing caption is [engagement bait](/patterns/engagement-bait/).

Meta said in April 2025 that accounts posting captions unrelated to their images, or running networks that share the same content, would have their reach and monetisation cut ([Meta](https://about.fb.com/news/2025/04/cracking-down-spammy-content-facebook/)). Plenty still get through to feeds.

## What to do

1. **Don't react or comment.** Even a comment calling it fake counts as engagement.
2. **Choose Not interested** from the post's three-dot menu ([Facebook Help](https://www.facebook.com/help/268028706671439)). The [first guide in this series](/guides/facebook/facebook-reduce-suggested-posts/) covers that and the other ways to see fewer suggested posts.
3. **Report it if it's selling something that doesn't exist** or asking for personal details. Report post is in the same menu.
4. **Tell the person who shared it, privately**, if it's a friend or relative. A public correction tends to embarrass rather than help.
