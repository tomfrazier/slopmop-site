A padded post spends its words on set-up, restatement and decoration, and very few on claims you can check or use. You can usually tell within two minutes by reading the first three paragraphs and the last one: if they could swap places without anyone noticing, there's little in between. Substack also has a built-in **Scan for AI text** tool, but it answers a different question, so use it as one clue among several.

## What padding looks like

Padding has a few reliable shapes. You'll see more than one at once in a bad issue.

- **The long run-up.** Two or three paragraphs about the week, the weather or how busy the writer has been before the first point arrives.
- **Restating the title.** The headline promises "Why remote teams miss deadlines", and the first 300 words say that remote teams miss deadlines and that this matters.
- **Lists that never land.** Seven numbered points, each a sentence of advice with no example, number or source behind it.
- **The recap ending.** A closing section that repeats each heading in a sentence. If the summary would serve as the whole post, the post was too long.
- **Borrowed authority.** "Studies show" or "experts agree" with no link, name or date.

Here's the same point made twice:

:::slop
In today's ever-evolving world, communication has never been more important. Furthermore, as we all know, clear writing matters. It's not just about words, it's about connection. Let's explore why this matters more than ever.
:::
:::fine
We cut our weekly update from 1,200 words to 300 in June. Replies from the team went up, from 4 a week to 11. The thing people said they missed was the list of decisions, so that's now the first line.
:::

The first has [stiff phrasing](/patterns/stiff-phrasing/) ("Furthermore", "as we all know"), a ["not X, but Y"](/patterns/not-x-but-y/) turn and a promise of exploration that hasn't started. The second has a date, two numbers, a trade-off and something a reader could copy.

## The tells that suggest a machine did the padding

People padded newsletters long before chatbots. What AI tools changed is the cost: a writer can now produce 2,000 smooth words from a one-line idea. Text produced that way tends to carry a recognisable set of habits, and our [patterns](/patterns/) pages cover each one in detail:

- **[Flowery prose](/patterns/flowery-prose/):** tapestries, symphonies, "a delicate dance between".
- **[Hype words](/patterns/hype-words/):** game-changing, seamless, unlock, elevate.
- **[Empty praise](/patterns/empty-praise/):** "a truly transformative read" with nothing about what it transformed.
- **[Too-tidy stories](/patterns/too-tidy-story/):** an anecdote with no names, dates or places that exists only to deliver the moral.
- **Even rhythm.** Paragraphs of the same length, each ending in a neat summary sentence, with no asides, jokes or second thoughts.
- **No author in it.** Nothing the writer saw, did, got wrong or changed their mind about.

One or two of these mean little. Plenty of human writers love a flourish. A post that shows four or five, and gives you nothing specific in return, is what people mean by [AI slop](/glossary/ai-slop/). A tidier, more expensive-looking version of the same emptiness is sometimes called [tasteslop](/glossary/tasteslop/).

## Using Substack's Scan for AI text

Substack added an AI transparency tool that uses a detection service called Pangram to estimate how much of a post or note is human-written or AI-assisted.

**To scan a post on the web**

1. Open the post from your **Subscriptions** so it opens in the Substack Reader.
2. Click the **three dots** in the top right corner.
3. Select **Scan for AI text**.

**To scan a note:** click the three dots on the note and select **Scan for AI text**. Short notes show a "not enough text" message instead.

**In the app:** tap a post or note, tap the three dots in the upper right, then **Scan for AI text**. Substack lists this for the iOS app, with Android marked as coming soon.

**To label your whole feed:** in Settings, under **Content preferences → AI Content**, turn on **Label content**. Posts and notes from other people get a badge when Pangram judges them to be 100% AI-generated, or when the author has switched detection off. It's off by default and only affects your view.

**Where it doesn't work.** Substack says the scan covers only posts and notes published on or after 21 July 2026. It isn't available for video or audio posts, for posts read on a publication's own site or custom domain, or in emails. Writers can also disable detection on any post or note, in which case you'll see "AI detection unavailable". That isn't proof of anything; some writers simply opt out.

## Why the scan isn't the whole answer

The scan estimates *who or what produced the text*. Your question as a reader is usually *is this worth my time*. Those come apart in both directions:

- A writer can use an AI tool to tidy a post full of their own reporting, numbers and judgement. That post may scan as AI-assisted and still be the best thing in your inbox this week.
- A writer can type every word of a padded, recycled post themselves. It will scan as human and still waste ten minutes.

Detection tools also make mistakes, which is why Substack gives writers a **Report detection error** button. Use the percentage to decide where to look harder, then judge the writing.

## A two-minute test for any issue

1. **Read the first paragraph.** Does it make a claim, or announce that a claim is coming?
2. **Skip to the middle.** Pick any paragraph. Does it contain a name, number, date, example or source?
3. **Read the last paragraph.** Does it add something, or repeat the headings?
4. **Count what you'd keep.** If you'd forward one sentence to a colleague, which one? If you can't find one, the issue was padding.

Two or more "no" answers is a good reason to stop reading. If it's the third issue in a row from the same writer, it's a reason to move them to the Go pile in [your subscription clean-up](/guides/substack/substack-too-many-subscriptions/).

## If you're still not sure

- **Look at the comments.** A thin post with a thoughtful comment thread may still be worth the visit for the thread.
- **Check the writer's older posts.** A good writer can have a lazy week. A run of identical-feeling issues is a pattern.
- **Give it one specific test.** Pick the post's most important claim and ask what would have to be true for it to be wrong. If the post never says, it hasn't done the work.

Slop Mop, which judges the writing rather than guessing who wrote it, is coming soon to Substack; today it works on LinkedIn only.
