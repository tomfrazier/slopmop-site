Treat the free preview as the writer's pitch: if the opening paragraphs state a specific problem, show that the writer has done the work, and promise something you can't get from the headline, the rest is probably worth it. If the preview is all hook and no substance, the paywalled part rarely makes up for it. A few minutes on the preview can save one of your limited monthly reads.

## What a member-only story is

Medium lets writers in its Partner Program put stories behind a paywall. Those are member-only stories, marked with a star on the preview. Members pay for unlimited access: Medium's [membership page](https://help.medium.com/hc/en-us/articles/115004545567-Become-a-Medium-Member) lists US$5 a month or US$50 a year for a standard membership, and US$15 a month or US$150 a year for Friend of Medium, which pays writers more for each member-only read. Part of the fee goes to the writers you read.

Non-members see a preview of a member-only story and can read a limited number in full each month. Medium has changed the allowance more than once, so check the count shown on the story itself rather than relying on a number from an old blog post.

One policy point is useful for readers. Medium's [AI content policy](https://help.medium.com/hc/en-us/articles/22576852947223-Artificial-Intelligence-AI-content-policy) says stories with AI-generated writing, disclosed or not, can't be paywalled in the Partner Program. That's a rule, not a guarantee: it depends on enforcement, and it says nothing about whether a human-written paywalled story is any good.

## Why the preview matters more on Medium

For a member, a weak story costs time. For a non-member, it also costs one of a few reads that month. Either way, the paywall gives writers a reason to make the visible part as gripping as possible, and some put all their effort there. A good preview isn't proof of a good story, but a bad preview is strong evidence of a bad one.

## The preview checklist

### 1. Does the opening state a specific problem?

Good previews tell you exactly what the story covers. Weak ones open with a promise and a cliffhanger, the [scroll-stopper](/patterns/scroll-stopper/) built to make you click "read more".

:::slop
Most people will never understand this. I didn't either, until a single conversation changed everything. What I discovered will shock you.
:::
:::fine
Our team spent four months moving a payments service from a monolith to three smaller services. This covers the two decisions I'd reverse, with the incident numbers before and after.
:::

The second tells you the subject, the scale and what you'll get. You can decide in five seconds.

### 2. Is there any evidence in the visible part?

Look for one concrete thing before the paywall: a figure, a named method, a date, a short example. Writers who have real material tend to show a little of it early. A preview with none at all usually belongs to a story with none at all.

### 3. Does the headline promise something with no cost?

"How I made $10,000 in a month from writing" with no mention of how long it took to get there, what it cost or how typical it is: that's a [no-catch promise](/patterns/no-catch-promises/). These are common behind paywalls, because the reveal is the product. The full story often turns out to be "be consistent".

### 4. Is the framing doing all the work?

Previews built on a reversal, "Productivity isn't about time. It's about energy," are setting up an argument rather than delivering information. That's [the "not X, but Y" tell](/patterns/not-x-but-y/). An opinion essay can still be worth reading; just know that's what you're buying.

### 5. Who is the writer?

Click the writer's name before you spend the read. Look at what else they've published: if it's twenty stories a week on unrelated subjects, the paywall is a volume strategy. If they've written a handful of pieces in one field, with detail that matches the profile, you're more likely to get first-hand knowledge.

### 6. Does the reading time fit the topic?

A 3-minute member-only story is mostly preview. A 25-minute one on a simple question is probably padded. Neither rules a story out, but compare the time to how much the headline claims to cover.

## Quick decision table

| In the preview | Read it | Skip it |
|---|---|---|
| Opening | A specific problem or result | A cliffhanger or "this will shock you" |
| Evidence | At least one fact, figure or example | Only promises |
| Headline | Scope and cost are clear | Big result, no catch |
| Writer | Consistent subject, real detail | High volume, scattered topics |
| Length | Fits the question | Far too short or far too long |

## Things that don't tell you much

- **The star.** It means the story is paywalled, not that it's better. Plenty of excellent stories on Medium are free.
- **Clap counts.** Members clap more freely than they read, and early stories have had no time to collect any.
- **Publication logos.** Standards vary by publication and by editor.

## If you're a member

Membership removes the read limit but not the time cost. The same checklist applies, and you can be stricter: back out after the preview if the first screen fails, use show less like this so Medium learns, and save the good ones to a list. The earlier [two-minute check](/guides/medium/medium-spot-low-quality-articles/) is the next step once you're past the paywall.

## If that doesn't work

If you keep being disappointed after the paywall by the same writers or publications, mute them so their previews stop tempting you; the [muting guide](/guides/medium/medium-mute-writers-publications/) has the steps. Slop Mop is coming soon to Medium. For now, the preview is the best free sample you'll get, so read it as one.
