Empty praise

Big verdicts like incredible, powerful and transformative, with no detail to show what the writer actually saw.

evaluation with no detail behind it

Empty praise is a verdict without the evidence. Something was incredible, powerful, inspiring, transformative or "exactly what this industry needs", and the post never says what happened to earn the word. It's the review equivalent of five stars and a blank comment box, written at length.

What empty praise looks like

slop

What an incredibly powerful keynote today. Truly inspiring, deeply insightful and exactly what our industry needs right now. 🙌

fine

The keynote's best moment was a slide comparing the speaker's 2019 sales forecast with what actually happened. They were out by 40%, and they walked through which assumption caused most of it.

slop

Huge shout-out to our amazing team for an absolutely phenomenal quarter. So proud of what we've achieved together. Truly world-class work.

fine

Thanks to the support team for this quarter: average reply time fell from 9 hours to 2, and Priya rewrote the refund macros so customers stopped getting three emails for one refund.

slop

This book is a must-read. Profound, timely and genuinely transformative. It will change how you think about everything.

fine

The chapter on pricing changed how I quote. The author suggests giving three options with the middle one as the real offer, and I've used it on four proposals since. The chapters on hiring were weaker.

What these have in common:

  • Intensifiers stacked on adjectives: incredibly powerful, truly inspiring, absolutely phenomenal.
  • Claims about the reader's future: "will change how you think", "you'll never see X the same way".
  • Timeliness with no reason: "more than ever", "exactly what we need right now".
  • Praise that could be pasted under any post about any event, team, book or product.

Why it wastes your time

When you read praise, you want to know whether to care: should you watch the talk, read the book, hire the agency? Empty praise gives you the writer's mood and nothing to decide with. Every keynote is "incredibly powerful" in this register, so the word tells you nothing about this one.

It also costs the person being praised. A team thanked for "world-class work" has learned nothing about what they did well, and readers outside the team can't tell whether the quarter was good or merely survived. Specific thanks is more useful to both, and more flattering.

The overall effect is inflation. When every event is transformative, the reader starts discounting all praise, including the deserved kind.

Google asks the same of anyone writing a product review. Its advice for review writers asks for evidence of your own experience, quantitative measurements, and the drawbacks as well as the benefits. A post that praises something is a small review, and the same standard helps.

When it's fine

Short, warm, unspecific praise has a real place:

  • Replies between people who know each other. "Congratulations, well deserved!" under a friend's promotion doesn't need a citation. Both of you know the detail.
  • Thank-you notes and condolences. "It was a lovely service" is kind, and nobody expects a breakdown.
  • Reviews where the detail is elsewhere. A star rating alongside a list of what was ordered, or a headline above a review that goes into specifics.
  • Quoted reactions. A post that reports "the audience gave it a standing ovation" is describing something that happened.

The tell is about posts that offer evaluation as their main content. If the praise is the whole post and nothing in it could be checked, readers are being asked to share a feeling with no reason given.

How Slop Mop scores empty praise

Empty praise is one of the nine questions the model asks about each post, one spoke on the Slopprint. It asks whether the post evaluates something without the detail to back the evaluation up. A warm word in a post full of specifics should barely move it.

The model says how confident it is, and shaky answers count for less. If empty praise is the only tell that stands out, the score is cut, since a short, kind congratulation is ordinary and harmless. It often appears with hype words, no-catch promises and a moral or two, and that combination is what gets flagged.

The counter-signals can only lower the score. A post that's useful to readers, sounds like one particular person or has drawn real comments and reposts gets a share taken off. Some tells count for more than others, and the weights aren't published. The verdict depends on your sensitivity setting, and if you think it's wrong, your vote overrides it in your feed.

How to fix it in your own writing

  1. Underline every evaluation. Great, powerful, inspiring, must-read, world-class.
  2. For each one, write what you saw. The moment, the number, the sentence someone said, the thing that changed afterwards.
  3. Keep the fact and drop most of the adjectives. One honest "good" next to a specific detail does more than three intensifiers.
  4. Name the people. If you're thanking a team, say who did what (with their permission).
  5. Include one reservation if you have it. "The second half dragged" makes the first half's praise believable.
slop

Just finished an incredible two-day workshop with an amazing group of leaders. Truly transformative and so inspiring. Grateful for every moment!

fine

Two days on a workshop about giving feedback. The most useful exercise was rewriting one real review we'd given in the past year, in pairs. Mine went from 600 words to 150 and finally said what the problem was. Day two's role-play felt forced.

Where empty praise shows up

Event recaps and team shout-outs on LinkedIn are full of it, and so are the reply threads under popular posts on X and Facebook, "great post!" comments on Reddit and Medium, and testimonials on the websites you read. It's also common in book-cover blurbs, award announcements and the opening lines of sales email.
