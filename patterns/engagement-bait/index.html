Engagement bait

Formula requests for comments, reposts and follows, added to a post to buy reach rather than start a conversation.

asking for comments, reposts and follows

on this page What engagement bait looks like Why it wastes your time When it's fine How Slop Mop scores engagement bait How to fix it in your own writing Where engagement bait shows up

Engagement bait is any part of a post whose job is to make you comment, repost, react or follow so the post reaches more people. Feeds tend to reward posts that collect comments quickly, and the bait is a way of collecting them. The writer wants the comments far more than anything written in them.

What engagement bait looks like

slop

Comment "PLAN" and I'll DM you my 30-day content calendar. 📩

♻️ Repost to help someone in your network.

Follow me for more.

fine

I've put our 30-day content calendar in a public spreadsheet: one tab per week, with the posts we cut and why. Use whatever helps.

slop

Agree or disagree? 👇

Remote work is dead.

Drop a 🔥 if you're with me.

fine

Our team went from 2 office days a week to 3 in September. Desk bookings rose, but so did Friday sick days. If you've made the same change with a team under 50, I'd like to know what you saw after six months.

slop

Only 1% of people will understand this post.

Type "YES" if you're one of them.

fine

One thing that confused me for years: a percentage point and a percent are different. A rate going from 4% to 5% is 1 point, but a 25% rise.

The common forms:

  • Keyword giveaways. "Comment GUIDE and I'll send it." The comment is the price of a document that could simply be linked.
  • Repost and follow prompts. "♻️ Repost to help your network", "Follow for more".
  • Reaction polls. "🔥 if you agree, 👍 if you don't." The reactions are the point; the vote is meaningless.
  • Agree-or-disagree bait. A blunt claim followed by "Thoughts?", with nothing to discuss underneath.
  • Tag requests. "Tag someone who needs this."

Why it wastes your time

Bait turns the comment section into a queue of "GUIDE", "YES" and fire emojis. If you open a post to see what people with experience thought, you have to scroll past dozens of keyword replies to find one sentence of substance.

It also distorts what the feed shows you. A post that collects 300 one-word comments looks like a lively discussion to a ranking system, so it reaches more people than a post with 12 thoughtful replies. You end up seeing more of whatever was best at asking.

The platforms themselves have said as much. In December 2017 Facebook announced it would show posts that ask for likes, shares, comments and tags less often in News Feed, while leaving requests for help or advice alone. In May 2022 LinkedIn said it would reduce the reach of posts that explicitly ask for reactions or comments. The habit has outlasted both announcements.

When it's fine

Asking readers to respond is ordinary and often useful. What matters is whether the writer wants the answer.

  • A genuine question. "Has anyone moved payroll from one provider to another mid-year? What broke?" The writer needs the replies, and the replies will be useful to the next reader too.
  • A request for help. Missing pets, blood drives, job hunts and fundraisers ask for shares because spreading the word is the whole purpose.
  • A clear pointer. "The full report is linked below" tells readers where something is. It doesn't charge a comment for it.
  • An invitation with a reason. "We're testing the new form with 20 customers. If you'd like to try it, reply here and I'll send a link" names who it's for and what happens next.

A useful test: if every reply were deleted after a minute, would the writer lose anything? If the answer is no, the replies were for the feed.

How Slop Mop scores engagement bait

Engagement bait is one of the nine questions the model asks about each post, and one spoke on the Slopprint. A genuine question barely registers. Formula asks, keyword giveaways and repost or follow prompts are what push the answer up.

The model also gives its confidence, and unsure answers carry less weight. When engagement bait is the only tell that stands out, the score is cut. Plenty of good posts end with one clumsy "follow for more", and that alone shouldn't fold them away. Bait often sits under a scroll-stopper hook with a few hype words in between, and several tells at once is what gets flagged.

The counter-signals can only lower a score. One of them, reader response, is counted from the reactions, comments and reposts shown under the post, and it discounts hollow engagement: hundreds of reactions with almost no comments or reposts earn less credit. So a bait post can't buy its way out with emoji replies. Some tells count for more than others, and the exact weights aren't published. Your sensitivity setting decides where the verdict lands, and your vote overrides the score in your own feed.

How to fix it in your own writing

  1. Find every line that asks for an action. Comment, repost, follow, tag, react, DM.
  2. Ask whether you need the response. If you'd read and use the answers, keep the request and make it specific. If not, delete it.
  3. Give away the thing. If you were going to DM a template to anyone who commented, link it in the post or the first comment.
  4. Ask a question only you could ask. Narrow it to people who can answer: team size, industry, a specific situation.
  5. Say what you'll do with the answers. "I'll share the results in a fortnight" gives people a reason to reply.
slop

I made a checklist for running better one-to-ones. ✅

Comment "121" and I'll send it over.

♻️ Repost if your network needs this.

fine

I made a one-page checklist for one-to-ones after six months of trying versions with my team. It's linked in the first comment. The part I'm least sure about is the career section: if you manage engineers and have a better way of running it, I'd like to hear it.

Where engagement bait shows up

The keyword giveaway is most common on LinkedIn at the moment. Reaction polls and "tag someone who needs this" are older Facebook habits, X has "like and repost if you agree", Reddit has karma-farming posts that ask for upvotes, and Substack and Medium writers end posts with "restack this" or "clap if it helped". Newsletters ask you to "reply to this email" and never read the replies.
